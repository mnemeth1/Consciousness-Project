## Recognition, room details and information access

The later Dutch account says TG knew from the chart that the patient had returned to the ward, then received recognition associated with the dentures, and discussed the experience at greater length later in the shift. Smit’s translated earlier presentation emphasizes surprise at the patient’s arrival. These are retrospective presentation differences, not independently established minute-by-minute observations. Dutch **evening shift** becomes **night shift** in English, while the later remembered greeting is **good afternoon**. TG allows that he probably greeted the patient first. Exact time of day and exclusion of voice recognition cannot be recovered from those statements. [I01 pp. 54–55; I02 PDF 5/reprint 4; I03 §§85–96, 160–169.]

The detailed article does not establish a perfect independent description of all staff and every object. The cupboard was not explicitly named by the patient, and other staff descriptions are limited. Rivas correctly distinguishes failing to describe someone from being unable to describe them. Neither fact supplies missing contemporaneous scoring. [I03 §§97–107, 202; I04 p. 7; I06 point 8.]

A specific discrepancy must remain visible. The Dutch patient-attributed account uses **laatje**, a drawer-like term, as does a television caption. Rivas’s reply places the error in that caption, but the interview itself also contains the word. The Dutch phrase places the dentures **on** it, while the English table says **in** a drawer. Later TG explains a flat wooden pull-out shelf already extended under an unusual converted cart. The wording therefore does not independently prove a metal drawer; equally, the reply does not erase the patient-attributed wording. [I02 PDF 5/reprint 4, account and caption; I03 §95 and §§171–179; I06 point 4.]

The unusual shelf remains the defenders’ strongest specific objection to a reconstruction based on the sound of opening a metal drawer. Its existence, open state and match to the patient’s report still reach us through TG. No independently scored description, contemporaneous photograph or case-specific reconstruction experiment is supplied. Correcting an implausible account of a particular sound does not exclude all ordinary information paths. [I01 p. 54; I08 pp. 199–202.]

## Procedure, two phases and correction history

The Dutch interview supports the two-phase reading of “stop” and “continue”: pain under pumping with a wish for it to stop, followed much later by fear that the team would abandon resuscitation and a wish that it continue. TG says the patient could not identify the experience’s beginning or end and reported no experience in the field. This can reconcile the reports at the level of remembered sequence; it is not a measured clock linking perception to a cerebral state. [I03 §§114–127; I01 pp. 57–59.]

The pre-Thumper argument has an actual correction history. In I03 §§55–58, an interviewer-led exchange moves from absent rhythm to circulation and brain activity; TG then gives a pump-first account before mouth inspection. Other passages deny circulation at removal and describe preparation before continued CPR. TG’s Winter 2008 reply explicitly places removal before switching on the pump. Both the earlier wording and the later correction must be retained. Selecting only the correction would conceal the critic’s textual basis; selecting only the earlier sentence would conceal the witness’s actual reply. [I03 §§55–60, 191–192; I05 p. 8.]

TG estimates more than five minutes from entrance to CCU with no heart massage while walking, then more than a minute for transfer/preparation. The 2010 English quotation weakens the Dutch no-heart-massage wording to resuscitation being hardly possible. These estimates and the claimed absence of circulation are retrospective testimony, not instrument readings. Pre-Thumper removal does not imply no previous ambulance or bystander CPR. Neither unavailable EEG nor pupil observations demonstrate zero brain activity. [I05 p. 8; I08 pp. 197–198.]

Other differences should not be silently harmonized. The translated Addink presentation uses intubation and upper/lower dentures; the detailed account identifies a loose upper denture, Mayo airway and mask, with endotracheal intubation later in ICU. Smit emphasizes absent pupil responses and permits restored circulation as an explanation for pain. Rivas’s detailed interview allows some pupil change after more than fifteen minutes and leaves the simultaneous pain/awareness account puzzling. Approximate return-of-rhythm/circulation timing also varies. The full earlier original and clinical record would be needed to resolve these differences. [I01 pp. 53–54, 58–60; I03 §§49–51, 70, 76, 190–196; I05 p. 8.]

## Strongest criticism and actual replies

Woerlee’s 2008 article explicitly corrects assumptions made in 2004 from sparse information. He proposes an ordinary account involving hypothermia, effective CPR, sensations, hearing, voice recognition and reconstruction. This is a developed direct challenge, not simply dismissal of testimony. Its clinical citations are not independently validated in this task, and no measured case temperature, perfusion or awareness probability is supplied. His conclusion that all elements are explainable is a claim to assess, not a demonstrated mechanism adopted here. [I04 pp. 4–7.]

TG and Rivas directly answer the Dutch criticism. They dispute pump timing, felt versus seen denture removal, the auditory/cart inference and staff-description wording, while accepting that field duration and prior bystander action are not known. Their strongest factual reply relies on TG’s explicit pre-Thumper correction. The drawer correction problem remains a limit within that reply. These texts give a complete inspected 2008 challenge/reply chain even while the exact 2010 response remains partly inaccessible. [I05 complete; I06 points 1–8.]

The 2010 rejoinder verifies its own reply relation to the response in the same issue, repeats TG’s correction and develops the unusual-shelf argument. It acknowledges reliance on TG and says clinical records were destroyed. That destruction statement and the claimed close match to Addink’s transcript are published assertions about record review; no independent chart audit or full original transcript supports them here. The rejoinder’s flat-EEG wording is not accompanied by a case EEG. Consultations with cardiologists and the related Verdegaal account are reported by the authors, not newly inspected independent originals. [I08 pp. 193–198, 203–205.]

The suggested experiment with ten blindfolded people is a proposal with predicted outcomes, not an experiment actually reported as performed. The blind-acquaintance illustration is also not a scored test of this case. The pain discussion offers psychogenic pain within the extracerebral interpretation, then conditionally allows ordinary physical pain if cortical activity had returned, while preserving the OBE interpretation. Those alternatives and Smit’s earlier physical-pain allowance must remain separate. [I08 pp. 199–203; I01 pp. 58–60.]

The rejoinder’s concluding choice between accepting its extraordinary interpretation and dismissing the account is too narrow for this dossier. One can take the report seriously while remaining uncertain about accuracy, timing or information access. Equally, identifying a possible ordinary mechanism does not show that it actually produced the whole account. No causal winner follows. The bibliography’s “Woerlee 2010” entry is a **Skeptiko comment**, distinct from the JNDS response named in the opening; its body was not separately read. [I08 pp. 193, 204–205.]
