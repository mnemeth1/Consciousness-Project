## Strongest support, challenge and reply

The strongest support is not Alexander’s professional status. It is a combination of the reported clinical impairment and specific externally matchable details, if their timing and information provenance can be secured. Mays reports Sylvia White’s2013 retrospective confirmation that an earlier description resembled Betsy’s photograph. He also develops a time-anchor argument from recalled faces. That is additional testimony worth preserving. It is not a blind identification procedure or an independently dated pre-photo description. White describes viewing the photograph after Holley introduced it; common family exposure matters. [S004, pp88–91](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321361929_Eben_Alexander's_near-Death_experience_How_an_esquire_article_distorted_the_facts).

Dittrich reports Potter describing sedation and delirious responsiveness. The clinical challenge matters independently of the article’s career narrative; this dossier makes no inference from professional controversy to false NDE testimony. [S003, clinical interview passage](https://www.esquire.com/entertainment/interviews/a23248/the-prophet/).

Mays replies that serious illness preceded sedation and publishes an attributed Potter objection to the reporting. Its provenance is mediated through family/publisher; Mays reports unsuccessful attempts to obtain her confirmation and her2013 removal request. The project cannot authenticate it as a direct retraction or infer deliberate deception by either side. [S004, pp67–68,72–78](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321361929_Eben_Alexander's_near-Death_experience_How_an_esquire_article_distorted_the_facts).

Shermer’s strongest point is that uncertainty about consciousness and vividness do not establish a supernatural mechanism. He reports asking Alexander about a separate mind’s mechanism and receiving an acknowledgment that it was unknown. His comparative examples leave ordinary alternatives open, but do not diagnose this individual episode. This is Shermer’s original argument; Sacks is cited within it and was not independently inspected. [S008, opening and closing arguments](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-near-death-experience-isnt-proof-heaven/).

Alexander’s best reply, reconstructed in A-T06-001, is that sufficiently verified experience during conditions excluding the targeted brain model would challenge that model, especially if accurate unavailable information were obtained. A-T06-002 identifies the outstanding bridge: retrospective narrative must be reliably aligned with physiological exclusion. A-T06-003 separates the mixed causes of unresponsiveness from claims about complete neural inactivity. These are conditional argument maps, not three independent observations.

The ordinary alternatives are residual/recovering processing, incorporation of accessible information, later reconstruction, or combinations. None is demonstrated as the cause here. Conversely, failure to identify one ordinary mechanism would not establish which nonphysical ontology is true. A continuation claim also needs a criterion of personal identity; a recovered person’s memory of an amnesic experience does not itself demonstrate survival after irreversible bodily death.
