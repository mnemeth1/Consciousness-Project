**Adams’s developed defense.** The original essay explicitly requires the relevant religious attributes and values, together with the kinds and amounts of evils in the actual world, to be represented in an internal-consistency argument (pp. 297–299). An omnipotent egoist would miss the proposed loving God, just as a pleasure-maximizer need not be the Christian target. The internal test can invoke Christian goods, but their availability within a system does not independently establish that the system is true.

Adams defines horror through a prima facie threat to the positive meaning of a participant’s life, encompassing doing as well as suffering evil. Her criterion is expressly objective but relative to individuals; personal vulnerability and the participant’s perspective matter, while that person is not declared infallible (pp. 299–300). This objective qualification was already in the 1989 original. A subsequent clarification must not be described as her first introduction of it.

The central distinction separates goodness as producing a globally good world from goodness to each created person (pp. 302–305). Even a global benefit can leave a victim’s life ruined, and retributive suffering inflicted on a perpetrator can add another participant whose life needs repair. She therefore rejects an unsupplemented global answer as adequate to both problems. This is her criticism of the cited strategies, not this packet’s independent adjudication of every free-will or global theodicy.

The proposed response retains the idea that a loving God would have a reason for permitting horror, while denying that a defender must identify that reason to exhibit compossibility. Adams distinguishes readily intelligible reasons, reasons requiring greater cognitive resources, and reasons beyond one’s present maturity; she places our ignorance of divine permission in the third category. Her child-and-parent analogy is an argument for how intimate care could reassure despite that ignorance, not independent evidence about divine action (pp. 304–306).

Adams first proposes that intimacy with an incommensurately good God can overwhelm the threat to a life’s worth. She then proposes meaningful defeat through identification with Christ, divine gratitude or suffering understood as a form of encounter with divine life (pp. 306–309). These are theological possibilities with different assumptions, not three observed routes or independent confirmations. She expressly acknowledges that shared pain and Christological metaphysics need further development (p. 310). The same participant’s continued existence, the proposed goods and the relevant identity relations are not demonstrated by describing them.

**Sutherland and the added responses.** Sutherland recognizes the internal-consistency aim and does not accuse Adams of simple indifference or wholesale abandonment of reason (pp. 313–316,321). His Tolstoy illustration asks what could justify revising or displacing a moral judgment. The quoted historical documents and biographical claims are uninspected dependencies, not new empirical cases.

His strongest point is that relief, a worthwhile subsequent life and justice are distinguishable. His job-rejection example allows someone to be glad about the eventual outcome while continuing to judge the initial decision unjust (p. 319). A reassured Job cannot answer merely for himself when the question concerns his children (p. 318). The concluding questions concern other victims, those without transcendent bliss, common moral language and whether assurance suppresses justified judgments of injustice or cold-bloodedness (pp. 320–323). This objection remains relevant even if his first-person reading of Adams’s criterion is mistaken.

The revised reproduction supplies three explicit responses. First, it says the participant’s horror gives everyone a prima facie reason for concern, not only the participant (PDF 2–3). Second, it denies an illicit shift from reason to emotion: intimacy is offered as an objective good that gives reason to judge that participant’s life positively (PDF 5, added note responding to Sutherland pp. 314–315). Third, it denies that defeating a horror entails that the event was not horrendous, unjust or serious, or that moral intuitions about it were unreliable (PDF 6, added note responding to Sutherland pp. 321–323).

These are substantive answers to particular interpretations, not merely a repeat of the original thesis. They block the shortcut from “the life can finally be good” to “the act was therefore just.” They also clarify why observer reassurance cannot do the victim’s work. But the third response is itself a denial of an alleged implication; it does not independently establish the proposed defeat or show that allowing the original harm was morally permissible. The live question is whether participant-specific goodness and the further theological premises adequately establish the required compatibility with antecedent permission. Lack of a known reason is not by itself a proof of incompatibility, and restoration is not by itself a proof of justification.

A small source anomaly is retained: Sutherland p. 317 visibly prints an affirmative “can be” clause within a surrounding discussion denying an immanent trump. It is not silently emended as an OCR error. The strongest objection rests on the clear passages at pp. 318–323, not that isolated wording.

**Howard-Snyder’s defense and its limits.** Howard-Snyder distinguishes a desire for immediate relationship from an all-things-considered desire. He treats reasonable belief as a condition for the specified explicit reciprocal relationship while keeping belief separate from loving reciprocation and voluntary choice (pp. 433–437). His formulation explicitly departs from Schellenberg’s earlier version. The added overriding-reason qualification and separate no-reason premise must therefore be attributed to his reconstruction, not silently copied into every hiddenness argument.

He grants inculpable nonbelief for argument and stipulates a prima facie permission criterion that allows a means no worse than alternatives, rather than demanding strict logical necessity (pp. 437–439). He concedes that unaided cognition may not warrant identifying an actual sufficient candidate reason. The narrower aim is to make confidence in the absence of such a reason unwarranted. Possible goods, prima facie reasons, epistemically unexcluded reasons and actual all-things-considered permission remain separate.

His examples distinguish those disposed to reject God, remain indifferent or reciprocate, and distinguish a likely response from inability to respond otherwise. Among well-disposed seekers, type 4 concerns freely owning an initially unchosen fixed disposition; type 1 concerns the depth or motivation of love already voluntarily cultivated (pp. 440–447). He assumes extension to types 2 and 3 without argument (p. 447). A person’s lack of belief does not itself establish any of these psychological descriptions. His discussion of hypothetical likelihoods supplies no measured probabilities.

The concessions are consequential. The proposed delay may span an earthly life because survival is assumed, but it is not said to last forever. Note 18 concedes that the sketched reason does not apply where a willing seeker recognizes deficient love and strongly wants improvement (p. 447). A sufficiently fitting seeker would escape the objection; Howard-Snyder then disputes our ability to identify one (p. 450). Improvement is not perfect love demanded before disclosure. Cultural nonbelief remains unaddressed, and he explicitly resists inferring a group-level explanation from possible individual reasons (pp. 451–452).

The article’s internal anticipated objections also distinguish prima facie from incontrovertible reasons and freely owning a disposition from improving the love to which one is disposed (pp. 448–451). These are not separately inspected later critics. His value comparison between chosen and unchosen love rests on case reflection, not a proof from more obvious premises. The claim that withholding a relationship is no worse than enabling improvement within it remains contestable.

Two further boundaries matter. Note 14 rules out the proposal that God should have prevented initial ill-dispositions because that would connect this version of hiddenness to the problem of evil (p. 442). That is his disputed scope argument, not a project restriction on critics. The closing discussion also says the defense does not rule out eventual coerced love as a second-best possibility (p. 452); it does not establish that as an actual plan. His final epistemic fallback challenges an inference from our failure to conceive a reason to there being none (p. 453). Its reach must be compared with the positive evaluations of fitting love and divine goodness on which the defense also relies.

**The actual Schellenberg reply.** The supplied journal PDF independently verifies the exact 1996 article, printed 455–462 (PDF 3–10), with all ten notes now read. Wrapper dates concern online publication in 2013 and download in 2014; the primary Cambridge record’s 2020 date is another web-version date. Receipt in April 1996 and revision in May 1996 are on p. 462. The earlier Cambridge notes-only exposure remains a separate conservative inspection unit, not an independent argument or corroboration.

Schellenberg first disputes the reconstruction (pp. 455–459, §I). Howard-Snyder’s overriding-reasons clause and separate no-reason premise make the argument vulnerable to a Rowe-style inference from unrecognized reasons to their absence. Schellenberg acknowledges the vulnerability of that inference but argues that his own approach instead offers positive, defeasible support for a perfectly loving God’s intention to make belief available. He accepts that a demonstrated overriding reason would undermine that claim and accepts commitment to the negative existential consequence; he denies needing an *independent* inference to it (especially p. 459). Thus he does not make ignorance into a proof that there are no reasons, and he does not make his conclusion immune to a countercase. The full positive support refers back to his 1993 book, which is not freshly inspected here. The distinction between divine desire and intention and the proposed analogies remain argued premises.

He then addresses three proposed reasons for delay (pp. 459–462, §II). For certain powerless rejection, he contests whether the person meets the argument’s relationship-capacity condition. For likely rejection, the manner of disclosure matters: a loving encounter and continued help could alter the disposition and make development after belief preferable (p. 460). Note 9 expressly forbids transferring this hypothetical divine-world response into a conclusion about actual-world counterparts’ acceptance. No psychological frequencies or actual seeker classifications follow.

For initially unchosen good dispositions, freely confirming love and choosing to continue it can occur after belief begins, even amid competing allegiances (pp. 460–461). Neither immediate nor delayed belief changes how the initial disposition was acquired. For inadequately centered or motivated love, improvement may likewise occur within a relationship; some resources for improvement presuppose belief. Note 10 distinguishes experienced absence from lack of belief (p. 461). These proposals directly challenge the comparative claim that withholding belief is no worse than enabling development within it, although comparative feasibility and value are not independently established for actual individuals.

Schellenberg also rejects an unqualified human-lover analogy (p. 462). His reasons concern the greater benefits withheld by God, creatures’ proposed unique fulfillment in divine relationship, the relationship’s inequality and absence of divine need. These are theological and normative premises, including a claimed purpose of creation; they are not observed facts or a derivation of moral authority merely from creator status. He concludes that the argument survives this defense while explicitly leaving later discovery of reasons open (§III). No subsequent Howard-Snyder rebuttal has been inspected or invented.

The strongest live dispute therefore concerns the positive warrant for universal access and the available means of development, not merely whether anyone can imagine a benefit of delay. Howard-Snyder’s distinctions between prima facie reasons, fittingness and ownership remain relevant; Schellenberg’s actual response confronts their comparative bridge and the fallback’s argument structure. This packet establishes their disagreement more precisely without certifying either full hiddenness argument.

**Other branches and decision limits.** Free-will defenses are relevant to responsibility and available alternatives, but a possible value in choice does not by itself settle the cost to each victim. Soul-making is relevant to developmental goods and delayed relationship, but the comparison with development within an available relationship remains necessary. Skeptical theism concerns the warrant for inferences about unknown reasons; local uncertainty need not erase every moral judgment, while unrestricted skepticism may compromise positive recognizability claims too. These are explicit relevance and burden screens.
