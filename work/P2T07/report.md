## The positive account and the newly read qualifications

Enoch characterizes robust realism through objective irreducible normative truths, allowing attitudes to matter normatively without constituting fundamental truths. He separates this ontology from epistemic warrant. His introduction outlines a parity argument: deliberative indispensability can justify belief as explanatory indispensability does. The supplied chapter 3 opening targets general normative realism, which can coexist with denial of moral realism. Its concluding page makes the proposed reason defeasible and acknowledges incomplete rejection of alternatives. Reading these endpoints does not reconstruct the intervening premises or establish their truth. [Published preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780191618567_A23589271/preview-9780191618567_A23589271.pdf), printed 1–5,11–12/PDF 14–18,24–25; Google images 50–52,84. **CL-P2T07-001–004,014; A-P2T07-001.**

Chapter 4 explicitly denies that moral objectivity plus general normative realism entails robust moral realism. It offers further motivation conditional on the earlier arguments succeeding. Familiar metaphysical and epistemic objections are said to concern normativity generally; note 2 on 90 assumes general normative realism remains justified after considering objections. Detailed defenses are deferred to chapters 6–9. Enoch does not dismiss extra costs merely because a cost was already accepted: page 91 rejects that quick inference. He instead proposes that moral and other normative facts are of the same relevant kind, so the moral extension adds no new ontological kind. Whether that analogy succeeds remains contestable. Google images 85–93, especially 90 notes 2–3 and 91–93. **CL-P2T07-015; A-P2T07-004.**

The chapter distinguishes several issues that should not be collapsed into “categorical reasons.” A rule can apply despite indifference without supplying an actual reason. Actual reasons can apply regardless of contingent motivation. Desire-grounding is a further issue: page 95 note 13 allows that, if Schroeder's proposed account succeeds, desire-grounded agent-neutral reasons could apply whatever the agent wants. Enoch doubts that success and retains a separate anti-reduction burden. The original Schroeder account was not inspected here. Moral/nonmoral and categorical/hypothetical distinctions need not coincide. Google images 93–95, especially 95 notes 12–13. **CL-P2T07-016.**

Enoch's moral-rationalist claim is expressly weak. Morality's connection to reasons is not wholly contingent; he does not require morality itself to be the source of the reasons or “moral reasons” themselves to be real reasons. He allows possible exceptional cases with no reason to comply and certainly allows other considerations to override morality's normative force. Chapter 2 alone remains compatible with objectivist naturalist reduction. Chapter 4 challenges a mixed position using both earlier arguments, without delivering an every-agent/every-case compliance guarantee. Google images 95–97, especially 97 and notes 15–16. **CL-P2T07-017.**

The chapter's expressivism discussion has limited scope: a conditional rejection of moral expressivism would undermine global expressivism, while some local nonmoral expressivisms could remain. The closing section presents an open-ended challenge and allows unconsidered motivations for resisting robust moral realism. It does not convert the proposed bridge into entailment or certify its chapter 2/3 antecedents. Google images 98–99. **CL-P2T07-015; A-P2T07-004.**

Section 5.1 begins with a challenge to moving from indispensable normative thought to irreducible normative facts. Conceptual nonidentity does not establish property nonidentity. Identity, reduction, grounding, constitution and type/token claims require distinctions; mere supervenience is insufficient for the intended metaphysical conclusion. If “naturalism” meant only normative-on-natural supervenience, Enoch says he would count as a naturalist. He does not identify natural with physical or stipulate a natural reduction base is nonnormative, since that would exclude the reductionist's proposal by definition. His ordinary-science characterization of natural facts is admitted to be imprecise. Google images 100–103, especially 101 notes 1–3 and 103. **CL-P2T07-018.**

Enoch acknowledges that familiar objections to reduction are inconclusive and better proposals remain possible. His response to Schroeder does not prove irreducibility: it identifies an impasse if an initial normative-force intuition is not shared, and distinguishes failure to find an anti-reduction argument from an increase in reduction's metaphysical plausibility. His first-person intuition remains a philosophical commitment subject to appraisal. He explicitly permits revising it for a sufficiently strong specific reduction, credits naturalism's parsimony advantage, and in note 29 proposes developing and assessing token-level naturalist reduction without useful general reductive laws. That suggestion requires details and answers to analogous philosophy-of-mind difficulties; it supplies neither a completed theory nor an endorsement. Google images 104–109, especially 105,107–109 and 109 note 29. **CL-P2T07-019–020; A-P2T07-005.**

## The direct criticism and reply

Lenman's dilemma is that thin deliberation may be unavoidable without robust truths, while thicker deliberation may need them but be optional. His schmeliberation uses critically examined concerns to distinguish success and failure and, he argues, feels like deliberation. Reflective desires differ from isolated impulses; concern need not be selfish. His expressivism differs from descriptive naturalism. Normative truth conditions could concern consciousness without being constitutively determined by consciousness. These are philosophical claims, not population findings. [Lenman original](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-013-0214-7), printed 835–841, especially 837–841; supplied PDF 1–7. **CL-P2T07-005–007; A-P2T07-002.**

Enoch replies that similarity to choosing does not establish identity with deliberation, and formal standards need not deserve allegiance. He allows divergent phenomenology and interprets instrumental indispensability through retaining sufficient reason to engage, beyond preserving behavior. The indispensability story supplies his claimed reason to believe; it does not make normative truths true. Contrary evidence could revise his claim or lead him to accept a diminished project. His wider expressivism response is preliminary. In the adjacent epistemological discussion he acknowledges lacking a criterion separating legitimate Moorean reasoning from bad bootstrapping. [Enoch reply](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11098-013-0216-5), sections 3–4,6, printed 859–862,864–865; section 5,863–864. **CL-P2T07-008–011; A-P2T07-003.**

The live question is whether deserved allegiance identifies an unavoidable requirement or imports the disputed realist demand. The critic offers a thinner project; the defender says it loses something justificatorily important. Neither description becomes decisive because an author reports its phenomenology. The missing chapter 3 derivation remains needed to assess the original move from commitment to warranted belief. The complete moral bridge and qualified reduction discussion do not fill that gap. Enoch's color-blindness analogy is an argument, not an established diagnosis of Lenman.

## Exact comparison with v1 and accepted constructivist context

Craig locates value in divine nature and obligation in commands from a qualified authority. He corrects earlier wording: some commands vary while respecting essential goodness. An unrestricted-voluntarism objection misses that account; the transcript does not establish the proposed authority. [Official transcript](https://www.reasonablefaith.org/media/reasonable-faith-podcast/reasons-for-divine-commands), authority/value/discretionary-hypothesis responses; web lines 168–189,197–205. **CL-T15-001,002,019 retained.**

Railton makes natural dispositional facts the ground of objective interests. His impartial informed criterion is controversial. Obligation can apply without every rational agent having a categorical reason to comply. His naturalistic definitions are theoretical proposals with explanatory success still a burden. [Original scan](https://r.jordan.im/download/philosophy/railton1986.pdf), printed 173–177,189–191,201–205. **CL-T15-003–005 retained; CL-P2T07-012 adds methodological qualification.** Enoch's weak moral-rationalist conclusion and distinctions among senses of categorical reasons do not by themselves refute Railton's specific account. Enoch's openness to reduction also prevents presenting irreducibility as a completed refutation of naturalism.

These routes assign different jobs to value, duty, reasons and motivation. A reason to believe a normative truth differs from its ground. A consciousness-related good differs from a truth made normative by consciousness. No inspected passage supplies unique conscious moral authority. Shared subjecthood may affect incentives without establishing impartial duty or rights; endurance may enable accountability without explaining wrongness. The new concept/property distinction also blocks inferring metaphysical difference from conceptual difference alone. **CL-T15-016–019 unchanged; CL-P2T07-013,015–020.**

Its reported reply at printed 233 addresses formal universality; the humanity and publicity arguments remain further disputed steps. Nagel's challenge and Korsgaard's response remain live disagreement, and shareable reasons do not establish one numerical subject. Railton's specific concession does not generalize to every secular account.
