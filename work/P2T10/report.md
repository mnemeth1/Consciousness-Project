The 1999 argument distinguishes abstract world-complete facts from the contingent concrete universe they describe. Its possible-to-actual step uses the maximal contingent conjunction to identify the world in which an explanation is possible with the actual world. That step depends on totality, modality and explanation premises, not on a temporal beginning. Later steps exclude scientific and contingent-agent explanations, privilege libertarian action as an explanatory stopping point, and then add teleology for the creator’s qualities. The paper acknowledges both unconceived explanation types and difficulty establishing actual benevolence. Its opening footnote also preserves disagreement between its coauthors over gratuitous evil. These are substantive qualifications, not incidental reservations. (1999, pp461–476, especially (1)–(18), pp469–474.)

Oppy’s selected direct criticism attacks the advertised weak-premise advantage. Under veridicality, dissectivity and normal modal reasoning, weak PSR entails strong PSR. He separates this result from a conditional epistemic argument: a non-theist with independent rational grounds can reconsider an initially attractive premise after learning its consequences. He also distinguishes an explained physical duplicate from the same world-complete fact. His criticism does not establish atheism or independently validate those rational grounds. (Manuscript §§II–V, pp4–15; endnotes1,3–4.)

The 2002 reply accepts the deduction and restricts its formal PSRs to contingent propositions. Its defense turns on epistemic warrant: understanding need not encompass every consequence, and explanation-seeking allegedly favors W-PSR over possible brute facts. The comparison with materialism is expressly not argued there. The reply retains the goodness gap, offers a conditional BCCF* fallback, and relies on a defeasible transition from epistemic to logical possibility. Its Davey–Clifton discussion is inspected as the reply authors’ account; the critic’s own original is not covered. (2002, pp89–99.)

The central disagreement therefore concerns warranted premises and the direction of belief revision. Given a shared deduction, one side can use initial confidence in possible explanation to support stronger explanation; the other can use independent reasons against the consequence to challenge the initial principle. Neither direction is selected merely by validity. The reply is right to resist a requirement that ordinary understanding include every logical consequence. That does not by itself show that the particular world-complete instance is warranted, or that the critic lacks independent reasons. Conversely, the fact that a principle is controversial or supports theism does not alone show that it is question-begging. These are project assessments of the opposed arguments, not further quotations or accepted premise truths.

The modal bookkeeping matters. The 1999 formal W-PSR ranges over propositions at every world; the 2002 formulation explicitly ranges over contingent propositions. Calling both simply “weak PSR” conceals that qualification. For the restricted deduction, its special conjunction must itself satisfy the stated contingency restriction. The formal identity route and the Fitch-style route must also remain distinct: the latter uses explanatory distribution rather than maximal-world identification. A policy of searching for explanations is not itself a demonstration that every world-complete contingent fact can be explained. Nor does failure to verify an unexplained fact prove that it has an explanation.

The additional agency argument requires equally careful treatment. The strongest foundation-to-mind countercase within the selected defender is the impersonal indeterministic alternative reported from Quinn’s correspondence. That correspondence is not a separately inspected Quinn original. The project’s central unresolved question is whether the free-action stopping point receives a justification that the impersonal alternative lacks. Context-sensitive explanatory fullness makes that comparison harder, rather than automatically answering it. A necessary bearer and a contingent action can be distinguished without proving that a suitable bearer exists. Rejecting necessary-world collapse does not establish that every remaining explanation must be mental. No bridge from a necessary creator to the identity or survival of human persons is supplied.

Lennox distinguishes mechanism–agent compatibility from positive claims about intelligibility, lawlike sophistication and rational warrant. The essay also treats laws as regularities that do not independently exclude supernatural action. Its historical quotations and resurrection claims were not corroborated. (HTML L12–30, L60–76.) The relevant project comparison is consequently limited: compatibility leaves room for agency, while positive comparative support requires defensible expectations about alternatives. The selected PSR exchange makes some of those additional premises explicit; it does not measure them. Shared appeals to order and explanatory preferences cannot be counted again as independent observations of creation.
