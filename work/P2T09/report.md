## Exact exchange and the strongest countercase

[AWARE II](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2023.109903), Resuscitation 191 (2023) 109903, was reopened from the frozen 12-page user PDF. [Greyson and van Lommel](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2023.109924), 194 (2024) 109924, is a one-page published letter obtained from the author’s site. [Parnia’s reply](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2023.110080), 194 (2024) 110080, was reopened from the supplied one-page PDF. Their argument concerns interpretation of the same AWARE II data, not independent observations.

The critic’s strongest point is the missing EEG–recollection intersection: the main paper reports EEG data for only two of the 28 interviewees, neither with explicit cognitive recall (AWARE II p. 7, Fig. 2 footnote). This does not establish a positive association between the recorded patterns and a particular recalled experience. The letter also raises CPR artifact concerns. Its stronger suggestion that the patterns are therefore not markers of consciousness goes beyond this missing-data result: absent explicit recollection is not an established absence of consciousness. Its opening description of suppression as affecting about half the patients also changes the main paper’s unit; the original reports 47% of EEG data, not 47% of people (main p. 8; critic p. 1).

Parnia explicitly answers that lack of explicit recall does not establish lack of consciousness, and points to implicit memory and clinical EEG interpretation (reply p. 1, second paragraph). This is a substantive rebuttal to the negative inference. It does not demonstrate consciousness in those two people, reconstruct what they experienced, or provide the missing timing linkage. His artifact response states that recordings were taken during pulse checks with minimal movement, artifacts removed, and expert interpretation performed under ACNS standards (third paragraph). The main methods support pulse-check sampling and visual classification on p. 4, with adapted ACNS criteria continued on p. 6, but the letter is an author assurance, not an independent artifact audit or data reanalysis.

The reply also distinguishes correlation from production and proposes disinhibition as an explanation of the experience’s features (remaining paragraphs). The first distinction does not establish brain independence; the proposed mechanism is not a measured mediator. Thematic differences from dreams or hallucinations do not alone establish the external accuracy of a report. Nor is the reply’s general claim about the absence of a causal explanation of consciousness independently verified by this packet.

[Martial et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2023.109980), 194 (2024) 109980, was independently reopened in the published two-page institutional copy. It challenges screenshot classification, terminology, chance fruit selection, and experience timing (p. 1). The 110080 reply cannot be presented as its direct answer. The supplied [Parnia et al. 110205 reply](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2024.110205), *Recalled experience of death: Disinhibition not degeneration…*, Resuscitation 199 (2024), explicitly addresses Martial et al. in its opening. References 1 and 2 identify both the inspected 109980 letter and the separate 2022 *Studying death and near-death experiences requires neuroscientific expertise*. That 2022 original and the later 2025/2026 exchange remain uninspected branches.

The 110205 reply’s strongest methodological point is that a changing ischemic/reperfusion state cannot simply be equated with a stable neural state. It explains that practical constraints on multichannel EEG limited detailed time–frequency analysis, while defending appropriately adapted standards (p. 1). This supplies a reason for the limitation, not the omitted analysis or a validation of the resulting marker. Its appeal to expertise and funding cannot replace that validation.

On guessing, the reply appeals to more than 80,000 English nouns to call selection of apple, pear and banana improbable (p. 1, EEG paragraph continuing into the right column). The original procedure instead asks the interviewee to name any three fruits (main p. 3). The relevant distribution is therefore conditional on that cue and its scoring rule; the letter supplies no justified distribution over fruit choices. Neither an English-noun denominator nor assumptions of equal likelihood and independent guesses yield an earned p-value here. This objection leaves the one-person response’s source attribution intact while withholding its claimed discriminating weight.

The letter’s recoverability continuum and discussion of disinhibition offer a positive account, and its defense of RED terminology emphasizes the reported themes (pp. 1–2). Its claims about death declarations, recoverability, millions of survivors, exclusive thematic differences and the absence of a neural explanation remain correspondence assertions, not fresh clinical findings in this packet. The first paragraph of p. 2 explicitly leaves the functional/evolutionary benefit unknown while assuming adaptation. Its cited cellular recovery and gene-expression works have not been independently inspected and cannot simply stand for demonstrated subjective consciousness. The reply supplies neither additional participant-linked EEG/recollection data nor supplemental files.

The strongest favorable reading is therefore that unusual recollections deserve investigation, clinically occult cognition remains possible, and limitations of recall prevent the EEG findings from being dismissed simply because the small interviewed EEG subset reported no experience. The strongest objection to an extracerebral reading is that the studies do not establish when a particular experience or memory formed under independently measured exclusion of ordinary processing and information access. Neither side obtains its strongest metaphysical conclusion from the current intersections.

## Denominators and opportunities

AWARE II’s flow is 567 arrests, 213 sustained ROSC, 53 discharge survivors, and 28 interviewed people, with 11 reporting memories/perceptions (pp. 6–7). The 25 survivors not interviewed comprise four refusals, seven disabled, three other reasons, and eleven with no reason reported in the displayed flow. CPR-induced consciousness (2), post-resuscitation experiences (2), dreams (3), and recalled experiences of death (6) total 13 category memberships among 11 people; these categories cannot be added as disjoint people. This is a report among interviewees, not an all-arrest rate of consciousness.

The 110080 reply’s opening uses approximate 40%, 20% and 3% summaries and the broad label “survivors.” Its description of the first group as perceiving consciousness without explicit recall cannot replace the main paper’s detailed category definitions. The underlying analyzed interview denominator is 28, not all 53 discharge survivors. These compressed descriptions are not new results or a revised participant classification.

Equipment was deployed in 365 of 567 cases. The protocol displays an image after one minute of activation and begins repeated fruit words after five minutes; equipment deployment is not proof that either stimulus was successfully delivered for a particular surviving interviewee (p. 3). The main paper reports no explicit recall of target stimuli, no correct implicit image choice, and one correct implicit fruit choice among 28 interviewees (p. 7). This fruit result is distinct from the separately reported one-person overt visual recollection of clinical surroundings (p. 6); their overlap is unknown. The fruit prompt asks for any three fruits rather than defining a uniform finite choice distribution, so no calibrated chance probability is calculated here.

The EEG units are 851 screenshots from 85 people; 466 interpretable screenshots from 53 people; 49 people also with cerebral oximetry (p. 7). Repeated screenshots are not independent subjects. The two interviewed EEG participants do not identify the monitored-and-reporting subset needed for a positive EEG–experience association. Reported delta/theta activity up to 60 minutes and alpha up to 35 minutes are aggregate observations under CPR; the source cannot distinguish physiological alpha from alpha coma (p. 9). Late plotted points in Fig. 1A and the greater-than-60-minute bin in Fig. 1B (both p. 4) require the tabular source to resolve their relationship to the prose summaries. No exact corrected maximum or experience timing is inferred from the plot.

The required intersections remain unknown: valid image delivery × interview; valid auditory delivery × interview; target identification × delivery × EEG time; and particular recalled content × participant-specific neural state. No missing measurement is coded as absent brain activity. Cardiac arrest undergoing resuscitation is not evidence of subjective survival after irreversible death.

The community arm’s 126 selected narratives have a different ascertainment rule and no all-arrest denominator (pp. 2, 6). Its overlap with the prospective sample and public retellings is unknown. Companion EEG/oximetry analyses mentioned on p. 9, footnote 3, may reuse observations; neither they nor letters discussing this article count as new independent evidence.

## AWARE I final published flow

[AWARE I](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.resuscitation.2014.09.004), Resuscitation 85 (2014) 1799–1805, was obtained in its seven-page final journal layout. Printed pp. 1801–1802 retain 2,060 events, 330 survivors, 140 first-stage interviews and 101 second-stage interviews. The same version says 52 interviews occurred in hospital and 90 after discharge, while Table 1’s footnote uses 142. The final graph does not repair these inconsistent expressions of the first-stage denominator.

Fig. 1, p. 1802, subtracts 188 ineligible survivors and two refusals from 330 to reach 140. It divides first-stage responses into 85 without memories and 55 with memories; its arrows place 39 second-stage noncompleters on the former branch. The resulting 101 comprise 46 without memories, 46 non-NDE recollections, seven NDEs without event recall and two with event recall. These are graphical dispositions; raw participant data were not obtained. The graph labels the top box in-hospital cardiac arrests, whereas the protocol also permits ongoing out-of-hospital resuscitations arriving in the emergency department (p. 1800). The mismatch is preserved.

The graph labels stage-three interview n=2 and splits one verified/one unverified recollection; text says one could not receive further follow-up because of poor health (pp. 1801–1803). Do not convert this into two independent corroborations. Both event-recall cases were in locations without shelves; approximately 22% of all events were in shelf-equipped areas, which does not supply the exposed-interviewed denominator. The audio cue was abandoned after the pilot (final p. 1801, Methods). The author’s chart check and AED-based timing inference are reproduced as attributions, not independently reopened medical records. The body’s awareness estimate of up to three minutes and footnote’s at-least-three-minute arrest refer to different quantities. Neither substitutes for that participant’s EEG or cerebral blood-flow measurement (pp. 1802–1803).

## Dutch published layout and exact correction

[Van Lommel et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(01)07100-8), Lancet 358 (2001) 2039–2045, was reopened in the seven-page published layout rather than treating the prior author reformat as an independent study. It reports 344 participants, 509 successful resuscitation episodes, 62 broad WCEI-defined reports and 41 core reports (pp. 2039–2041). Dividing participant reports by repeated episodes is not automatically an independent episode incidence. The printed 248 interviewed within five days and accompanying 74% still lack a clear matching denominator (p. 2040). No correction is manufactured.

The two-year interviews initially involve 37 from the original NDE group and 37 selected controls. Six are reclassified out of the NDE group and four into it, producing 35/39; the eight-year follow-up is 23/15. Table 5’s two- and eight-year comparisons refer to the same retained 38 people, not two fresh cohorts (pp. 2041–2043). These reclassifications do not retroactively replace the baseline 62/344. Recruitment variation and estimated physiological durations limit comparisons. The study did not record participant EEG; physiological citations in the discussion cannot establish their individual neural states.

The supplied correction is Lancet 2002 April 6;359(9313):1254, *Department of Error*, bottom-right second notice. Its exact page and target were independently read and visually checked. It replaces **psilocarpine** with **psilocybine** in the third sentence of p. 2044’s third paragraph, concerning induced near-death-like experiences. Reference 28 remains after LSD and reference 21 after mescaline, matching the original superscripts. The references themselves, Grof and Halifax’s book and Schröter-Kunhardt’s chapter, are uninspected backgrounds rather than additional verified evidence. The notice changes no participants, episodes, prevalence figures, follow-up groups or Table 5 results. It does not resolve 248(74%) or validate the discussion’s neural interpretation. This closes the exact correction-content access requirement, subject to review; it does not supply a corrected incidence or neural timing result. Its exact DOI remains unverified. Same-page letters discovered in Q005 have their own DOIs and are not the notice. Unrelated correspondence and the other correction on the supplied page are excluded.
