# P2T06 — Constructivism, rational grounding and the scope of obligation

Submitted for independent P2R06 review under protocol_v2. The selected original exchange materially improves v1’s constructivist coverage. It supplies a positive grounding proposal and an actual objection/reply, while leaving the success of the grounding argument disputed. It does not establish that fundamental, universal or enduring consciousness grounds morality.

The operative distinction is between needing a reason to act and having a particular moral reason. A further distinction separates an agent’s reason to value herself from a demand she owes another person. The selected theory addresses these transitions explicitly. They must be assessed as arguments, rather than credited merely because the theory invokes reflection or consciousness.

## Positive account and direct opposition

Korsgaard connects reasons and obligations to reflective agency and practical identity, then argues for valuing humanity. She expressly separates formal universalization from the further moral-law step (pp. 99–100), and does not treat reflective self-conception as proof of a metaphysical self (p. 100). Her extension to others relies on shareable reasons (§4.2); nonhuman moral patients are included (§4.3). The precise commitments and qualifications are CL-P2T06-001–008.

Nagel challenges both the move from freedom to law and the claim that identity supplies reasons. His alternative puts evaluative reasons prior to the identity they help shape; intelligible public reasoning need not by itself defeat egoism (pp. 201–208). The objection is not a claim that morally indifferent people never deliberate. Its target is the justificatory transition. CL-P2T06-009–010 preserve the original argument rather than substituting a survey’s description.

Korsgaard’s reply makes general commitments constitutive of willing (§1), distinguishes an incentive from its endorsement (§4), and rejects an explanation of motivation as sufficient justification (§5). At p. 233 she expressly limits the new universality argument: humanity-wide scope and publicity still require the earlier arguments. CL-P2T06-011–013 record the reply and its limits. The earlier arguments remain available for assessment; the acknowledgment does not itself concede that they fail.

## Assessment of the disputed transitions

The strongest countercase asks whether the theory imports the authority it proposes to explain. Describing an episode as reflective can establish that the agent confronts a question; it does not immediately establish which answers count as justified. Describing language as publicly understandable likewise does not by itself settle whether another person’s interests supply me with a reason. A consistent egoist can apparently understand another’s demand while disputing its authority. The defender must explain what makes that position rationally defective, beyond noting that ordinary social interaction often involves shared deliberation.

The strongest reply has more structure than an appeal to personal preference. It distinguishes being guided by a principle from being the site of changing impulses, and distinguishes the normative standpoint from an external causal description. It also allows another person’s needs to remain the object of concern: identifying with a moral principle need not replace those needs with an expected personal reward. These distinctions answer a crude charge of psychological selfishness. They do not, on their own, establish that every rationally defensible principle must have the required interpersonal scope.

The crucial remaining question is therefore whether the humanity and publicity arguments establish their necessary connections. We should assess separately: a requirement to act on reasons; a requirement that reasons have some general form; valuing the capacity that makes agency possible; valuing other bearers of that capacity; and the authority of their claims on us. Failure at one transition would narrow the result without automatically showing that practical normativity is wholly illusory. Conversely, success at an earlier transition cannot substitute for the next one.

This is a conditional appraisal, not a validity proof or a verdict that either author has been defeated. Nagel’s realist alternative also owes an account of warrant for the evaluative reasons with which it begins. Korsgaard’s objection that he assumes too much in identifying reflection with an impersonal standpoint therefore matters. The rival stopping points must be compared under the same demand for justification; neither an irreducible reason nor a constitutive rule is warranted merely by its label.

## Value, obligation, categorical force and motivation

Value concerns what is worth caring about; obligation concerns a binding requirement; categorical force concerns applicability independently of contingent desires. These questions differ from what actually motivates compliance. They also differ from which requirement wins when obligations conflict. Korsgaard’s discussion allows conflicting obligations and does not claim that every moral obligation always overrides every personal commitment (pp. 125–128). Thus categorical force must not be silently rewritten as an exceptionless priority rule.

The constructive project is more ambitious about categorical reasons than the concession recorded for Railton in CL-T15-005. That ambition is now an inspected alternative, not a demonstrated success and not a reason to revise Railton’s attribution. Korsgaard also leaves complete practical normative skepticism unrefuted in §4.4.2. The packet does not interpret that qualification as either an empirical psychological diagnosis or an instruction about how to live.

An account of reflective authority is not a proof of all substantive duties. Korsgaard explicitly leaves detailed content and negotiation among obligations largely open (p. 92). Her reply’s treatment of an immoral practical identity preserves a sense of nonmoral obligation while arguing that fuller reflection supplies a deeper moral demand (pp. 255–258). That discussion answers an objection attributed there to Cohen; Cohen’s original chapter was not independently inspected and is not proposed as a fourth source. The exact status of partial reflection remains a cost of the theory.

The claims about pain, animal awareness, guilt and psychological development are philosophical premises or illustrations in this packet. No clinical study, animal-cognition experiment or evolutionary history was independently inspected. The distinction between agents capable of obligation and patients to whom duties are owed must be retained; extending concern beyond rational agents does not establish that all living things have the same standing.

## Relation to the consciousness project

The account gives consciousness a specified role: reflective agency helps explain demands on agents, while conscious animal life matters to the proposed scope of concern. It supplies no inference to one numerically shared subject, a nonphysical mind, survival or timelessness. The question of what unifies a practical agent is not an operational test of postmortem numerical identity.

A common conscious foundation might be proposed to unite subjects’ value, interpersonal claims and preservation. Such unification would be an additional grounding proposal with possible explanatory value. It would still need an argument connecting that foundation to the agency and authority relations identified here. Neither the existence of morally significant conscious subjects nor the possibility of reasoning together establishes the foundation’s existence, goodness or authority.

H-F, H-U, H-B, H-S, H-T and H-P receive no upgrade. H-I is not implied by finite agency. H-G and H-A remain conditional questions about the proposed foundation; moral value and objective reasons alone would not establish its goodness or authority. IR and X-EX remain distinct: the selected theory neither proves irreducible consciousness nor explains why reality or its laws exist. Removing endurance and cosmic-unity assumptions leaves the selected normative dispute intact, while removing the humanity/publicity bridges limits the constructivist moral conclusion.
