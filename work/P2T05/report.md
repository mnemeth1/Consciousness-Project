Frankish’s position is stronger than rejecting only ineffability, privacy or a particularly demanding definition of qualia: he denies genuinely qualitative properties, whether physical or nonphysical. He nevertheless retains experiences in an inclusive sense, their functions, reports and introspective representations. His original offers a philosophical programme and candidate content mechanisms, while expressly declining to supply a detailed theory. This distinction is essential to evaluating the account fairly. [Original, eprint pp1–4, 8–10, 20–22](https://keithfrankish.github.io/articles/Frankish_Illusionism%20as%20a%20theory%20of%20consciousness_eprint.pdf).

Nida-Rümelin’s strongest selected challenge concerns how the target is identified. She disputes the event-property framework, appeals to subjects’ experiential properties and introduces them through examples and shared reference. Her immediate-awareness argument says that instantiating such a property already involves awareness of it, without another potentially erroneous introspective operation. Her concluding anti-illusion result is explicitly conditional on an experiential example actually being instantiated. This is more developed than simply repeating that experience exists. It remains a philosophical argument with disputed premises. [Manuscript pp2–9, especially p7 and §4 on p9](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Martine-Nida-Ruemelin/publication/316515031_The_illusion_of_illusionism/links/5ef6271b92851c52d6fe5823/The-illusion-of-illusionism.pdf?origin=publication_detail).

Frankish’s actual reply grants the subject-property reformulation for argument, accepts ordinary examples and commonality, and disputes whether ostension fixes their realist nature. He offers a representational interpretation instead. He rejects robust unmediated acquaintance from his physicalist commitments and acknowledges that this will not convince Nida-Rümelin. His reply also corrects causal shorthand in the original, distinguishes illusionism from physicalism, and concedes that Russellian realism need not contradict science. Those qualifications constrain the argument attributed to him. [Reply, eprint pp4–7, 24–27](https://keithfrankish.github.io/articles/Frankish_Not%20disillusioned_eprint.pdf).

The illusion/regress objection needs its own treatment. Its strongest useful form asks whether the supposed illusion contains the phenomenal feature being eliminated, whether a merely propositional belief could capture apparent richness, and whether further representations only postpone that burden. Nida-Rümelin notes the first line and deliberately sets it aside; she does not provide an infinite-regress proof. The selected Frankish texts allow nonphenomenal seeming, reject a conscious internal spectator, and consider fine-grained representations and limited higher-order introspection. [Manuscript pp4–5](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Martine-Nida-Ruemelin/publication/316515031_The_illusion_of_illusionism/links/5ef6271b92851c52d6fe5823/The-illusion-of-illusionism.pdf?origin=publication_detail); [original pp17–21](https://keithfrankish.github.io/articles/Frankish_Illusionism%20as%20a%20theory%20of%20consciousness_eprint.pdf); [reply pp9–10, 23](https://keithfrankish.github.io/articles/Frankish_Not%20disillusioned_eprint.pdf).

The project assessment is conditional in both directions. Defining every seeming as phenomenal would assume the premise under dispute. Allowing a nonphenomenal representation avoids that immediate contradiction but does not explain its content or show that it captures the intended target. Likewise, reference by example can secure something to investigate without automatically resolving its nature; yet a proposed reinterpretation can fail if it discards the very feature the example was meant to identify. An adequate account must address this disagreement rather than win it by terminology. The Penrose/Gregundrum diagram on original p5 was visually checked; it illustrates possible misrepresentation and supplies no empirical mechanism for consciousness.
