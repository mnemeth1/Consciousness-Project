The 1981 model gives eternity four features: life, illimitability, duration and absence of succession. It differs from infinite temporal life and from the atemporality of an abstract object. The authors distinguish real temporal and eternal modes without making time illusory. Their ET relation connects an eternal and a temporal entity through presentness relative to each mode. It is symmetric but not reflexive or transitive: two temporal events related to the same eternal entity need not be temporally simultaneous. These are proposed conceptual relations, not evidence that such entities exist. The initial exposition leaves existence and the necessity of divine eternity unproved. (1981, pp. 430–440, especially p. 431 and pp. 439–440; CL-P2T12-001–002.)

The positive account distinguishes the temporal location of an effect from the mode of an agent’s action. An eternal act of willing could, on this account, have an effect at a particular time. A prayer and a later temporal response-effect could depend on that act without the eternal agent first learning the prayer and subsequently deciding to act. This conditional account assumes capacities whose possibility remains disputed; its mountain and prayer examples do not verify creation or prayer efficacy. The mental-life sketch excludes deliberation, memory and anticipation while proposing knowing, willing and awareness. An independent immaterial mind is assumed for argument, and the authors expressly describe their positive mental sketch as incomplete. (1981, pp. 446–451; CL-P2T12-004–005.)

Lewis 1984 objects where the relationship must support genuine observation and causation. His principle P requires a temporally observed object to exist at the time of observation or to have left a causal remnant then available for observation. He takes the divine observation in question to be immediate, excluding the remnant exception, and argues that an observed God belongs to the temporal order. This is a claimed broadly logical necessity, not an empirical finding. His distinction between beginning-or-ending temporal entities and unbounded entities existing alongside them blocks a simple equation of temporality with temporal boundaries. A defender can contest his transfer of temporal location to a timeless object; asserting either side’s necessity does not settle the issue. (1984, pp. 74–76; CL-P2T12-008–009.)

His causal objection asks whether an eternal cause belongs to reality when its effect occurs, and denies that common epistemic presentation by itself establishes possible causal connection. His knowledge objection is stronger than a complaint about calendar information. Lewis grants that an eternal being could know dated events and their temporal relations. He distinguishes being presently actual from belonging to actual history rather than a never-actual possibility. If the former is an objective feature, knowing every dated event does not automatically identify the presently actual one. Stump and Kretzmann’s 1981 knowledge of each temporal now answers the dated-location issue; Lewis disputes whether it captures the additional ontological distinction. Both the objective-present premise and the cross-mode answer remain contested. (1984, pp. 77–79, including n. 6; 1981, pp. 455–458; CL-P2T12-007, 010–011.)

Lewis 1988 develops a distinct argument. He interprets Boethius, Anselm and Aquinas as requiring temporal things to be existentially present in eternity, beyond their being objects of divine awareness. On his reading, the spatial-containment and circle analogies require different times to be equally real. He argues that eternal omniscience entails tenseless time and identifies tension with the tensed picture used to motivate eternity. Those historical editions were not independently reopened; the attribution is to Lewis’s inspected argument and quotations. His further rejection of tenseless moral persons does not become an established result. At p. 83 he relies on his separate *Persons, Morality and Tenselessness* (1986), cited at p. 86 n. 31. Revision 01 independently inspects that exact prior argument and Oaklander’s direct reply, described below. Its moral-agent conclusion remains a disputed inference, rather than an accepted denial of agency. (1988, pp. 75–86; CL-P2T12-012–014.)

The 1992 reply contests a requirement that direct awareness or metaphysical presence must involve one shared mode of existence. Stump and Kretzmann compare it with a nonspatial God’s awareness of spatial creatures and argue against treating the spatial and temporal cases differently without a reason. They replace the original observational wording with ET′, whose clauses include capacity for direct and immediate causal relations and, where capable, direct awareness from both eternal and temporal reference frames. This clarifies that the intended relation is more than a representation of an absent world. The paper targets Lewis 1988’s existential-presence objection alongside a Hasker objection. Hasker’s original was not independently read. (1992, pp. 475–478; p. 482 nn. 34–37; CL-P2T12-017–019.)

The reply is more than a new label, but its definition does not prove the capacities possible. The authors offer analogical grounds for rejecting the shared-mode requirement, and those grounds need assessment alongside the definition. Their imagined Aleph world stipulates cognitive and causal access across dimensions. It illustrates how a mistaken restriction might arise, but does not establish that the same answer works for time. They acknowledge at p. 477 that three-dimensional spatial here lacks an analogue of the absolute temporal present, while n. 33 maintains compatibility with real temporal passage. Lewis’s strongest tense objection therefore remains substantive. The 1992 reply does not supply a complete response to the earlier 1984 arguments or the separate 1986 responsibility argument; the latter now has its own inspected Oaklander response. No absence of other replies is inferred. (1992, pp. 470–478, 481–482; CL-P2T12-020–021.)

The 1992 defense also uses analogical predication and a specious-present illustration. Neither is new empirical psychology. The authors distinguish an allegedly indivisible experienced whole from its divisible underlying interval, and n. 21 qualifies a possible reading of James. Their treatments of Fitzgerald, Nelson and Leftow remain the defenders’ presentations; they do not substitute for independent inspection of those critics. The original 1981 relativity discussion is an introductory analogy, after which the authors revert to a simpler temporal framework. It is not a derivation of an eternal mind from relativity. Lewis explicitly distinguishes questions about what persons are and what cross-time sameness means from the evidence needed to identify a particular person at a later time; he sets the evidential question aside at p. 306. His argument therefore concerns metaphysical identity, not merely a title’s suggestion of it. He claims tenselessness requires temporal composites with distinct stages and contrasts them with strictly continuing persons under a tensed ontology. At pp. 307–308 he challenges responsibility both for the whole, which he denies is itself conscious, and for a stage held answerable for another stage’s deed. He also rejects bundles as suitable bearers. These denials are premises of his objection, not measured facts. (Lewis 1986, pp. 305–308; CL-P2T12-027–029.)

His stronger action argument says that an embodied act and many mental activities require an interval during which the agent persists. A momentary stage does not last through it; the tenseless whole, Lewis argues, cannot intend or have bodily members. If those claims hold, there are no agents performing the relevant actions and thus no moral responsibility for them. He retains the quoted Quinn passage’s qualifications about possible instantaneous mental or disembodied acts. His use of *perduring* means persisting in this discussion; it must not silently be read as endorsement of the modern technical temporal-parts account he opposes. (Lewis 1986, pp. 308–309, especially p. 309 n.5; CL-P2T12-030.)

Oaklander first disputes the supposed entailment from tenselessness to temporal parts, presenting Mellor and Butterfield as counterexamples. He explains a token-reflexive treatment of present-tense judgments with a continuing person, but expressly leaves the tense debate open at p. 183; n.8 separately flags his concern with Mellor’s tenseless facts. He then offers temporally indexed properties of one person and distinguishes reference to a stage from reference to the whole person whose stage it is. Different stages need not be identical for the whole referred to at those stages to be the same person. His broad use of *continuant* and *enduring* includes this whole-with-parts analysis and should not erase the technical endurance/perdurance distinction. These are proposed alternatives to Lewis’s necessity claim, not an empirical or comprehensive vindication of each account. (Oaklander, pp. 181–185; pp. 186–187 nn.4–8,12–13; CL-P2T12-031–033.)

Oaklander finally distinguishes an ordinary same-agent requirement from a particular simple-substance interpretation. He sketches psychological connection or memory and physical continuity of a living human body as other analyses of persistence. If the ordinary requirement is neutral, denying one substantialist analysis does not eliminate all agents; if it already assumes simple substances, the argument risks assuming the disputed conclusion. His closing suggestion about what Lewis might reply is hypothetical, not an actual subsequent response. These sketches do not resolve branching, circular memory evidence, interruption, or the identification of an alleged surviving subject. Nor does the indexed temporal structure become a nonsuccessive human mind. The new exchange answers the missing bounded original-and-reply coverage requirement for review; it does not settle identity or establish survival. (Oaklander, pp. 185–187 nn.14–15; Lewis 1986, p. 306; CL-P2T12-034–038.)

Human persistence is a separate requirement. In the 1981 hypothetical Nixon example, the authors expressly deny that temporal life and death being present to eternity means Nixon himself exists eternally. ET′ relates distinct entities; it supplies neither numerical identity nor a constitution rule for human subjects. An eternal knower’s awareness of a finite life does not establish experiences beyond that life, and any proposed further experiences would require a defended same-person relation to count as survival. The brief incarnation proposal is rudimentary and depends on a special dual-nature doctrine, not a general human identity criterion. Animalist, psychological, simple-bearer, branching and interruption questions remain open. Accepted P2T02 contributes derivative limits on inferring survival from substantial selfhood, not fresh source inspection or an automatic bridge. (1981, p. 443 and pp. 451–453; 1992, pp. 477–478; CL-P2T12-003, 006, 022–023.)
