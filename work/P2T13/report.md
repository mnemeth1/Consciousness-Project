The inspected exchange strengthens the distinction between losing further valuable life and erasing the value of a life already lived. Yoshizawa develops the strongest selected final-loss challenge to Metz’s treatment of immortality, but ultimately rejects the inference that death makes every life meaningless. Metz’s actual reply grants the importance of that challenge while disputing whether its rationale really escapes an appeal to perfection. Wolf’s final first lecture and Fischer’s exact reprint offer different, compatible conditional possibilities: finite engagement may matter, and extended engagement may be desirable. Neither conclusion verifies survival, a creator’s purpose, or the necessity of endless existence for every kind of meaning.

**The selected objection and actual reply.** Yoshizawa’s complete 2015 article is available as 16 journal pages, 134–149, preceded by a repository wrapper. His analysis distinguishes the person who possesses a meaning-making property from the life assessed as meaningful; it also separates whole/part lives and pro tanto/on-balance assessment (pp. 135–138). His central criticism targets Metz’s 2013 chapter 7, not the 2000 purpose article. His earlier Japanese paper concerned Metz 2003; the 2015 article explicitly identifies this development (p. 141 n. 8). These are three distinct original versions.

The final-loss rationale is stronger than an objection about boredom: even an engaged life and ordinary loving relationships end, and another person’s death can remove a valued relationship. On this reading, the attraction of endurance need not begin with a demand for unusually exalted goods. Yoshizawa reconstructs that rationale at pp. 139–145. It would nevertheless misrepresent his position to cite him as endorsing the necessity of immortality. He distinguishes an existing person without meaning-making properties from the absence of a person altogether, and rejects the general inference from the latter to a meaningless life (p. 146, especially nn. 18–19). Particular deaths may prevent achievements; this does not make all finite lives meaningless. Nor does his interpretation establish what Tolstoy actually intended: p. 141 n. 9 expressly withholds that historical claim.

Metz’s 2015 §3.1, pp. 253–254, directly identifies Yoshizawa and acknowledges that defending ordinary goods against loss differs from demanding perfect goods. He objects that saying death ends an already meaningful life concedes the point denied by a strict immortality requirement. He suggests a formulation under which ending the activities or relationships is itself what makes life meaningless. He then asks whether love or contemplation retained forever is already ideal, and whether imperfection best explains the claimed loss of meaning. His proposed response on Yoshizawa’s behalf is hypothetical; it is not an inspected later Yoshizawa rebuttal. The unresolved issue is the bridge from an ending to lack of significance, and whether endless duration changes the relevant kind of value. Neither author’s argumentative concession settles that bridge.

**Finite meaning in the final Wolf text.** The retrieved book bears Princeton’s 2010 copyright and ISBN 9780691145242. The complete first lecture, pp. 1–33, is freshly read; the second lecture, comments and response remain outside the selected extent. It retains the combination of fitting concern, independently worthy objects and positive engagement, rather than pleasure alone (pp. 8–10, 17–26). Being interested in something is not sufficient, and incidental benefits from an activity do not automatically provide the actor with the relevant engagement (pp. 20–22). The final text explicitly says its appeal to the quality of one’s contribution does not directly answer distress about smallness or the absence of a large, lasting impact (p. 29 n. 14). This repairs the version gap for the selected v1 claims without asserting identity with the earlier lecture draft. Page 33 also explicitly declines to promise a theory or infallible test of objective value; the selected reading does not resolve moral grounding or all meanings of “meaning.”

**Endurance as a possible enhancement.** Fischer’s complete 15-page review copy, printed 224–238, separates numerical identity from the attractiveness of future life. Absorption in an activity need not eliminate ownership of its experiences (pp. 224–227). A mixed life need not consist of one relentlessly repeated activity, and an appealing life need not be agreeable at every instant (pp. 228–229). Repeatable pleasures, appropriately spaced and relative to the individual, might continue to support categorical desires; changes arising through reflection might remain attractive even where coercive transformations would not (pp. 230–237). These are philosophical possibility arguments supported by examples and analogies, not empirical observation of immortals. The stronger Williams concern about exhausted projects and present concern for a remote successor remains a live challenge under the accepted original review. Merely extrapolating familiar repetition indefinitely does not establish that every person or every compulsory endless life remains worthwhile.

**Purpose, context and remaining bridges.** Metz’s selected 2015 pp. 230–234 separate an individual’s variable meaning from questions about the human species and distinguish the object assessed from a natural or supernatural source of value. The declared pp. 254–257 include his concession that he did not conclusively establish either the impossibility of timeless purposiveness or the necessity of his particular divine qualitative properties. These are partial extents of §§2.1 and 3.2; this packet does not claim a complete Tartaglia or Poettcker exchange or substitute their quotations for their originals. The now-inspected exact Metz 2000 text and the separate, uninspected Affolter 2007 criticism/reply are not interchangeable with that 2015 exchange.

The complete 2000 article, printed 293–313 with notes 1–27, now repairs the exact original gap. Metz explicitly defines purpose theory as conditional: it does not assert that God exists, and it can permit meaning without an afterlife (pp. 293–295). Assignment and fulfilment are distinct, and ignorance of the purpose does not alone refute an account of what grounds meaning (pp. 295–297). His replies to the disrespect objection depend on a respectful request for free moral action; they do not demonstrate that any actual purpose, punishment or creator is justified (pp. 297–302). His strongest objection concerns strict divine necessity rather than mere enhancement: the allegedly unique value of divine atemporality, simplicity, immutability and infinitude is set against purposiveness (pp. 304–310). The support for those values is expressly sketchy. Crucially, the original already says the objection does not require disbelief and offers compatibility and alternative-grounding response strategies (p. 311; n. 26 at p. 313). The 2015 qualifications therefore do not establish a new wholesale retreat. Neither this analysis nor its hypothetical universe, free-choice or travel examples verifies a metaphysical or empirical premise.

For the project’s decision, necessity, sufficiency and enhancement require different premises. Desirable added experience presupposes a bearer whose future remains appropriately related to that person and contains relevant goods. It does not establish actual future experience. Preservation in memory, records, a completed history or another knower’s awareness can preserve information or value without extending the original person’s subjectivity. Yoshizawa’s p. 147 n. 20 likewise distinguishes four-dimensional existence from eternity; his citation is not an independent inspection of Singer or a demonstration of a timeless mind. An intended purpose further requires agency, intention, an end and a grounding relation; its worth requires an additional normative premise. Consciousness occurring does not establish that it was intended.
