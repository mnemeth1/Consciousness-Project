The central attribution is sound. Yoshizawa reconstructs a final-loss rationale: ordinary goods and relationships may be valuable, yet an advocate can worry that their ending defeats significance. But he ultimately rejects the general necessity inference, distinguishing absence of a person from an existing person without meaning-making properties (146, nn. 18–19). He allows particular deaths to prevent achievements and does not thereby declare every finite life meaningless. His note 9 at 141 also declines to certify Tolstoy’s historical intention. Those qualifications are preserved throughout the submitted claims and argument.

Metz’s actual 2015 reply at 253–254 grants the distinction between losing imperfect goods and lacking perfect ones. He argues that an “already meaningful” life concedes what strict necessity denies, reformulates the rationale, and questions whether indefinitely retained goods escape ideal value. His proposed further response on Yoshizawa’s behalf is hypothetical. The packet does not invent a later rebuttal or mistake recognition of a rationale for endorsement. The strongest unresolved issue is still the connection between ending and whole-life meaninglessness; merely describing an eternal good as perfect does not independently settle its explanatory role.
