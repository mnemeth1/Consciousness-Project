It works through the moral-grounding audit's evil and hiddenness material and the Phase 2 deepening of both exchanges. ## Benevolence as a testable specification

<!-- id: c10-p003 -->
The article frames the matter as a trade, and the trade is worth stating plainly. Adding goodness to a conscious foundation increases both its attraction and its vulnerability. A life-valuing agent might explain why conscious beings arise. But suffering and nonresistant nonbelief then become relevant to what such an agent would permit. The objections that follow target specified benevolence, knowledge, power or openness to relationship. They need not undermine every possible conscious ground in order to weaken a particular providential explanation. This is the discrimination discipline of the meaning chapter's factory design, applied to a named attribute package. A specified benevolent intention generates expectations, and expectations can be tested.

<!-- id: c10-p004 -->
The evidential argument from suffering is taken from its inspected source, and its author's own concessions come with it. Rowe's argument targets an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being, and it does so through preventable suffering that lacks the relevant goods or the evil-avoidance constraint. He concedes that the hypothetical fawn does not prove gratuitousness; he claims rational support rather than certainty, and he permits rational theistic countergrounds. His permission conditions are stated as necessary rather than sufficient, with footnotes qualifying irreplaceability and equal-value cases [@S-T15-003]. Three cautions apply. The fawn is a thought experiment, not a case record. Rowe's statements about the abundance of suffering are his premises, not a new empirical survey. And all three claims are recorded as attribution only.

<!-- id: c10-p005 -->
The audit's reconstruction keeps the argument evidential rather than deductive. Suppose an agent who knows the relevant harms and the feasible alternatives, who can prevent them, and who is perfectly good. Suppose also suffering that appears to lack any dependence on goods, or on the avoidance of comparable evil, that would justify permitting it. The reconstruction then uses two bridges. Our inability to find such a dependence is defeasible evidence of its absence in the relevant domain. And a perfectly good agent does not permit harms without adequate justification, with the equal-value and irreplaceability qualifications specified. The conclusion the audit records is conditional: suffering challenges that attribute package if the evidential and normative premises hold. It is not a deduction from the mere occurrence of any evil. The objection it records is the limited-sample worry. Our grasp of goods, causal relations and feasible worlds may be too small to assess what an omniscient being could justifiably permit. The reply it records is that finite limits do not automatically erase all evidence. Compare what each hypothesis actually leads us to expect, and ask what independently supports the claimed limitations; and theistic countergrounds can rationally matter. No measured prevalence or likelihood is supplied anywhere in the record, and the free-will and soul-making originals beyond the inspected discussions remain declared gaps.

<!-- id: c10-p006 -->
The proposed justifying goods are recorded with their scope limits. Freedom and developmental opportunity can be proposed as goods. But their value alone does not justify every harm, every denied opportunity or animal suffering; scope and feasible alternatives matter. This is a project-authored conditional analysis, and it prices the goods on its own terms. A good that would justify some harms does not automatically justify these harms, for these victims, when these alternatives are in view. The restoration and hiddenness defenses below are measured differently, by their own records of distinct burdens and comparative means.

<!-- id: c10-p007 -->
The symmetry rule takes a specifically epistemic form here. Unknown divine reasons can weaken an inference from unrecognized purpose. But unrestricted skepticism about our access to divine reasons weakens positive claims of recognizable divine goodness just as much. The cost depends on the scope of the skepticism. The discipline the audit records is local skepticism rather than blanket skepticism: name what remains knowable, and test whether the positive and negative inferences genuinely rely on the same inaccessible domain. This constraint returns at the chapter's close as the article's second internal cost.

## Horrendous evils and person-level defeat

<!-- id: c10-p008 -->
Adams's response to horrendous evils, inspected in the original symposium contribution, is recorded as the morally stronger form of reply, and the reason is where it locates the repair. She requires goodness within each participant's life, not merely a favorable global total. She distinguishes outweighing from an integration that defeats a horror's threat to personal meaning, and here she invokes divine intimacy. And she argues that internal consistency can be defended through how persons are restored, without known divine reasons why; she acknowledges that the account is conceptually underdeveloped [@S-T15-004]. As the article puts it, this keeps the victim in view as the subject whose life requires repair, rather than an impersonal balance of aggregate goods.

<!-- id: c10-p009 -->
The Phase 2 inspection sharpens what Adams is doing and what she is not doing. Her project is an internal consistency test. It uses the relevant religious attributes and valuables together with the kinds and amounts of evil, and availability within the system does not independently establish actuality. Her 1989 text already states an objective, individually relative criterion of horror that covers both doing and suffering; so the later clarification cannot be credited with first introducing objectivity. She distinguishes global goods from goodness to each created person, and she requires defeat within each participant's life, perpetrators included. She retains reasons for permission, while arguing that to specify how God could be good to each participant may exhibit compossibility without identifying why permission occurs [@S-T15-004]. The limit the packet records is decisive for what follows. An unknown why is not no reason, and how-restoration does not by itself demonstrate morally adequate permission.

<!-- id: c10-p010 -->
Her constructive proposals are recorded with their assumptions in plain view. She distinguishes engulfing a horror's threat from meaningful defeat through divine intimacy, and she proposes alternative Christological or mystical integrations. These alternatives carry different assumptions, and there is no observed restoration, no verified survival and no cumulative corroboration behind any of them. She acknowledges conceptual underdevelopment concerning shared pain and Christological metaphysics, and her cited further work is uninspected in this packet [@S-T15-004].

<!-- id: c10-p011 -->
Sutherland's criticism, inspected in the same volume, presses on what integration cannot settle. He distinguishes reassurance, or a worthwhile outcome, from the justice of the earlier harm. He presses the claims of other victims and the preservation of moral judgment: reassurance experienced by one observer cannot settle other victims' claims for them [@S-T15-005]. His strongest objection is recorded as surviving his own disputable first-person reading of Adams. A printed-317 pagination anomaly is preserved in the record and not silently corrected. The two contributions share one PDF container, and the accounting section carries that dependence fact.

<!-- id: c10-p012 -->
The revised Adams reproduction answers part of the criticism, within declared version limits. The inspected reproduction explicitly clarifies that horror gives everyone a prima facie reason for concern about the participant's life. It answers Sutherland's reason/emotion criticism by presenting intimate relationship as a good that supplies a reason to value the participant's life as a whole. And it denies that defeat entails that the original evil lacked horror, injustice or seriousness, or that moral intuitions about it were unreliable [@S-T15-004]. Each claim carries the same limit. This is an author-attributed reproduction whose exact 1990 print identity remains unverified, and denying an alleged implication is not demonstrating actual defeat or adequate permission. The inspected passage that answers Sutherland also contains a visibly incomplete first proposition, which has been preserved rather than silently completed.

<!-- id: c10-p013 -->
The recorded adjudication of the whole exchange separates three burdens. The article states Sutherland's other-victims point in its own sentence, and it then closes on a two-item distinction between a possible mode of defeat and actual restoration with justified permission. The inspected exchange narrows the reply gap. But it leaves participant restoration, observers' reassurance and the justification of the original permission as distinct burdens. Restoration can improve an account of what becomes of victims, while an account of why harms were permitted remains independently contestable. The audit preserves both the objection and the reply. The objection is that a later good may quiet grief without making the original injustice permissible, and that one person's reconciliation cannot speak for another victim. The reply, in its strongest version, addresses each victim and distinguishes redemption from approval of wrongdoing; an internal consistency defense is entitled to its own specified goods, without claiming that their actual existence is thereby proved. Adams's later clarifications, and any reply to Sutherland beyond the reproduction, remain uninspected.

## Hiddenness and the comparative question

<!-- id: c10-p014 -->
Schellenberg's hiddenness argument enters through a chain of versions, and the record declares the chain. The inspected text is his 2025 prepublication précis, which substitutes for an inaccessible 2004 chapter. In it, perfect love is linked to an available conscious relationship whenever a person is capable and nonresistant, and belief is required for that specified relationship. The resistance in question must be causally relevant to the nonbelief, so unrelated culpability does not defeat the premise. His own scope limits are recorded too: the conclusion does not establish metaphysical naturalism, and it does not exclude every nonpersonal religious ultimate [@S-T15-006]. The audit's reconstruction adds the conditional form. If the premises hold, the specified perfectly loving personal God is excluded; other ultimate foundations remain open.

<!-- id: c10-p015 -->
The contested bridge is named in the record. The argument needs perfect love to entail availability at each relevant time, and not only eventual welfare or eventual access. The audit marks this as the point a defense must attack, for a reason to delay developing a relationship does not automatically explain withholding the conditions of being open to one. No observed distribution of dispositions, and no actual divine reasons for permission, appear anywhere in the record. Availability, fittingness and the no-worse-alternative criterion all remain disputed.

<!-- id: c10-p016 -->
Howard-Snyder's defense, inspected complete, is recorded with its structure and its stipulations. He distinguishes immediate desire from all-things-considered desire. He explicitly modifies the earlier hiddenness argument with an overriding-reason qualification and a separate no-reason premise; that is his reconstruction, and the record forbids transferring it into Schellenberg's 1993 or 2025 wording. He grants inculpable nonbelief for the sake of argument. He stipulates, rather than establishes, a prima facie criterion that permits a means no worse than the alternatives, and he does not claim that unaided cognition identifies an actual sufficient divine reason. The record preserves a printed premise-number anomaly at page 439 without silent correction. His account distinguishes belief in God's existence from willing reciprocity; belief is not loving reciprocation. His central good is relational. Delayed disclosure might allow a person freely to own or to improve a disposition to love, and he distinguishes ownership of an initially unchosen love from the greater fittingness of a love voluntarily cultivated. The extension from his first disposition type to the other two is assumed without argument [@S-T15-009].

<!-- id: c10-p017 -->
His concessions are recorded as carefully as his defense. By his own note, the sketched reason fails for the willing seeker who recognizes deficient love and strongly desires improvement. A sufficiently fitting seeker escapes his objection, though our ability to identify such a seeker is disputed. The account is limited to temporary nonbelief. It leaves cultural distribution unaddressed. It presupposes survival if the delay lasts a whole earthly life. And it does not rule out eventual coerced love as a second-best possibility [@S-T15-009]. None of these concessions is proof about the actual population, but every one of them prices the defense.

<!-- id: c10-p018 -->
The epistemic fallback is the defense's last line, and the record splits it into two parts. Howard-Snyder's final move challenges the inference from our failure to conceive of justifying reasons to their absence. That clause is attribution. The recorded addition is a project inference: the fallback's relation to the positive recognition of goodness requires a scope argument. Local uncertainty about divine reasons does not automatically license blanket uncertainty, and blanket uncertainty would cost the theist's positive claims too. This is the same symmetry stated at c10-p007, now located inside the inspected defense.

<!-- id: c10-p019 -->
Schellenberg's actual 1996 response, inspected complete, restructures the dispute rather than merely rebutting it. He disputes the inserted overriding-reason clause and the independent no-reason premise, and he proposes positive defeasible support for divine intention instead. He accepts a commitment to the absence of overriding reasons, and he accepts vulnerability to actual counterconsiderations; what he denies is any requirement to establish that absence by an independent Rowe-style inference [@S-P2T08-001]. The packet's structural point is this. Accepting an entailed proposition differs from independently establishing it, so the target of Howard-Snyder's fallback depends on which argument is being defended. That distinction alone establishes no full positive case and no metaphysical conclusion, and the positive case of the 1993 book is referenced but not read.

<!-- id: c10-p020 -->
His three substantive responses are recorded with their status as premises marked. First, he contests capacity in cases of certain powerless rejection, and against likely-rejection cases he invokes the manner of disclosure and continued help, with a note denying any inference to the acceptance of actual-world counterparts. Second, he argues that freely confirming love and choosing its continuation can occur after belief, and that neither timing changes the origin of the initial disposition. Third, he proposes improving fittingness within belief, distinguishes experienced absence from nonbelief, and disputes the human-lover analogy through four divine-human disanalogies [@S-P2T08-001]. Every one of these is held in the records as a philosophical comparative premise. They are theological counterpossibilities, not observed psychology and not quantified likelihoods.

<!-- id: c10-p021 -->
The recorded adjudication leaves the exchange genuinely open, and it does so under a chronology that the ledger requires and the article's summary does not supply. The selected exchange leaves positive warrant for universal access, and for comparative developmental means, contested. Neither the imagined benefits of delay nor the reply establishes actual divine conduct. Howard-Snyder's available internal replies (ownership versus fittingness, improvement versus perfection, prima facie versus conclusive) are defenses already present in his 1996 article. They are not a later rebuttal to Schellenberg's response, and no such later rebuttal was inspected. Notice the dates. The 1996 reply addresses the earlier Schellenberg formulation, while the 2025 précis emphasizes resistance causally relevant to nonbelief. The exchange is real, it is complete on neither side, and it is split across three decades of versions. The article's summary survives all of this. The issue is comparative: does the better-relationship rationale warrant withholding an available relationship, rather than improving the person within it? It is not whether spiritual development is conceivable.

## Internal costs: survival reliance and inscrutability symmetry

<!-- id: c10-p022 -->
The first internal cost is survival reliance, and the record states it as a circularity constraint. A delay that lasts an earthly lifetime relies on survival if the relationship is ever to occur; that is Howard-Snyder's own recorded limitation. The audit's synthesis premise makes the general rule explicit: claims of actual survival, or of intended moral education, need evidence apart from their usefulness in a reply. In other words, the survival that the hiddenness defense needs cannot be inferred from the defense's need for it. The empirical and continuity chapters of this thesis hold the survival record, and nothing in this chapter's material adds to it. The same rule binds the restoration defense. Adams's integration proposals presuppose continued same-person life and coherent divine intimacy. Both are recorded as contested, and neither is supplied by the defense that requires them.

<!-- id: c10-p023 -->
The second internal cost is inscrutability symmetry. If unknown reasons answer every discrepancy between suffering or nonbelief and a good agent's supposed preferences, then the recognition of favorable evidence needs equal scrutiny. In the form the audit records it, unrestricted skepticism weakens positive claims of recognizable divine goodness at the same rate that it weakens the objections. The record is explicit that this is not a refutation of every appeal to limited human understanding. The reply it records distinguishes local skepticism from blanket skepticism, and it asks whether the positive and negative inferences rely on the same inaccessible domain. What survives is the article's constraint. A proposal may not use specified intentions to explain life while treating their apparent failures as wholly inscrutable.

<!-- id: c10-p024 -->
The chapter's constructive point is the article's closing sentence, and it rests on the audit's design discipline. A benevolent unification must allow its claims about goodness to restrict what counts as an expected world. This is the same precommitment requirement that the meaning chapter recorded for the consciousness-factory comparison, now applied to the benevolence attribute: declared ends, declared constraints, and outcomes that can weaken the model because the commitments were fixed in advance. A specified benevolent foundation that predicts nothing is not strengthened by the evil and hiddenness records. It discriminates weakly. Until its ends, population, means, constraints and tradeoffs are fixed in advance, it cannot face the qualitative tests that tightly constrained variants can face. And the tests differ by specification. Specified developmental purposes and specified preserving purposes face different conditional tests, concerning deprivation, opportunity and same-subject preservation. That is the recorded reason why it is the specification, not the family, that the evidence engages.

<!-- id: c10-p025 -->
What the objections accomplish is bounded on both sides. They weaken particular providential specifications if their premises hold. They do not undermine every possible conscious ground; that is Schellenberg's own recorded scope limit, and it is the article's too. Nothing in the record supplies a verdict of equal support between the specified benevolent foundation and its rivals, and nothing licenses the reverse inference, that surviving these objections confirms the specification. The recorded sensitivity note also carries forward. Rejecting moral realism would change both the moral argument and some formulations of the evil objections, though internal inconsistency arguments could still run on an opponent's own commitments.

The value judgments that support the moral-grounding proposal of the previous chapter also underwrite the evil objections here. They are one set of premises, not two bodies of evidence. Knowledge of goodness and knowledge of permission-justifying reasons can have different scope, so a defense may not spend the same epistemic humility twice. The audit's synthesis maps these burdens to the goodness and authority components, with conditional effects on intention, preservation and survival, and it identifies neither a numerical posterior nor a winning metaphysics. Let me be clear about what this chapter has not shown, and cannot conclude.
