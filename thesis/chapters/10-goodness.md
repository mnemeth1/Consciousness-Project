## Benevolence as a testable specification

<!-- id: c10-p003 -->
The article's framing is a trade the record preserves exactly: adding goodness to a conscious foundation increases both its attraction and its vulnerability. A life-valuing agent might explain why conscious beings arise — but suffering and nonresistant nonbelief then become relevant to what such an agent would permit. The objections that follow target specified benevolence, knowledge, power or openness to relationship; they need not undermine every possible conscious ground to weaken a particular providential explanation. This is the discrimination discipline of the meaning chapter's factory design applied to a named attribute package: a specified benevolent intention generates expectations, and expectations can be tested.

<!-- id: c10-p004 -->
The evidential argument from suffering is held at its inspected source with its own concessions. Rowe's argument targets an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being through preventable suffering lacking the relevant goods or evil-avoidance constraint; he concedes that the hypothetical fawn does not prove gratuitousness, claiming rational support rather than certainty and permitting rational theistic countergrounds; and his permission conditions are stated as necessary rather than sufficient, with footnotes qualifying irreplaceability and equal-value cases [@S-T15-003]. The fawn is a thought experiment, not a case record; Rowe's statements about the abundance of suffering are his premises, not a new empirical survey; and the records mark all three claims as attribution only.

<!-- id: c10-p005 -->
The audit's reconstruction keeps the argument evidential, not deductive. Given an agent who knows the relevant harms and feasible alternatives, can prevent them and is perfectly good, and given suffering that appears to lack a permission-justifying dependence on goods or the avoidance of comparable evil, the recorded bridges are two: our inability to find such dependence is defeasible evidence of its absence in the relevant domain, and a perfectly good agent does not permit harms without adequate justification, with the equal-value and irreplaceability qualifications specified. The recorded conclusion is conditional — suffering challenges that attribute package if the evidential and normative premises hold; it is not a deduction from the mere occurrence of any evil. The recorded objection is the limited-sample worry: our grasp of goods, causal relations and feasible worlds may be too small to assess what an omniscient being could justifiably permit. The recorded reply: finite limits do not automatically erase all evidence; compare what each hypothesis actually leads us to expect and what independently supports the claimed limitations — and theistic countergrounds can rationally matter. No measured prevalence or likelihood is supplied anywhere in the record, and the free-will and soul-making originals beyond the inspected discussions remain declared gaps.

<!-- id: c10-p006 -->
The proposed justifying goods are recorded with their scope limits. Freedom and developmental opportunity can be proposed goods — but their value alone does not justify every harm, denied opportunity or animal suffering; scope and feasible alternatives matter. This project-authored conditional analysis prices the goods on its own terms — a good that would justify some harms does not automatically justify these harms, for these victims, when these alternatives are in view — while the restoration and hiddenness defenses below are measured by their own distinct-burden and comparative-means records.

<!-- id: c10-p007 -->
The symmetry rule has a specifically epistemic form here. Unknown divine reasons can weaken an inference from unrecognized purpose — but unrestricted skepticism about our access to divine reasons equally weakens positive claims of recognizable divine goodness; the cost depends on the scope of the skepticism. The recorded discipline is local rather than blanket skepticism: name what remains knowable, and test whether the positive and negative inferences genuinely rely on the same inaccessible domain. This constraint returns as the article's second internal cost at the chapter's close.

## Horrendous evils and person-level defeat

<!-- id: c10-p008 -->
Adams's response to horrendous evils, inspected in the original symposium contribution, is recorded as the morally stronger reply form because of where it locates the repair. She requires goodness within each participant's life, not merely a favorable global total; distinguishes outweighing from integration that defeats a horror's threat to personal meaning, invoking divine intimacy; and argues that internal consistency can be defended through how persons are restored without known divine reasons why — while acknowledging conceptual underdevelopment [@S-T15-004]. As the article puts it, this preserves the victim as the subject whose life requires repair, rather than an impersonal balance of aggregate goods.

<!-- id: c10-p009 -->
The Phase 2 inspection sharpens what Adams is and is not doing. Her project is an internal consistency test using the relevant religious attributes and valuables together with the kinds and amounts of evil — system-internal availability does not independently establish actuality. Her 1989 text already states an objective, individually relative criterion of horror encompassing doing and suffering, so the later clarification cannot be credited with first introducing objectivity. She distinguishes global goods from goodness to each created person and requires defeat within each participant's life, including perpetrators; and she retains reasons for permission while arguing that specifying how God could be good to each participant may exhibit compossibility without identifying why permission occurs [@S-T15-004]. The recorded limit is decisive for what follows: unknown why is not no reason, and how-restoration does not by itself demonstrate morally adequate permission.

<!-- id: c10-p010 -->
Her constructive proposals are recorded with their assumptions exposed. She distinguishes engulfing a horror's threat from meaningful defeat through divine intimacy, and proposes alternative Christological or mystical integrations — alternatives with different assumptions, no observed restoration, no verified survival, no cumulative corroboration; and she acknowledges conceptual underdevelopment concerning shared pain and Christological metaphysics, with her cited further work uninspected in this packet [@S-T15-004].

<!-- id: c10-p011 -->
Sutherland's criticism, inspected in the same volume, presses precisely what integration cannot settle. He distinguishes reassurance or a worthwhile outcome from the justice of the earlier harm, and presses the claims of other victims and the preservation of moral judgment — reassurance experienced by one observer cannot settle other victims' claims for them [@S-T15-005]. The record holds his strongest objection as surviving his own disputable first-person reading of Adams, and preserves a printed-317 pagination anomaly without silently correcting it. The two contributions share one PDF container, a dependence fact the accounting section carries.

<!-- id: c10-p012 -->
The revised Adams reproduction answers part of the criticism, within declared version limits. The inspected reproduction explicitly clarifies that horror gives everyone a prima facie reason for concern about the participant's life; answers Sutherland's reason/emotion criticism by presenting intimate relationship as a good that supplies a reason to value the participant's life as a whole; and denies that defeat entails the original evil lacked horror, injustice or seriousness, or that moral intuitions about it were unreliable [@S-T15-004]. Each claim carries the same limit: this is an author-attributed reproduction whose exact 1990 print identity remains unverified, and a denial of an alleged implication is not a demonstration of actual defeat or adequate permission; the inspected passage answering Sutherland also retains a visibly incomplete first proposition, preserved rather than silently completed.

<!-- id: c10-p013 -->
The recorded adjudication of the whole exchange separates three burdens; the article states Sutherland's other-victims point in its own sentence and then closes on a two-item distinction between a possible mode of defeat and actual restoration with justified permission. The inspected exchange narrows the reply gap but leaves participant restoration, observers' reassurance and justification of original permission as distinct burdens; restoration can improve an account of what becomes of victims, while an account of why harms were permitted remains independently contestable. The recorded objection and reply are both preserved: a later good may quiet grief without making the original injustice permissible, and one person's reconciliation cannot speak for another victim; the strongest version addresses each victim and distinguishes redemption from approval of wrongdoing, with an internal consistency defense entitled to its own specified goods without claiming their actual existence is thereby proved. Adams's later clarifications and any reply to Sutherland beyond the reproduction remain uninspected.

## Hiddenness and the comparative question

<!-- id: c10-p014 -->
Schellenberg's hiddenness argument enters through a version chain the record declares. The inspected text is his 2025 prepublication précis, substituting for an inaccessible 2004 chapter: perfect love is linked to available conscious relationship whenever a person is capable and nonresistant, with belief required for that specified relationship; the required resistance must be causally relevant to nonbelief, so unrelated culpability does not defeat the premise; and his own scope limits are recorded — the conclusion does not establish metaphysical naturalism or exclude every nonpersonal religious ultimate [@S-T15-006]. The audit's reconstruction adds the conditional form: if the premises hold, the specified perfectly loving personal God is excluded; other ultimate foundations remain open.

<!-- id: c10-p015 -->
The contested bridge is recorded by name. The argument needs perfect love to entail availability at each relevant time, rather than only eventual welfare or eventual access — and the audit marks this as the point a defense must attack: a reason to delay developing a relationship does not automatically explain withholding the conditions of being open to one. No observed distribution of dispositions and no actual divine permission reasons appear anywhere in the record; availability, fittingness and the no-worse-alternative criterion all remain disputed.

<!-- id: c10-p016 -->
Howard-Snyder's defense, inspected complete, is recorded with its structure and its stipulations. He distinguishes immediate from all-things-considered desire and explicitly modifies the earlier hiddenness argument with an overriding-reason qualification and a separate no-reason premise — a reconstruction the record forbids transferring into Schellenberg's 1993 or 2025 wording. He grants inculpable nonbelief for argument and stipulates — rather than establishes — a prima facie criterion permitting a means no worse than alternatives, without claiming unaided cognition identifies an actual sufficient divine reason; the record preserves a printed premise-number anomaly at page 439 without silent correction. His account distinguishes belief in God's existence from willing reciprocity — belief is not loving reciprocation. His central good is relational: delayed disclosure might allow a person to freely own or improve a disposition to love, distinguishing ownership of initially unchosen love from the greater fittingness of voluntarily cultivated love — with the extension from his first disposition type to the other two assumed without argument [@S-T15-009].

<!-- id: c10-p017 -->
His concessions are recorded as carefully as his defense. The sketched reason fails, by his own note, for the willing seeker who recognizes deficient love and strongly desires improvement; a sufficiently fitting seeker escapes his objection, with our ability to identify one disputed; the account is limited to temporary nonbelief, leaves cultural distribution unaddressed, presupposes survival if the delay lasts a whole earthly life, and does not rule out eventual coerced love as a second-best possibility [@S-T15-009]. None of these concessions is proof about the actual population; all of them price the defense.

<!-- id: c10-p018 -->
The epistemic fallback is the defense's last line, and the record splits it. Howard-Snyder's final move challenges the inference from our failure to conceive justifying reasons to their absence — that clause is attribution; the recorded addition is a project inference: the fallback's relation to positive goodness-recognition requires a scope argument, because local uncertainty about divine reasons does not automatically license blanket uncertainty, and blanket uncertainty would cost the theist's positive claims too. This is the same symmetry stated at c10-p007, now located inside the inspected defense.

<!-- id: c10-p019 -->
Schellenberg's actual 1996 response, inspected complete, restructures the dispute rather than merely rebutting it. He disputes the inserted overriding-reason clause and the independent no-reason premise, proposing positive defeasible support for divine intention instead; and he accepts commitment to the absence of overriding reasons and vulnerability to actual counterconsiderations, while denying any requirement to establish that absence by an independent Rowe-style inference [@S-P2T08-001]. The recorded structural point: accepting an entailed proposition differs from independently establishing it, so the target of Howard-Snyder's fallback depends on which argument is being defended — and no full positive case or metaphysical conclusion is established by that distinction alone, with the 1993 book's positive case referenced but not read.

<!-- id: c10-p020 -->
His three substantive responses are recorded with their premise status marked. He contests capacity in cases of certain powerless rejection and invokes manner of disclosure and continued help against likely-rejection cases, with a note denying any inference to actual-world counterparts' acceptance; he argues that freely confirming love and choosing its continuation can occur after belief, with neither timing changing the origin of the initial disposition; and he proposes improving fittingness within belief, distinguishes experienced absence from nonbelief, and disputes the human-lover analogy through four divine-human disanalogies [@S-P2T08-001]. The records hold every one of these as philosophical comparative premises — theological counterpossibilities, not observed psychology or quantified likelihoods.

<!-- id: c10-p021 -->
The recorded adjudication leaves the exchange genuinely open, under a chronology the ledger requires and the article's summary does not supply. The selected exchange leaves positive warrant for universal access and comparative developmental means contested; neither imagined benefits of delay nor the reply establishes actual divine conduct. Howard-Snyder's available internal replies — ownership versus fittingness, improvement versus perfection, prima facie versus conclusive — are defenses already present in his 1996 article, not a later rebuttal to Schellenberg's response; no such later rebuttal was inspected. And the 1996 reply addresses the earlier Schellenberg formulation, while the 2025 précis emphasizes resistance causally relevant to nonbelief: the exchange is real, complete on neither side, and split across three decades of versions. The article's summary survives all of this: the issue is comparative — whether the better-relationship rationale warrants withholding an available relationship rather than improving the person within it — not whether spiritual development is conceivable.

## Internal costs: survival reliance and inscrutability symmetry

<!-- id: c10-p022 -->
The first internal cost is survival reliance, and the record states it as a circularity constraint. A delay lasting an earthly lifetime relies on survival if the relationship is eventually to occur — Howard-Snyder's own recorded limitation — and the audit's synthesis premise makes the general rule explicit: claims of actual survival or intended moral education need evidence apart from their usefulness in a reply. The survival that the hiddenness defense needs cannot be inferred from the defense's need for it; the empirical and continuity chapters of this thesis hold the survival record, and nothing in this chapter's material adds to it. The same rule binds the restoration defense: Adams's integration proposals presuppose continued same-person life and coherent divine intimacy, both recorded as contested and neither supplied by the defense that requires them.

<!-- id: c10-p023 -->
The second internal cost is inscrutability symmetry. If unknown reasons answer every discrepancy between suffering or nonbelief and a good agent's supposed preferences, the recognition of favorable evidence needs equal scrutiny — the recorded form: unrestricted skepticism weakens positive claims of recognizable divine goodness at the same rate it weakens the objections. The record is explicit that this is not a refutation of every appeal to limited human understanding: the recorded reply distinguishes local from blanket skepticism and asks whether the positive and negative inferences rely on the same inaccessible domain. What survives is the article's constraint: a proposal may not use specified intentions to explain life while treating their apparent failures as wholly inscrutable.

<!-- id: c10-p024 -->
The chapter's constructive point is the article's closing sentence, grounded in the audit's design discipline. A benevolent unification must allow its claims about goodness to restrict what counts as an expected world — the same precommitment requirement the meaning chapter recorded for the consciousness-factory comparison, applied to the benevolence attribute: declared ends, declared constraints, outcomes that can weaken the model because the commitments were fixed in advance. A specified benevolent foundation that predicts nothing is not strengthened by the evil and hiddenness records; it discriminates weakly, unable to face the qualitative tests that precisely constrained variants can face until its ends, population, means, constraints and tradeoffs are fixed in advance. And the tests differ by specification: specified developmental and preserving purposes face different conditional tests concerning deprivation, opportunity and same-subject preservation — the recorded reason it is the specification, not the family, that the evidence engages.

<!-- id: c10-p025 -->
What the objections do and do not accomplish is bounded on both sides. They weaken particular providential specifications if their premises hold; they do not undermine every possible conscious ground — Schellenberg's own recorded scope limit, and the article's. Nothing in the record supplies an equal-support verdict between the specified benevolent foundation and its rivals, and nothing licenses the reverse inference that surviving these objections confirms the specification. The recorded sensitivity note also carries: rejecting moral realism would change both the moral argument and some formulations of the evil objections, though internal inconsistency arguments could still run on an opponent's own commitments.
