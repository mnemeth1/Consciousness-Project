The previous chapter priced what a theory buys by taking experience as basic. This chapter examines the two recorded routes by which an experiential ground might be argued to exist and to be an agent: contingency arguments from a principle of sufficient reason to a necessary foundation, and fine-tuning arguments from life-permitting order to a designer's intentions. The question throughout is inherited from the article's fourth section: what such arguments can conditionally establish, where the bridge from a necessary foundation to a mind stays open, and what a discrimination argument must assume before its comparison does any work. The chapter closes with the circularity constraint on designer preferences, the component boundaries, a dependence accounting and an explicit statement of what it cannot conclude.

## Contingency and the weak principle of sufficient reason

<!-- id: c05-p003 -->
The demand the contingency route answers is the article's: a foundational theory must also address why this concrete order exists. The record's historical anchor is Leibniz's 1714 Monadology, inspected in the Bennett English modernization at the relevant sections — the original French was not compared. There Leibniz states a principle of sufficient reason for true or existent facts, distinguishes necessary from contingent truths, and argues that even an infinite sequence of contingencies lacks its ultimate reason internally, concluding to a necessary substance [@S-T10-002]. The record keeps a further step separate: unity, perfection, understanding and will are attributed to the foundation in a distinct move, and calling a foundation necessary establishes none of them.

<!-- id: c05-p004 -->
The record's historical counterweight is Hume's Dialogues, inspected in the Bennett English modernization at its relevant sections with marked editorial additions, and read with speaker discipline: no speaker uniformly expresses Hume's settled view. In Part IX Cleanthes objects to Demea's necessity argument through conceivability, the possibility of a necessary material universe, and the explanation of parts; Philo adds an analogy with hidden necessity [@S-T10-003]. Elsewhere Philo challenges the extrapolation from human artifacts and asks what a mental plan explains, and Cleanthes replies that explaining a cause does not require explaining its cause. These are dialogue positions with contestable premises of their own — the record marks the conceivability premise and the causal-priority conception as open.

<!-- id: c05-p005 -->
The project's reconstruction of the classical route is explicitly conditional. A restricted principle of sufficient reason covering the contingent totality, a noncircular external-explanation requirement and a concrete-ground premise conditionally yield necessary concrete reality — and mentality needs further premises. The record marks each step as substantive, and it keeps the recorded objection and reply in their roles: the objection is that local explanations may exhaust the legitimate demand and that a necessary impersonal reality remains possible; the reply is that local causal links can leave why the totality obtains unanswered and that exemptions from the principle need justification — while the principle's own scope stays a recorded unresolved issue, not a settled symmetric burden. Totality contingency is not inferred merely from contingent parts, modal necessity is not temporal or causal priority, and the alternatives — brute totality, a necessary impersonal ground, rejection of the concrete-ground premise — remain live throughout.

<!-- id: c05-p006 -->
The record's developed modern version is the Gale–Pruss argument, inspected in the full selected text of the 1999 article. Its advertised novelty is modal weakness: instead of asserting that the totality of contingent truths has an explanation, it assumes only that it could have one — a weak principle of sufficient reason over propositions true in any possible world — and combines that with a maximal world-individuating contingent conjunction, bivalence and compossibility to derive an actual explanation [@S-P2T10-001]. The record separates the formal statement from its informal gloss: the formal scope is broader than an actual-world-only reading, and the two must not be interchanged. The article's framing of the attraction is inherited here: explain contingency through something that does not merely add another unexplained contingent member.

<!-- id: c05-p007 -->
Oppy's criticism, inspected in the selected manuscript text of the 2000 reply — the print pagination was not collated — attacks the advertised modesty. Under stated assumptions about explanation — veridicality and dissectivity within normal modal reasoning — the weak principle entails the strong one, by a Fitch-style route that does not depend on the maximal-world construction [@S-P2T10-002]. The record retains his care with the logic: dissectivity is not agglomerativity, and an explanation existing for a conjunction is not the conjunction of two explanations explaining the joint obtaining. The epistemic point rides on the logical one: a person who assented to the weak wording without grasping its consequences may rationally withdraw assent once the entailment is explicit, if they have independent grounds for beliefs that conflict with the conclusion — a condition of the argument, the record notes, not a demonstrated fact about all non-theists.

<!-- id: c05-p008 -->
Two further Oppy distinctions are recorded because the downstream assessment uses them. An explained counterpart or physical duplicate of the universe is not the same object as the actual world-complete fact, so the intuitive possibility of the former does not by itself support the weak principle over the latter. And questions about abstract propositions and the difference between the big conjunctive fact and its contingent restriction are flagged but severable — the record notes Oppy's statement that his main criticism does not depend on resolving them, and the totality ontology remains a separate open issue.

<!-- id: c05-p009 -->
The Gale–Pruss reply of 2002, inspected in the full selected version, concedes the entailment and relocates the defense. Weak and strong principles are reformulated for contingent propositions and the deduction is accepted; the reply argues that understanding a proposition does not require grasping all its consequences, that explanation-seeking practice gives the weak principle epistemic appeal, and — against Davey and Clifton as presented in the reply itself, whose original was not inspected, with the Pruss 2001 cardinality proof behind the construction also uninspected — that a restricted totality fallback is available conditionally [@S-P2T10-003]. The record's sharpest annotation concerns a bridge inside the defense: the reply moves from epistemic possibility to logical possibility as a reasonable step after adequate inquiry, and that modal warrant is defeasible and contested, not a deduction from ignorance.

<!-- id: c05-p010 -->
The article's verdict on this exchange, which this thesis keeps, is that the reply blocks an easy accusation of unintelligibility but does not make the disclosed consequences costless. The question becomes whether commitment to the explanatory principle, understood at full modal strength, remains better justified than its rivals — and that is a comparison of intuitions and practices, not a further theorem. The cosmological argument, on the record as inspected, provides a structured challenge to brute contingency, not an uncontested passage from intelligibility to providence.

## From necessary foundation to mind: the open bridge

<!-- id: c05-p011 -->
Even granting an actual explanation of the totality, the step to an agent is a separate argument with its own recorded pressure points. The Gale–Pruss route treats personal and scientific explanation as exhaustive — a premise their own text qualifies by conceding that unconceived kinds of explanation may exist — excludes scientific explanation because contingent laws and states fall inside the totality and cannot explain themselves, distinguishes necessary existence from contingent creative action to avoid modal collapse, and terminates the regress in a necessary being's libertarian free action, presented as self-explaining with fullness of explanation relative to the questioner's context. The record prices each move: the exhaustiveness premise is not a measured inventory, the free-action exception is a substantive explanatory premise, and further reasons can still be requested.

<!-- id: c05-p012 -->
The recorded impersonal rival enters through the authors themselves. Gale and Pruss report Quinn's objection that a necessary impersonal source could produce the cosmos by blind indeterministic causation — only their published quotation of correspondence was inspected, not any independent Quinn original — and their response allows a generic necessary powerful cause as a fallback while defending personality by appeal to cosmic order and a distinction between free and merely indeterministic action. The project's strongest recorded question sits exactly here: why is libertarian self-explanation sufficient where impersonal indeterminism is not, once explanatory fullness has been made context-sensitive? The record leaves the question open on both sides.

<!-- id: c05-p013 -->
The attribute stage is recorded as candidly incomplete in the original texts. The 1999 argument adds teleological premises for the creator's qualities while explicitly withholding omni-properties and acknowledging that actual benevolence needs theodicy and faith; a footnote records the two authors disagreeing between themselves about whether the possibility of gratuitous evil defeats an essentially perfect necessary God. The 2002 reply preserves the same gap from the other direction: evidence against benevolence does not alone exclude every necessary creator, since an explanation of a horrendous universe could fall short of God. Nothing in the exchange converts a necessary source into a good one.

<!-- id: c05-p014 -->
The record's third voice on the bridge is Lennox, inspected as a full essay in HTML — an apologetic venue whose quoted historical statements and resurrection case the record reads as context, not corroborated events. The Ford analogy makes mechanism and agent compatible: explaining how a process operates does not exclude an agent explanation of why it was arranged [@S-T10-001]. The essay then separately advances positive claims — discoverable intelligibility and sophisticated lawlike order as reasons for a rational creator, and a challenge to naturalism's warrant for reason — and treats natural laws as regularities that alone do not rule out supernatural agency.

<!-- id: c05-p015 -->
The record's assessment discipline separates the two steps exactly. Compatibility removes one alleged incompatibility and by itself favors nothing; comparative support requires independently motivated expectations under which the observed order is more expected on a rational origin than on a defined impersonal alternative. The recorded objections hold both conditions open: agent preferences and the contrast class may be fitted after observing the world, impersonal structure and selection-plus-learning alternatives are not empirically tested here, and no equation of nonintentional origins with unreliable reason is validated. The order appeals also share assumptions with the cosmological argument's attribute stage, so their conjunction adds no independent sample.

<!-- id: c05-p016 -->
The open bridge can now be stated in the record's own terms. A necessary explanatory source does not alone establish a conscious bearer, unity, bodily independence, personal survival, timelessness, preserving purpose or moral authority; each component needs its own bridge, and the absence of a proven rival does not prove its negation. The article's framing sharpens the methodological point: personal explanation cannot be privileged solely because the intended conclusion is personal. What the contingency route delivers, at the reconstruction's conditional maximum, is a necessary concrete ground — and everything this thesis cares about lies on the far side of that result.

## Fine-tuning, stringency and screening

<!-- id: c05-p017 -->
Fine-tuning promises a more discriminatory route: not that something necessary exists, but that the specific life-permitting character of the order favors an agent with discernible preferences. The record carries Collins in two versions, and the difference matters. The abridged teaching excerpt of the 1999 chapter was inspected as a partial original with ellipses, without its referenced probability appendix, and with a printed formula missing a division preserved as a reproduction issue [@S-T10-004]; the 1998 webpage, inspected in sections, appendices and endnotes, contains a probability appendix restricted to an illuminated range with a defended parameterization — but it is not an authenticated complete copy of the 1999 chapter, and its second endnote repeats a malformed likelihood factor whose corrected standard identity is the project's own labeled reconstruction [@S-P2T11-003]. On both versions the argument is explicitly comparative and short of proof: epistemic probability, a life-permitting range less expected under an atheistic single universe, no all-things-considered verdict.

<!-- id: c05-p018 -->
The design side of the comparison is carried by the designer's motivations, and the record prices that dependence before using it. Collins motivates life under theism through divine goodness and the value of intelligent conscious beings; goodness and life-value are premises of the likelihood, and the record forbids counting them again as independently established conclusions. The article's framing states the general constraint this chapter ends on: the dependence is not inherently circular, since a separately constrained conception of agency could predict life, but it becomes circular if life is the sole ground for attributing the preference then invoked to explain life.

<!-- id: c05-p019 -->
The stringency debate sharpens the evidential question to its cleanest recorded form. White, inspected in the published four-page 2011 article, separates what was old knowledge — that life exists, that the universe is hospitable — from what was newly learned: that the conditions for life are stringent [@S-P2T11-001]. From three premises — negative dependence of life on stringency without design, independence given design, and stringency not counting against design beforehand — he derives that newly learned stringency supports design even with existence already known, and he interprets the probabilities evidentially, so the absence of repeated universe creation is not itself a refutation. The record retains his own limits: the third premise is motivated by the absence of a known designer preference, and the conclusion is bounded by other fine-tuning objections. One version boundary matters here: White's stated target is a 2010 Weisberg article that was not inspected; the Weisberg text this record inspects is the 2012 manuscript of the next paragraph.

<!-- id: c05-p020 -->
Weisberg's critique, inspected in the seven-page author manuscript of the 2012 article rather than the journal pagination — the Bradley reply remains mediated through it and uninspected, while White's article is inspected separately as above — targets exactly that third-premise rationale [@S-T10-007]. His judge example shows the inference from no known preference to no negative evidential relevance can fail; his central result derives screening-off from matched divine and blind conditional distributions over life-permitting possibilities, so that once life is known, stringency adds nothing under those matching assumptions, and his Divine Intent assumption also supports an unconditional stringency-negative derivation in his larger argument. The record keeps the result conditional in both directions: it is valid under the matching distributions and is not a measure-free refutation of all design arguments, and Weisberg himself allows initial life evidence to support design while acknowledging uniformity, unbounded-space, parameterization and method-selection problems.

<!-- id: c05-p021 -->
The recorded extension is Dorst and Dorst, inspected as the complete ten-page 2022 Online First PDF — a version with visible formula and bibliography defects that required explicit project reconstruction, including a final inequality sign conflicting with an earlier equality [@S-P2T11-002]. They recast White's premise as indifference over stringency classes rather than life-worlds, obtaining opposite conditional support relations from the two uniformities — their setup also makes laxity disfavor design after conditioning on life exactly where stringency favors it, while the unconditional relation can differ — and they offer a mixture argument: if a possible ideal stringency-class distribution makes the observed stringency more expected under design, and reasonable credence gives that possibility sufficient weight, conditional positive support survives uncertainty and those departures from exact indifference that preserve the likelihood separation. The record's annotations are exacting: the tiny no-design likelihood at the bound is source-assumed from Collins 2003, a distinct source not inspected here and not either of the two Collins versions above; the diagrams are stipulated spaces rather than measured frequencies; and the weighting depends on disputed views of the designer, time and laws.

<!-- id: c05-p022 -->
The recorded counterweight is a supplied manuscript attributed by its archive to McCutcheon — the supplied bytes carry no author byline and no version tag, so the claimed version identity is unauthenticated — which introduces uncertainty over the laxity range parameter itself [@S-P2T11-005]. Its uniform-range-prior example collapses the enormous fixed-range ratio to about sixty-one — a small-interval approximation whose extreme input value is assumed from the exchange, not empirically validated — while explicitly allowing residual positive stringency evidence; its power priors approach a limiting ratio near unity while acknowledging that the exact endpoint prior is improper. The record's further limits on the model are retained: it assumes linear life-world weighting and a particular hyperprior, and its count-versus-proportion and maximum-versus-typical interpretations of the range are not independently established. The record keeps both directions conditional: the critique challenges the scale and robustness of the mixture advantage without invalidating the mixture's sufficient condition, its own near-neutral conclusion is conditional on its added model assumptions, and a capped forward search found no actual reply — absence of discovery, the record notes, is not absence of reply.

<!-- id: c05-p023 -->
Sober supplies the recorded observation-selection critique, and the access history is part of the record: the earlier task inspected only the abstract, notes and references of the publisher preview [@S-T10-006], and the later task read the full published scan, pages 63 to 90. His analysis distinguishes qualitative favoring from numerical strength and grants the fine-tuning inequality for argument; the critique conditions the observation of stable life-compatible constants on earlier observer existence, which is not automatically the same datum as newly learned stringency. The record retains a documented self-revision — he retracts his 2004 firing-squad treatment after Weisberg 2005 while keeping a different judgment for fine-tuning, with both older originals uninspected — and he declines to debunk every design argument; the record notes he does not claim the design probabilities actually equal zero.

<!-- id: c05-p024 -->
What the stringency exchange establishes is conditional model behavior, and the record refuses every stronger reading. Fine-tuning can supply conditional qualitative comparative support without a numerical posterior; sensitivity alone identifies neither a measure nor designer preferences; matched conditional distributions neutralize stringency exactly where their matching assumptions hold and nowhere else; and none of the inspected arguments establishes a physical measure or identifies brute reality with a uniform random sampler. The article's framing of the same point is inherited: an apparently enormous explanatory gain that comes from putting a life-valuing preference on one side and an invented uniform distribution on the other is an artifact of those inputs, since neither unspecified brute reality nor unspecified agency has that distribution by definition.

## Conditional discrimination and its assumptions

<!-- id: c05-p025 -->
The article's conclusion for this material, which this chapter keeps as inherited framing, is conditional discrimination. Observer selection does not trivially erase every relevant difference — Sober's recorded position declines to debunk every design argument — and unavailable numerical probabilities do not imply that all qualitative comparisons are empty. But a useful design argument must identify the evidence being updated on, the alternatives in play, and the assumptions governing their expectations; and a conscious ground earns additional support from design only through a defensible account of what it would be inclined to produce. The circularity constraint gives that requirement teeth: preferences attributed solely from the datum they are invoked to explain confer no support at all.

<!-- id: c05-p026 -->
The component boundaries translate the two routes' limits into the thesis's working vocabulary. The inspected existence and design routes do not by themselves establish universal subjecthood, human brain independence, survival, timelessness, preservation, goodness or moral authority. Life-directed preferences used to support design cannot then independently establish goodness, preservation or moral authority — the circular-credit prohibition is a recorded inference, not a stylistic caution. And the recorded conclusion about the selected versions is that they leave all twelve stronger-conclusion gates withheld; this chapter opens none of them.

<!-- id: c05-p028 -->
What this chapter cannot conclude, it now states. It cannot conclude that the totality of contingent facts has an actual explanation — that conclusion is conditional on a principle whose full modal strength is exactly what the recorded exchange contests. It cannot conclude that any necessary foundation exists, that any foundation is personal, or that fine-tuning confers any all-things-considered support on design: every recorded support claim in this chapter is a conditional likelihood comparison whose measures, preferences and priors remain unestablished. It cannot use the goodness assumed inside the design likelihood as evidence of goodness. All twelve withheld stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld through this chapter, and the assessments that use this material downstream inherit every conditional stated here.
