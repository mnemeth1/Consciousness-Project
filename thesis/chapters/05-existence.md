<!-- id: c05-p001 -->
The previous chapter asked what a theory buys, and what it owes, when it takes experience as basic. This chapter turns to a different question. By what routes might an experiential ground be argued to exist, and to be an agent? The record has two. Contingency arguments run from a principle of sufficient reason to a necessary foundation. Fine-tuning arguments run from life-permitting order to a designer's intentions. The question throughout comes from the article's fourth section: what such arguments can conditionally establish, where the bridge from a necessary foundation to a mind stays open, and what a discrimination argument must assume before its comparison does any work. The chapter closes with the circularity constraint on designer preferences, the component boundaries, a dependence accounting and a plain statement of what it cannot conclude.

<!-- id: c05-p002 -->
The caution that governed the previous chapter governs this one too, with one addition. Every input is an inspected philosophical text or a reviewed project analysis. Every "argues," "derives" and "concedes" is a documented attribution to an identified version, and an attribution never establishes that a premise is true. The addition concerns versions. The historical texts are English modernizations. One member of an exchange is known only through its opponents' quotation of it. One manuscript's version identity is unauthenticated. And the fine-tuning literature is recorded as a single shared dependency group. Nothing in this chapter multiplies into independent lines of support, and the dependence structure is set out in detail at the end.

## Contingency and the weak principle of sufficient reason

<!-- id: c05-p003 -->
The demand the contingency route answers is the article's own: a foundational theory must also say why this concrete order exists at all. The record's historical anchor is Leibniz's 1714 Monadology, inspected in the Bennett English modernization at the relevant sections. The original French was not compared. There Leibniz states a principle of sufficient reason for true or existent facts, and he distinguishes necessary truths from contingent ones. He then argues that even an infinite sequence of contingencies does not contain its own ultimate reason, and he concludes to a necessary substance [@S-T10-002]. The record keeps a further step separate. Unity, perfection, understanding and will are attributed to the foundation in a distinct move, and calling a foundation necessary establishes none of them.

<!-- id: c05-p004 -->
The record's historical counterweight is Hume's Dialogues, inspected in the Bennett English modernization at its relevant sections, with the editorial additions marked. It is read with speaker discipline, because no speaker uniformly expresses Hume's settled view. In Part IX Cleanthes objects to Demea's necessity argument through conceivability, through the possibility of a necessary material universe, and through the explanation of the parts. Philo adds an analogy with hidden necessity [@S-T10-003]. Elsewhere Philo challenges the extrapolation from human artifacts and asks what a mental plan explains, and Cleanthes replies that explaining a cause does not require explaining its cause. These are positions in a dialogue, and they have contestable premises of their own. The record marks the conceivability premise and the conception of causal priority as open.

<!-- id: c05-p005 -->
The project's reconstruction of the classical route is explicitly conditional. Its premises are a restricted principle of sufficient reason covering the contingent totality, a noncircular requirement of external explanation, and a concrete-ground premise. Together they conditionally yield a necessary concrete reality. Mentality needs further premises. The record marks each step as substantive, and it keeps the recorded objection and the recorded reply in their roles. The objection is that local explanations may exhaust the legitimate demand, and that a necessary impersonal reality remains possible. The reply is that local causal links can leave unanswered why the totality obtains, and that exemptions from the principle need justification. Meanwhile the scope of the principle itself stays a recorded unresolved issue, not a settled symmetric burden. Throughout, the contingency of the totality is not inferred merely from the contingency of its parts, and modal necessity is not temporal or causal priority. The alternatives also remain live throughout: a brute totality, a necessary impersonal ground, and rejection of the concrete-ground premise.

<!-- id: c05-p006 -->
The developed modern version in the record is the Gale–Pruss argument, inspected in the full selected text of the 1999 article. Its advertised novelty is modal weakness. Instead of asserting that the totality of contingent truths has an explanation, it assumes only that it could have one. That is a weak principle of sufficient reason, ranging over propositions true in any possible world. The argument combines that principle with a maximal world-individuating contingent conjunction, bivalence and compossibility, and derives an actual explanation [@S-P2T10-001]. The record separates the formal statement from its informal gloss. The formal scope is broader than a reading confined to the actual world, and the two must not be interchanged. The attraction, as the article frames it and this chapter keeps it, is this: explain contingency through something that does not merely add another unexplained contingent member.

<!-- id: c05-p007 -->
Oppy's criticism attacks the advertised modesty. It was inspected in the selected manuscript text of the 2000 reply; the print pagination was not collated. Under stated assumptions about explanation, namely veridicality and dissectivity within normal modal reasoning, the weak principle entails the strong one. The route is Fitch-style, and it does not depend on the maximal-world construction [@S-P2T10-002]. The record retains his care with the logic. Dissectivity is not agglomerativity, and an explanation existing for a conjunction is not the conjunction of two explanations explaining the joint obtaining. The epistemic point rides on the logical one. A person who assented to the weak wording without grasping its consequences may rationally withdraw that assent once the entailment is made explicit, provided they have independent grounds for beliefs that conflict with the conclusion. The record notes that this proviso is a condition of the argument, not a demonstrated fact about all non-theists.

<!-- id: c05-p008 -->
Two further distinctions of Oppy's are recorded, because the downstream assessment uses them. First, an explained counterpart or physical duplicate of the universe is not the same object as the actual world-complete fact. So the intuitive possibility of the former does not by itself support the weak principle over the latter. Second, questions about abstract propositions, and about the difference between the big conjunctive fact and its contingent restriction, are flagged but severable. The record notes Oppy's own statement that his main criticism does not depend on resolving them, and the ontology of the totality remains a separate open issue.

<!-- id: c05-p009 -->
The Gale–Pruss reply of 2002, inspected in the full selected version, concedes the entailment and moves the defense elsewhere. The weak and strong principles are reformulated for contingent propositions, and the deduction is accepted. The reply then argues as follows. Understanding a proposition does not require grasping all of its consequences. The practice of seeking explanations gives the weak principle epistemic appeal. And a restricted totality fallback is available, conditionally, against Davey and Clifton [@S-P2T10-003]. Version limits attach to that last point. Davey and Clifton are known here only as the reply itself presents them; their original was not inspected. The Pruss 2001 cardinality proof that stands behind the construction was also uninspected. Modern sufficient-reason treatments beyond this exchange were not inspected either. The record's sharpest annotation concerns a bridge inside the defense. The reply moves from epistemic possibility to logical possibility, treating that as a reasonable step after adequate inquiry. That modal warrant is defeasible and contested. It is not a deduction from ignorance.

<!-- id: c05-p010 -->
The article's verdict on this exchange, which this thesis keeps, is that the reply blocks an easy accusation of unintelligibility but does not make the disclosed consequences costless. The question becomes whether commitment to the explanatory principle, understood at full modal strength, remains better justified than its rivals. That is a comparison of intuitions and practices, not a further theorem. In the record as inspected, the cosmological argument provides a structured challenge to brute contingency. It does not provide an uncontested passage from intelligibility to providence.

## From necessary foundation to mind: the open bridge

<!-- id: c05-p011 -->
Suppose we grant an actual explanation of the totality. The step to an agent is still a separate argument, with pressure points of its own on the record. The Gale–Pruss route treats personal and scientific explanation as exhaustive, a premise their own text qualifies by conceding that unconceived kinds of explanation may exist. It excludes scientific explanation, because contingent laws and states fall inside the totality and cannot explain themselves. It distinguishes necessary existence from contingent creative action, to avoid modal collapse. And it ends the regress in a necessary being's libertarian free action, presented as self-explaining, with fullness of explanation relative to the questioner's context. The record sets a cost against each move. The exhaustiveness premise is not a measured inventory. The free-action exception is a substantive explanatory premise. And further reasons can still be requested.

<!-- id: c05-p012 -->
The recorded impersonal rival enters through the authors themselves. Gale and Pruss report Quinn's objection that a necessary impersonal source could produce the cosmos by blind indeterministic causation. Only their published quotation of his correspondence was inspected; no independent Quinn original was. Their response allows a generic necessary powerful cause as a fallback, while defending personality by appeal to cosmic order and to a distinction between free action and merely indeterministic action. The project's strongest recorded question sits right here. Why is libertarian self-explanation sufficient where impersonal indeterminism is not, once explanatory fullness has been made relative to context? The record leaves that question open on both sides.

<!-- id: c05-p013 -->
The attribute stage is recorded as candidly incomplete in the original texts. The 1999 argument adds teleological premises for the creator's qualities, but it explicitly withholds the omni-properties, and it acknowledges that actual benevolence needs theodicy and faith. A footnote records the two authors disagreeing between themselves about whether the possibility of gratuitous evil defeats an essentially perfect necessary God. The 2002 reply preserves the same gap from the other direction. Evidence against benevolence does not by itself exclude every necessary creator, since an explanation of a horrendous universe could fall short of God. Nothing in the exchange turns a necessary source into a good one.

<!-- id: c05-p014 -->
The third voice on the bridge in the record is Lennox, inspected as a full essay in HTML. The venue is apologetic, and the record reads the essay's quoted historical statements and its resurrection case as context, not as corroborated events. The Ford analogy makes mechanism and agent compatible: explaining how a process operates does not exclude an agent explanation of why it was arranged [@S-T10-001]. The essay then advances positive claims of its own, separately. It offers discoverable intelligibility and sophisticated lawlike order as reasons for a rational creator, and it challenges naturalism's warrant for reason. It also treats natural laws as regularities that do not, on their own, rule out supernatural agency. Direct critics of the essay were not inspected.

<!-- id: c05-p015 -->
The record's assessment keeps the two steps strictly apart. Compatibility removes one alleged incompatibility, and by itself it favors nothing. Comparative support is a different matter. It requires independently motivated expectations under which the observed order is more expected on a rational origin than on a defined impersonal alternative. The recorded objections hold both conditions open. Agent preferences and the contrast class may be fitted after the world has been observed. Impersonal structure and selection-plus-learning alternatives are not empirically tested here. And no equation of nonintentional origins with unreliable reason is validated. The order appeals also share assumptions with the attribute stage of the cosmological argument, so putting the two together adds no independent sample.

<!-- id: c05-p016 -->
The open bridge can now be stated in the record's own terms. A necessary explanatory source does not by itself establish a conscious bearer, unity, bodily independence, personal survival, timelessness, preserving purpose or moral authority. Each component needs its own bridge, and the absence of a proven rival does not prove its negation. The article sharpens the point about method: personal explanation cannot be privileged solely because the intended conclusion is personal. What the contingency route delivers, at the reconstruction's conditional maximum, is a necessary concrete ground. Everything this thesis cares about lies on the far side of that result.

## Fine-tuning, stringency and screening

<!-- id: c05-p017 -->
Fine-tuning promises a more discriminating route. Its claim is not that something necessary exists, but that the specific life-permitting character of the order favors an agent with discernible preferences. The record carries Collins in two versions, and the difference between them matters. The abridged teaching excerpt of the 1999 chapter was inspected as a partial original with ellipses. It lacks the probability appendix it refers to, and a printed formula in it is missing a division, which the record preserves as a reproduction issue [@S-T10-004]. The 1998 webpage was inspected in its sections, appendices and endnotes, and it does contain a probability appendix, restricted to an illuminated range with a defended parameterization. But it is not an authenticated complete copy of the 1999 chapter, and its second endnote repeats a malformed likelihood factor. The corrected standard identity of that factor is the project's own labeled reconstruction [@S-P2T11-003]. On both versions the argument is explicitly comparative and short of proof. It deals in epistemic probability. It claims that a life-permitting range is less expected under an atheistic single universe. And it offers no all-things-considered verdict.

<!-- id: c05-p018 -->
The design side of the comparison is carried by the designer's motivations, and the record states the cost of that dependence before it uses it. Collins motivates life under theism through divine goodness and the value of intelligent conscious beings. Goodness and the value of life are therefore premises of the likelihood, and the record forbids counting them a second time as independently established conclusions. The article states the general constraint this chapter ends on. The dependence is not inherently circular, since a separately constrained conception of agency could predict life. But it becomes circular if life is the sole ground for attributing the very preference that is then invoked to explain life.

<!-- id: c05-p019 -->
The stringency debate sharpens the evidential question to its cleanest recorded form. White, inspected in the published four-page 2011 article, separates old knowledge from new. The old knowledge is that life exists and that the universe is hospitable. The new knowledge is that the conditions for life are stringent [@S-P2T11-001]. He argues from three premises: that without design, life depends negatively on stringency; that given design, the two are independent; and that stringency did not count against design beforehand. From these he derives that newly learned stringency supports design even when existence is already known. He interprets the probabilities evidentially, so the absence of repeated universe creation is not by itself a refutation. He states limits of his own, and the record keeps them. The third premise is motivated by the absence of a known designer preference, and the conclusion is bounded by other fine-tuning objections. One version boundary matters here. White's stated target is a 2010 Weisberg article that was not inspected. The Weisberg text this record does inspect is the 2012 manuscript discussed in the next paragraph.

<!-- id: c05-p020 -->
Weisberg's critique targets that rationale for the third premise [@S-T10-007]. It was inspected in the seven-page author manuscript of the 2012 article rather than in the journal pagination. The Bradley reply is known only through that manuscript and remains uninspected, while White's article is inspected separately, as above. His judge example shows that the inference from no known preference to no negative evidential relevance can fail. His central result derives screening-off from matched divine and blind conditional distributions over life-permitting possibilities. Once life is known, stringency adds nothing, under those matching assumptions. His Divine Intent assumption also supports an unconditional stringency-negative derivation in his larger argument. The record keeps the result conditional in both directions. It is valid under the matching distributions, and it is not a measure-free refutation of all design arguments. Weisberg himself allows the initial evidence of life to support design, while acknowledging problems of uniformity, unbounded space, parameterization and method selection.

<!-- id: c05-p021 -->
The recorded extension is Dorst and Dorst, inspected as the complete ten-page 2022 Online First PDF [@S-P2T11-002]. That version has visible defects in its formulas and bibliography, which required explicit project reconstruction; one is a final inequality sign that conflicts with an earlier equality. They recast White's premise as indifference over stringency classes rather than over life-worlds, and the two uniformities then yield opposite conditional support relations. Their setup also makes laxity disfavor design after conditioning on life, in just the place where stringency favors it, while the unconditional relation can differ. They then offer a mixture argument. If some possible ideal distribution over stringency classes makes the observed stringency more expected under design, and reasonable credence gives that possibility sufficient weight, then conditional positive support survives uncertainty. It also survives those departures from exact indifference that preserve the likelihood separation. The record's annotations are strict. The tiny no-design likelihood at the bound is assumed from a source, Collins 2003, which is a distinct source not inspected here and not either of the two Collins versions above. The diagrams are stipulated spaces rather than measured frequencies. And the weighting depends on disputed views of the designer, of time and of laws.

<!-- id: c05-p022 -->
The recorded counterweight is a supplied manuscript that its archive attributes to McCutcheon [@S-P2T11-005]. The supplied bytes carry no author byline and no version tag, so the claimed version identity is unauthenticated. The manuscript introduces uncertainty over the laxity range parameter itself. Its uniform-range-prior example collapses the enormous fixed-range ratio to about sixty-one. That figure is a small-interval approximation, and its extreme input value is assumed from the exchange, not empirically validated. The example still explicitly allows residual positive stringency evidence. Its power priors approach a limiting ratio near unity, while acknowledging that the exact endpoint prior is improper. Further limits on the model are also recorded. It assumes linear life-world weighting and a particular hyperprior, and its count-versus-proportion and maximum-versus-typical interpretations of the range are not independently established. The record keeps both directions conditional. The critique challenges the scale and robustness of the mixture advantage without invalidating the mixture's sufficient condition. Its own near-neutral conclusion is conditional on the model assumptions it adds. And a capped forward search found no actual reply; absence of discovery, the record notes, is not absence of reply.

<!-- id: c05-p023 -->
Sober supplies the recorded observation-selection critique, and the history of access to it is part of the record. The earlier task inspected only the abstract, notes and references of the publisher preview [@S-T10-006]. The later task read the full published scan, pages 63 to 90. His analysis distinguishes qualitative favoring from numerical strength, and it grants the fine-tuning inequality for the sake of argument. The critique conditions the observation of stable life-compatible constants on the earlier existence of observers, and that is not automatically the same datum as newly learned stringency. A self-revision is also documented in the record. He retracts his 2004 firing-squad treatment after Weisberg 2005, while keeping a different judgment for fine-tuning; both of the older originals are uninspected. He also declines to debunk every design argument, and the record notes that he does not claim the design probabilities actually equal zero.

<!-- id: c05-p024 -->
What does the stringency exchange establish? Conditional model behavior, and nothing stronger; the record refuses every stronger reading. Fine-tuning can supply conditional qualitative comparative support without a numerical posterior. Sensitivity alone identifies neither a measure nor a designer's preferences. Matched conditional distributions neutralize stringency where their matching assumptions hold, and nowhere else. And none of the inspected arguments establishes a physical measure or identifies brute reality with a uniform random sampler. The article makes the same point in a way this chapter keeps. Put a life-valuing preference on one side and an invented uniform distribution on the other, and an apparently enormous explanatory gain appears. That gain is an artifact of those inputs, since neither unspecified brute reality nor unspecified agency has that distribution by definition.

## Conditional discrimination and its assumptions

<!-- id: c05-p025 -->
The article's conclusion for this material, which this chapter keeps as its framing, is conditional discrimination. Observer selection does not trivially erase every relevant difference, and the preceding paragraphs have said which differences it leaves standing. And the unavailability of numerical probabilities does not mean that every qualitative comparison is empty. But a useful design argument must identify the evidence being updated on, the alternatives in play, and the assumptions that govern their expectations. A conscious ground earns additional support from design only through a defensible account of what it would be inclined to produce. The circularity constraint gives that requirement teeth. Preferences attributed solely from the datum they are invoked to explain confer no support at all.

<!-- id: c05-p026 -->
The component boundaries translate the limits of the two routes into the thesis's working vocabulary. The inspected existence and design routes do not by themselves establish universal subjecthood, human brain independence, survival, timelessness, preservation, goodness or moral authority. Life-directed preferences that are used to support design cannot then independently establish goodness, preservation or moral authority. That prohibition on circular credit is a recorded inference, not a stylistic caution. And the recorded conclusion about the selected versions is that they leave all twelve stronger-conclusion gates withheld. This chapter opens none of them.

<!-- id: c05-p028 -->
Let me be clear about what this chapter has not shown. It cannot conclude that the totality of contingent facts has an actual explanation. That conclusion is conditional on a principle whose full modal strength is the very thing the recorded exchange contests. It cannot conclude that any necessary foundation exists, that any foundation is personal, or that fine-tuning confers any all-things-considered support on design. The fine-tuning support claims in this chapter are conditional likelihood comparisons whose measures, preferences and priors remain unestablished. The sufficient-reason and intelligibility routes are different in kind: one is a conditional deduction from a restricted principle and the other a conditional abduction, each still contested, and neither turns on a measure or a prior. It cannot use the goodness assumed inside the design likelihood as evidence of goodness. All twelve withheld stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld through this chapter, and every assessment that uses this material downstream inherits every conditional stated here.
