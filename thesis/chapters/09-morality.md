<!-- id: c09-p001 -->
The previous chapter separated finite meaning from valuable endurance and left the value of a conscious foundation conditional. This chapter takes up the harder normative question the article's seventh section closes with: whether anything in a consciousness-first metaphysics — or in its rivals — supplies moral authority, the relation under which one agent's interests give another agent binding reasons. It expands the second half of article section 7 through three packets: the moral-grounding audit's Craig and Railton attributions with the project's normative-bridge inferences; the Phase 2 inspection of the Korsgaard–Nagel exchange on constructivism and the scope of reasons; and the Phase 2 inspection of the Enoch–Lenman exchange on robust realism and indispensability. Component boundary H-A binds throughout — consciousness, unity, creation, power and endurance do not automatically supply binding reasons — as the project's declared boundary, which the chapter documents the inspected records leaving unmet, not as a theorem about the world. The evidential challenges to a good foundation — suffering and hiddenness — are the next chapter's subject; this chapter concerns where obligation itself could come from.

<!-- id: c09-p002 -->

The Korsgaard and Nagel texts were inspected as selected original chapters in a downloaded teaching copy of the 1996 Cambridge volume (fourteenth printing 2010), with marginal teaching annotations excluded as non-author text and no whole-book inspection claimed. The Enoch book is a partial inspection: chapter 1 from a supplied preview, chapter 3's opening and concluding pages, chapter 4 complete and section 5.1 complete from user-enabled page images — with pages 53 to 83 of chapter 3, the developed indispensability case, missing. The Lenman article is a complete published PDF; Enoch's 2014 reply was inspected in its Lenman-relevant sections with the Manne and Sobel branches unread. Every substantive position below is an attribution whose premise truth remains contested; nothing in this chapter is an established moral or metaphysical truth, and no fundamental-consciousness component receives support.

## From explanation of agents to obligations between them

<!-- id: c09-p003 -->
The article's framing sets the burden exactly. An account of why beings exist is not yet an account of what they owe one another; even shared consciousness would not settle whether another subject's interest supplies an overriding reason for me. The question is not whether a metaphysics can produce agents, or even valuable agents, but whether it can explain the normative relation between them — and the article's comparison finds each of its three stopping points, constructivism, realism and conscious authority, owing something at precisely this point.

<!-- id: c09-p004 -->
The audit's strongest integrated consciousness-grounding case is recorded as a chain of separately contestable bridges. Given subjects to whom conditions can be good or bad, the case must further assume that reasons to protect subjects are objective, and must propose one necessarily good personal foundation whose relation to agents grounds binding demands; its bridge principles require that valence has normative significance, that relevant others count even against my aims, that the foundation is the relevant good whose relation supplies legitimate authority, and that commands are identifiable and consistent with that nature. The recorded conclusion is doubly qualified: the full account could unify welfare, reasons, obligations and accountability if every bridge is warranted — and consciousness alone proves none of the later bridges. The recorded objection is that calling a being good or creative may presuppose the normativity being explained; the recorded reply is that grounding need not be definition or temporal production, an identity or constitution account that still owes warrant for the identity and the relation.

<!-- id: c09-p005 -->
The divine-command attributions are held at source with their self-limits. Craig grounds value in God's nature and obligation in commands from a qualified authority, with reasons for commands not themselves constituting obligations; and he permits contingent commands consistent with essential goodness, correcting his earlier necessary-command formulation [@S-T15-001]. The inspected text is a 2022 author-hosted podcast transcript; the authors Craig quotes or discusses there are not independently inspected, the audio alignment is unverified, and the records mark both claims as attribution only — not established moral or metaphysical truths. The inspected transcript contains no independent proof of the authority relation itself.

<!-- id: c09-p006 -->
The project's bridge inferences state what the package still owes, and they are the chapter's recurring measure. Creator status, causal dependence and superior power do not entail moral authority without a normative relation; necessary goodness and reliable command identification require their own warrant. Consciousness can supply subjects of benefit and harm, but identifying valenced experience as reason-giving and extending concern across subjects require explicit normative bridges. Shared subjecthood could align prudential incentives without establishing impartial obligations, permissible tradeoffs or protection of distinct persons; and personal endurance may enable eventual accountability and restoration while neither duration nor sanctions explains why an act is wrong. All four are project-authored conditional analyses, marked as such.

## Constructivism and the reflective bridge

<!-- id: c09-p007 -->
Korsgaard's proposal, inspected in the original chapters, is the article's constructivist bridge: an agent must take some consideration as a reason, and practical identity helps determine which considerations can be endorsed. She grounds practical authority in reflective agency without treating reflective self-conception as proof of a metaphysical self; links reasons and obligations to practical identity while distinguishing value from right action and authoritative requirements; argues that valuing anything requires valuing humanity as the source of contingent identities; and uses the shareability of reasons to defend obligations to others [@S-P2T06-001]. The recorded argument seeks to connect commitment to one's own humanity with recognition of humanity in others — rather than deriving obligation from sheer power or causal origin, which is exactly the derivation the previous section found missing.

<!-- id: c09-p008 -->
Korsgaard's own limits are recorded, and they matter because the exchange turns on them. She distinguishes formal universalization from a moral law ranging over all rational beings — the first argument does not by itself establish the interpersonal domain; the humanity argument's interpersonal extension requires an additional argument; she extends moral standing to conscious animals without making them reflective agents, on illustrations that are not independently verified empirical evidence in this packet; she denies that bare reflective endorsement makes every action right — psychological explanation alone is not her criterion of justification; and she permits conflicts between obligations while expressly not disproving complete practical normative skepticism [@S-P2T06-001]. A theory this candid about its remaining steps supplies the map of what any successor — including a consciousness-based one — would have to complete.

<!-- id: c09-p009 -->
Nagel's inspected objection reverses the direction of explanation. He disputes that free self-determination must have law-like form merely because it is causation, offering an external reflective standpoint as an alternative explanation of generality; and he argues that reasons ground practical identity rather than derive from it, with a reflective egoist able to understand a public demand while disputing its force — public intelligibility is not obviously impartial justification [@S-P2T06-002]. The record holds his realism as a substantive evaluative rival, not a proof by rejection of Korsgaard, and its own warrant is equally contested.

<!-- id: c09-p010 -->
Korsgaard's actual reply, inspected in the same volume, is preserved with its explicit scope. She argues that general commitments constitute willing — distinguishing genuine choice from a succession of impulses — and expressly limits this new argument to formal universality, adding at the recorded page no new defense of humanity-wide scope or publicity; she distinguishes an action's incentive from its endorsement, denying that identity-based motivation means seeking a reward; and she locates justificatory work in endorsement, answering the attributed Mafioso objection with the claim that fuller reflection supplies a deeper moral demand — while retaining a sense of nonmoral obligation under incomplete reflection, and with Cohen's original chapter uninspected [@S-P2T06-003]. The recorded adjudication: the reply answers a formal-agency objection without independently settling every bridge from agency to impartial moral authority.

<!-- id: c09-p011 -->
What the exchange contributes to this project is a narrowed gap, not a winner. The Phase 2 packet's closing inference is recorded twice over: the selected reply leaves the direction-of-justification dispute open, with Nagel's alternative — evaluative reasons independently justifying self-conceptions — still live; and the developed rational-grounding proposal narrows the first version's source gap while establishing neither a fundamental conscious foundation nor its moral authority. No new evidence of a universal subject, survival, timelessness, preservation, cosmic intention or foundational goodness enters through this exchange.

## Robust realism and indispensability

<!-- id: c09-p012 -->
Enoch's robust realism, inspected in the book's first chapter, is the article's second stopping point: objective, irreducibly normative truth, where attitudes may matter normatively without constitutively determining fundamental normative truths. Two recorded separations discipline everything that follows: realist ontology is separated from epistemic warrant, so robust realism can coexist with normative skepticism; and Enoch himself denies that his objectivity and metanormative-realism arguments, even combined, entail robust metaethical realism [@S-P2T07-001]. The position enters this chapter as a characterization, not a proof.

<!-- id: c09-p013 -->
The indispensability route carries a declared inspection gap at its center. Enoch's introduction presents deliberative indispensability in parity with explanatory indispensability as supporting belief in irreducible normative facts; the chapter that develops the argument was inspected only at its opening (which targets general normative realism, not moral realism) and its concluding page (which makes the claimed reason defeasible and acknowledges that full support requires rejecting alternatives not completely addressed there) — pages 53 to 83, including the epistemological derivation, remain uninspected [@S-P2T07-001]. The recorded argument form is: deliberation is rationally non-optional; normative truths and belief in them are instrumentally indispensable to that project; indispensability to an appropriate non-optional project can justify ontological commitment. Each step is contested in the exchange reported below.

<!-- id: c09-p014 -->
Lenman's inspected countercase asks whether the work can be done thinner. His schmeliberation uses reflectively scrutinized contingent concerns as success standards and claims deliberation-like phenomenology without robust truth; the challenge is that non-optionality and robust-truth-requiring thickness cannot be jointly satisfied — only the thinner practice is indispensable [@S-P2T07-002]. The record notes his expressivism differs from descriptive naturalism, and separately that he distinguishes psychological truth conditions from consciousness constitutively determining normative truth conditions — a conditional normative independence that is not evidence about fundamental consciousness. No population finding about how people deliberate is established by the phenomenological claims on either side.

<!-- id: c09-p015 -->
Enoch's inspected reply defends a remaining justificatory claim without converting it into truth. Similarity to choosing does not establish identity with deliberation; success standards may lack deserved allegiance; deliberative phenomenology and person-relative justification can vary — with the explicit denial that indispensability makes normative truths true; and his instrumental indispensability includes sufficiently diminishing reason to engage, revisable under contrary evidence [@S-P2T07-003]. The reply defends the claimed reason; it is not a substitute for the uninspected developed case at pages 53 to 83. The record also preserves a concession the article does not reach: Enoch acknowledges needing, but not supplying, a criterion distinguishing legitimate Moorean reasoning from bootstrapping, with Joyce's original uninspected. The recorded upshot is symmetric: Lenman's alternative does not by itself remove Enoch's claimed reason to believe, and the deserved-allegiance demand may restate rather than resolve the disputed richness.

<!-- id: c09-p016 -->
The moral bridge in Enoch's fourth chapter, inspected complete, is expressly conditional and expressly weak. Assuming moral objectivity and justified robust general normative realism, familiar motivations for withholding specifically moral realism are argued to weaken — the metaphysical and epistemic objections generalize, and moral facts are proposed to add no new ontological kind — while Enoch explicitly denies entailment and allows unconsidered motivations to remain [@S-P2T07-001]. The record's limits travel with the bridge: the cumulative-cost counterexample is acknowledged in the text, the detailed replies to metaphysics, knowledge, disagreement and motivation are deferred to chapters 6 through 9, which remain unread, and a local nonmoral expressivism is not ruled out. His categorical-reasons treatment distinguishes a rule's applicability despite indifference from an actual reason applying regardless of motivation, and concedes in a recorded note that a successful Schroeder-style desire-grounded account could meet one sense of categorical. His defended moral rationalism is the weak claim that morality's connection to reasons is not wholly contingent: no every-agent compliance guarantee, no overriding priority, exceptional cases allowed — he does not require morality itself to source the reasons, and the record notes this weak conclusion differs from, but does not by itself refute, Railton's separately acknowledged limitation. The antecedent chapters function as assumptions here; reading chapter 4 establishes neither.

<!-- id: c09-p017 -->
The reduction question is left open by the book's own inspected section. Enoch's section 5.1 distinguishes conceptual from metaphysical irreducibility and identity from reduction, grounding, constitution and type/token claims; admits the natural/normative framing is imprecise and his argument inconclusive against all naturalisms; acknowledges that familiar reduction objections are inconclusive and that his response to Schroeder does not prove irreducibility, locating an impasse over an initial normative-force intuition; and remains open to abandoning that intuition for a sufficiently strong specific reduction, crediting naturalism's parsimony and flagging token-level reduction as worth developing [@S-P2T07-001]. The cited critics' originals — Schroeder, the Moral Twin Earth literature, Smith, Fine, Parfit — remain uninspected; only Enoch's treatment is attributed. Robust realism thus enters the comparison with its own foundational debt priced by its own defender.

<!-- id: c09-p018 -->
Railton's naturalistic route, inspected in the original article, is a recorded secular alternative — the one that takes the reduction option seriously, with other secular families retained in the record. His non-moral good concerns objective interests — natural dispositional facts ground the idealized informed preference, not conversely; his substantive moral criterion is impartial consideration of affected interests under full information, a criterion he himself labels controversial; and he separates the applicability of obligation from categorical reasons for every rational agent, which his account does not guarantee [@S-T15-002]. His reforming naturalistic definitions are recorded as theoretical proposals whose empirical explanatory success remains a burden. The audit's conclusion holds: a physicalist ontology has a substantive route to moral realism; the route remains contested and does not settle every categorical-reason demand — with a fully informed egoist still able to reject impartiality, and the recorded reply separating truth and applicability from the agent's motivation.

## Conscious authority: the unmet relation

<!-- id: c09-p019 -->
The article's diagnosis, which this chapter has now documented exchange by exchange, is that a theory beginning with consciousness does not dissolve the normative question: explaining how a demand arises is different from explaining why it merits compliance. Korsgaard explains how reflective agents come to treat considerations as reasons; Nagel objects that this gets the direction of justification backwards; Enoch explains why deliberators may be entitled to believe in robust reasons; Lenman offers the same practice without the ontology; Railton builds normative properties from natural ones at a price he names. A common-consciousness proposal inherits the same fork: it can explain the genesis of demands through shared subjecthood, or assert their objective force, but the inspected records contain no derivation of the second from the first.

<!-- id: c09-p020 -->
The unmet relation can be stated precisely from the recorded bridges. What a conscious foundation would owe is not more consciousness but a normative relation: warrant that the foundation is good in the reason-giving sense rather than merely powerful, creative or enduring; warrant that its relation to agents legitimates demands rather than merely enabling sanctions; and identifiability of what it requires. The recorded reply on behalf of the strongest case — that grounding can be identity or constitution rather than definition or temporal production — is preserved as a live option that still owes warrant for the identity and the relation. This is component boundary H-A applied, not a refutation: the records leave open that such warrant could be supplied; they document that nothing inspected supplies it.

<!-- id: c09-p021 -->
The prudential shortcut is recorded as insufficient. If all subjects were one subject, harming another might become imprudent — but shared subjecthood aligning incentives is not impartial obligation, and the recorded analysis is explicit that unity does not by itself establish permissible tradeoffs or the protection of distinct persons; sanctions and endurance likewise enable accountability without explaining wrongness. The recorded objection to the strongest integrated case makes the same point from inside it: unity and sanctions can yield only self-interest, so an account on which wrongdoing is ultimately self-harm has moved from obligation to prudence rather than deriving one from the other. The normative gap survives the metaphysical merger.

<!-- id: c09-p022 -->
The dependence rule for normative premises binds this chapter to its neighbors. The audit's synthesis argument records that the value judgments supporting the moral-grounding proposal also underwrite the objections from evil, so the same moral premise must not be counted as independent evidence each time it recurs, and explanatory stopping-point and epistemic-access standards must be applied symmetrically to divine and secular accounts. That mapping assigns burdens to the goodness and authority components with conditional effects on intention, preservation and survival, and identifies neither a numerical posterior nor a winning full metaphysics. The evil and hiddenness exchanges that test the goodness component are the next chapter's material; what carries forward from here is the symmetry rule itself, binding this chapter and chapter 10 where the same moral premises recur. The endurance claims this chapter shares with chapter 8 rest on their own recorded supporting claims, a separate relation.

## Comparative stopping points

<!-- id: c09-p023 -->
The article's comparative conclusion stands as recorded: the normative comparison remains substantive without waiting for a verdict on fundamental ontology. Constructivism owes a bridge from agency to the scope and force of reasons — Korsgaard's own explicit remainder. Realism owes warrant for its normative starting point — Enoch's own priced impasse. Conscious authority owes a relation between its nature and legitimate claims on agents — the audit's unmet bridges. None gains exemption by naming its stopping point, and the article's further point holds in the records: a successful metaphysics of consciousness could illuminate the bearers and objects of value while still needing to explain why these reasons apply and when they override others.

<!-- id: c09-p024 -->
What the comparison does not license is as important as what it does. No account is declared the winner: Nagel's realism is not vindicated by Korsgaard's concessions, Korsgaard's constructivism is not refuted by Nagel's objection, Enoch's realism survives Lenman's countercase only as an undefeated claim to justified belief, and Railton's naturalism remains a contested proposal. The audit's earlier matrix reconstruction of constructivism and non-natural realism — recorded with no decisive original defender inspected — is not withdrawn; the Phase 2 original inspections now supply this chapter's constructivist and robust-realist attributions and narrow that source gap, while the corresponding gap register entry remains partial and open. Each family's stopping point remains a commitment, and the choice among them is not settled by anything in the evidence ledger.

What this chapter cannot conclude, it states. No component of the fundamental-consciousness package — fundamentality, universal subjecthood, brain independence, survival beyond irreversible death, timelessness, preservation, intention, goodness, moral authority — receives support from this chapter, and all twelve withheld stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld through it.
