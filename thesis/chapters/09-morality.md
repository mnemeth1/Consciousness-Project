The previous chapter separated finite meaning from valuable endurance, and it left the value of a conscious foundation conditional. This chapter takes up a harder question, the normative one with which the article's seventh section closes. Does anything in a consciousness-first metaphysics, or in its rivals, supply moral authority? Moral authority here means the relation under which one agent's interests give another agent binding reasons. The chapter expands the second half of article section 7 through three packets. The first is the moral-grounding audit's Craig and Railton attributions, together with the project's own normative-bridge inferences. The second is the Phase 2 inspection of the Korsgaard and Nagel exchange on constructivism and the scope of reasons. The third is the Phase 2 inspection of the Enoch and Lenman exchange on robust realism and indispensability. Component boundary H-A binds throughout: consciousness, unity, creation, power and endurance do not automatically supply binding reasons. That boundary is the project's declared boundary, and what this chapter documents is that the inspected records leave it standing: none of the five items is shown to supply binding reasons automatically, and no inspected argument establishes consciousness as their unique ground. It is not a theorem about the world. The evidential challenges to a good foundation, suffering and hiddenness, belong to the next chapter. This chapter asks where obligation itself could come from.

## From explaining agents to what they owe one another

<!-- id: c09-p003 -->
The article's framing sets the burden. An account of why beings exist is not yet an account of what they owe one another. Even shared consciousness would not settle whether another subject's interest supplies an overriding reason for me. So the question is not whether a metaphysics can produce agents, or even valuable agents. The question is whether it can explain the normative relation between them. The article's comparison finds each of its three stopping points, constructivism, realism and conscious authority, owing something at this very point.

<!-- id: c09-p004 -->
Consider first the strongest integrated case for grounding morality in consciousness, as the audit records it. It is a chain of bridges, and each bridge can be contested on its own. Start with subjects for whom conditions can be good or bad. The case must then assume that reasons to protect such subjects are objective, and it must propose one necessarily good personal foundation whose relation to agents grounds binding demands. Its bridge principles ask for four things. Valence must have normative significance. Relevant others must count even against my own aims. The foundation must be the relevant good whose relation supplies legitimate authority. And its commands must be identifiable and consistent with that nature. The audit's conclusion is qualified twice over. The full account could unify welfare, reasons, obligations and accountability if every bridge is warranted; and consciousness alone proves none of the later bridges. The objection the audit records is that calling a being good or creative may presuppose the very normativity being explained. The reply it records is that grounding need not be definition or temporal production; it may be identity or constitution. But an identity or constitution account still owes warrant for the identity and for the relation.

<!-- id: c09-p005 -->
The divine-command position is attributed to its source, and its own self-imposed limits travel with it. Craig grounds value in God's nature and obligation in commands from a qualified authority. On his account the reasons for a command do not themselves constitute the obligation. He permits contingent commands consistent with essential goodness, and in doing so he corrects his earlier necessary-command formulation [@S-T15-001]. The inspected text is a 2022 author-hosted podcast transcript. The authors Craig quotes or discusses there were not independently inspected, and the audio alignment is unverified. Both claims are recorded as attribution only, not as established moral or metaphysical truths. It should also be noted that the inspected transcript contains no independent proof of the authority relation itself.

<!-- id: c09-p006 -->
The project's own bridge inferences say what the package still owes, and they serve as this chapter's recurring measure. There are four. First, creator status, causal dependence and superior power do not entail moral authority without a normative relation, and necessary goodness and reliable command identification require their own warrant. Second, consciousness can supply subjects of benefit and harm; but to identify valenced experience as reason-giving, and to extend concern across subjects, requires explicit normative bridges. Third, shared subjecthood could align prudential incentives without establishing impartial obligations, permissible tradeoffs or the protection of distinct persons. Fourth, personal endurance may enable eventual accountability and restoration, yet neither duration nor sanctions explains why an act is wrong. All four are project-authored conditional analyses, and they are marked as such.

## Constructivism and the reflective bridge

<!-- id: c09-p007 -->
Korsgaard's proposal, inspected in the original chapters, is the article's constructivist bridge. An agent must take some consideration as a reason, and practical identity helps determine which considerations can be endorsed. She grounds practical authority in reflective agency, without treating reflective self-conception as proof of a metaphysical self. She links reasons and obligations to practical identity, while distinguishing value from right action and from authoritative requirements. She argues that valuing anything requires valuing humanity as the source of our contingent identities. And she uses the shareability of reasons to defend obligations to others [@S-P2T06-001]. Notice the shape of the argument. It seeks to connect commitment to one's own humanity with recognition of humanity in others. It does not derive obligation from sheer power or causal origin, and that is the very derivation the previous section found missing.

<!-- id: c09-p008 -->
Korsgaard states her own limits, and they matter because the exchange turns on them. She distinguishes formal universalization from a moral law ranging over all rational beings; the first argument does not by itself establish the interpersonal domain. The humanity argument's extension to other persons requires an additional argument. She extends moral standing to conscious animals without making them reflective agents, and she does so on illustrations that are not independently verified empirical evidence in this packet. She denies that bare reflective endorsement makes every action right, since psychological explanation alone is not her criterion of justification. And she permits conflicts between obligations, while expressly not disproving complete practical normative skepticism [@S-P2T06-001]. A theory this candid about its remaining steps does something useful. It supplies a map of what any successor, including a consciousness-based one, would have to complete.

<!-- id: c09-p009 -->
Nagel's objection, also inspected, reverses the direction of explanation. He disputes that free self-determination must have a law-like form merely because it is causation, and he offers an external reflective standpoint as an alternative explanation of generality. He then argues that reasons ground practical identity rather than derive from it. A reflective egoist can understand a public demand while disputing its force; public intelligibility is not obviously impartial justification [@S-P2T06-002]. His realism enters the record as a substantive evaluative rival. It is not a proof by rejection of Korsgaard, and its own warrant is just as contested.

<!-- id: c09-p010 -->
Korsgaard's actual reply, inspected in the same volume, comes with its scope stated. She argues that general commitments constitute willing, which distinguishes genuine choice from a mere succession of impulses. She expressly limits this new argument to formal universality, and at the recorded page she adds no new defense of humanity-wide scope or of publicity. She distinguishes an action's incentive from its endorsement, denying that identity-based motivation means seeking a reward. And she locates the justificatory work in endorsement. She answers the attributed Mafioso objection with the claim that fuller reflection supplies a deeper moral demand. She retains a sense of nonmoral obligation under incomplete reflection, and Cohen's original chapter remains uninspected [@S-P2T06-003]. The adjudication recorded in the packet is this. The reply answers a formal-agency objection, but it does not independently settle every bridge from agency to impartial moral authority.

<!-- id: c09-p011 -->
What does the exchange contribute to this project? A narrowed gap, not a winner. The Phase 2 packet's closing inference makes two points. The selected reply leaves the direction-of-justification dispute open, and Nagel's alternative, on which evaluative reasons independently justify self-conceptions, is still live. And the developed rational-grounding proposal narrows the source gap of the first version, while establishing neither a fundamental conscious foundation nor its moral authority. No new evidence of a universal subject, survival, timelessness, preservation, cosmic intention or foundational goodness enters through this exchange.

## Robust realism and indispensability

<!-- id: c09-p012 -->
Enoch's robust realism, inspected in the book's first chapter, is the article's second stopping point. It holds that there is objective, irreducibly normative truth, and that attitudes may matter normatively without constitutively determining the fundamental normative truths. Two separations, both recorded, discipline everything that follows. Realist ontology is separated from epistemic warrant, so robust realism can coexist with normative skepticism. And Enoch himself denies that his objectivity and metanormative-realism arguments, even taken together, entail robust metaethical realism [@S-P2T07-001]. The position enters this chapter as a characterization. It is not a proof.

<!-- id: c09-p013 -->
The indispensability route has a declared inspection gap at its center. Enoch's introduction presents deliberative indispensability as on a par with explanatory indispensability, and as supporting belief in irreducible normative facts. The chapter that develops the argument was inspected only at its opening and at its concluding page. The opening targets general normative realism, not moral realism. The concluding page makes the claimed reason defeasible, and it acknowledges that full support requires rejecting alternatives not completely addressed there. Pages 53 to 83, including the epistemological derivation, remain uninspected [@S-P2T07-001]. As the packet records it, the argument has three steps. Deliberation is rationally non-optional. Normative truths, and belief in them, are instrumentally indispensable to that project. And indispensability to an appropriate non-optional project can justify ontological commitment. Each step is contested in the exchange reported below.

<!-- id: c09-p014 -->
Lenman's countercase, also inspected, asks whether the work can be done with something thinner. His schmeliberation takes reflectively scrutinized contingent concerns as its success standards, and it claims a deliberation-like phenomenology without robust truth. The challenge is that non-optionality and a thickness that requires robust truth cannot both be satisfied at once; only the thinner practice is indispensable [@S-P2T07-002]. Two further notes are recorded. His expressivism differs from descriptive naturalism. And he distinguishes psychological truth conditions from consciousness constitutively determining normative truth conditions; that is a conditional normative independence, and it is not evidence about fundamental consciousness. It should also be said that no population finding about how people deliberate is established by the phenomenological claims on either side.

<!-- id: c09-p015 -->
Enoch's reply, also inspected, defends a remaining claim to justification without turning it into truth. Similarity to choosing does not establish identity with deliberation. Success standards may lack deserved allegiance. Deliberative phenomenology and person-relative justification can vary, and he explicitly denies that indispensability makes normative truths true. His instrumental indispensability also includes a sufficiently diminishing reason to engage, which is revisable under contrary evidence [@S-P2T07-003]. The reply defends the claimed reason. It is not a substitute for the uninspected developed case at pages 53 to 83. The packet also preserves a concession the article does not reach: Enoch acknowledges that he needs, but does not supply, a criterion that distinguishes legitimate Moorean reasoning from bootstrapping, and Joyce's original is uninspected. The upshot is symmetric. Lenman's alternative does not by itself remove Enoch's claimed reason to believe, and the deserved-allegiance demand may restate the disputed richness rather than resolve it.

<!-- id: c09-p016 -->
The moral bridge in Enoch's fourth chapter, inspected complete, is expressly conditional and expressly weak. Assume moral objectivity, and assume a justified robust general normative realism. Then, he argues, the familiar motivations for withholding a specifically moral realism weaken: the metaphysical and epistemic objections generalize, and moral facts are proposed to add no new ontological kind. Yet Enoch explicitly denies entailment and allows that unconsidered motivations remain [@S-P2T07-001]. The limits travel with the bridge. The cumulative-cost counterexample is acknowledged in the text. The detailed replies on metaphysics, knowledge, disagreement and motivation are deferred to chapters 6 through 9, which remain unread. And a local nonmoral expressivism is not ruled out. His treatment of categorical reasons distinguishes a rule's applicability despite indifference from an actual reason applying regardless of motivation, and in a recorded note he concedes that a successful Schroeder-style desire-grounded account could meet one sense of categorical. The moral rationalism he defends is the weak claim that morality's connection to reasons is not wholly contingent. There is no guarantee that every agent complies, there is no overriding priority, and exceptional cases are allowed. He does not require morality itself to be the source of the reasons. The record notes that this weak conclusion differs from Railton's separately acknowledged limitation, but does not by itself refute it. The antecedent chapters function here as assumptions; reading chapter 4 establishes neither of them.

<!-- id: c09-p017 -->
The reduction question is left open by the book's own inspected section. In section 5.1 Enoch distinguishes conceptual from metaphysical irreducibility, and he distinguishes identity from reduction, grounding, constitution and type/token claims. He admits that the natural/normative framing is imprecise and that his argument is inconclusive against all naturalisms. He acknowledges that the familiar reduction objections are inconclusive and that his response to Schroeder does not prove irreducibility; the impasse, as he locates it, is over an initial intuition of normative force. And he remains open to abandoning that intuition for a sufficiently strong specific reduction, crediting naturalism's parsimony and flagging token-level reduction as worth developing [@S-P2T07-001]. The originals of the critics he cites, Schroeder, the Moral Twin Earth literature, Smith, Fine and Parfit, remain uninspected; only Enoch's treatment is attributed. So robust realism enters the comparison with a foundational debt of its own, and its own defender has set the price.

<!-- id: c09-p018 -->
Railton's naturalistic route, inspected in the original article, is the recorded secular alternative that takes the reduction option seriously. Other secular families are retained in the record. His non-moral good concerns objective interests: natural dispositional facts ground the idealized informed preference, and not the other way round. His substantive moral criterion is impartial consideration of affected interests under full information, and he himself labels that criterion controversial. He separates the applicability of obligation from categorical reasons for every rational agent, which his account does not guarantee [@S-T15-002]. His reforming naturalistic definitions are recorded as theoretical proposals, and their empirical explanatory success remains a burden. The audit's conclusion holds. A physicalist ontology has a substantive route to moral realism. The route remains contested, and it does not settle every demand for categorical reasons: a fully informed egoist can still reject impartiality, and the recorded reply separates truth and applicability from the agent's motivation.

## Conscious authority: the unmet relation

<!-- id: c09-p019 -->
The article's diagnosis, which this chapter has now documented exchange by exchange, is simple. A theory that begins with consciousness does not dissolve the normative question, because explaining how a demand arises is different from explaining why it merits compliance. Korsgaard explains how reflective agents come to treat considerations as reasons. Nagel objects that this gets the direction of justification backwards. Enoch explains why deliberators may be entitled to believe in robust reasons. Lenman offers the same practice without the ontology. Railton builds normative properties from natural ones, at a price he names. A common-consciousness proposal faces the same fork. It can explain the genesis of demands through shared subjecthood, or it can assert their objective force. What the inspected records do not contain is any derivation of the second from the first.

<!-- id: c09-p020 -->
The unmet relation can be stated plainly from the recorded bridges. What a conscious foundation would owe is not more consciousness but a normative relation. It would need warrant that the foundation is good in the reason-giving sense, and not merely powerful, creative or enduring. It would need warrant that its relation to agents legitimates demands, and does not merely enable sanctions. And what it requires would need to be identifiable. The recorded reply on behalf of the strongest case, that grounding can be identity or constitution rather than definition or temporal production, remains a live option; but it still owes warrant for the identity and for the relation. This is component boundary H-A applied. It is not a refutation. The records leave open that such warrant could be supplied; what they document is that nothing inspected supplies it.

<!-- id: c09-p021 -->
What about the prudential shortcut? It is recorded as insufficient. If all subjects were one subject, harming another might become imprudent. But shared subjecthood that aligns incentives is not impartial obligation, and the audit's analysis is explicit that unity does not by itself establish permissible tradeoffs or the protection of distinct persons. Sanctions and endurance likewise enable accountability without explaining wrongness. The objection the audit records against the strongest integrated case makes the same point from inside it. Unity and sanctions can yield only self-interest. An account on which wrongdoing is ultimately self-harm has therefore moved from obligation to prudence; it has not derived one from the other. The normative gap survives the metaphysical merger.

<!-- id: c09-p022 -->
The dependence rule for normative premises ties this chapter to its neighbors. The audit's synthesis argument records that the value judgments which support the moral-grounding proposal also underwrite the objections from evil. So the same moral premise must not be counted as independent evidence each time it recurs, and the standards for explanatory stopping points and for epistemic access must be applied symmetrically to divine and secular accounts. That mapping assigns burdens to the goodness and authority components, with conditional effects on intention, preservation and survival. It identifies neither a numerical posterior nor a winning full metaphysics. The evil and hiddenness exchanges that test the goodness component belong to the next chapter. What carries forward from here is the symmetry rule itself, which binds this chapter and chapter 10 wherever the same moral premises recur. The endurance claims this chapter shares with chapter 8 rest on their own recorded supporting claims; that is a separate relation.

## Comparative stopping points

<!-- id: c09-p023 -->
The article's comparative conclusion stands as recorded. The normative comparison remains substantive, and it does not have to wait for a verdict on fundamental ontology. Constructivism owes a bridge from agency to the scope and force of reasons; that is Korsgaard's own explicit remainder. Realism owes warrant for its normative starting point; that is Enoch's own impasse, priced by him. Conscious authority owes a relation between its nature and legitimate claims on agents; those are the audit's unmet bridges. None of the three gains an exemption by naming its stopping point. The article's further point also holds in the records: a successful metaphysics of consciousness could illuminate the bearers and objects of value, and still need to explain why these reasons apply and when they override others.

<!-- id: c09-p024 -->
What the comparison does not license matters as much as what it does. No account is declared the winner. Nagel's realism is not vindicated by Korsgaard's concessions. Korsgaard's constructivism is not refuted by Nagel's objection. Enoch's realism survives Lenman's countercase only as an undefeated claim to justified belief. Railton's naturalism remains a contested proposal. The audit's earlier matrix reconstruction of constructivism and non-natural realism, recorded at the time with no decisive original defender inspected, is not withdrawn. The Phase 2 original inspections now supply this chapter's constructivist and robust-realist attributions and narrow that source gap, while the corresponding gap register entry remains partial and open. Each family's stopping point remains a commitment, and nothing in the evidence ledger settles the choice among them.

<!-- id: c09-p027 -->
Let me be clear about what this chapter has not shown, and cannot conclude. It cannot conclude that moral realism is true or false, that constructivism succeeds or fails, that categorical reasons exist, or that any account of moral authority is correct. It cannot conclude that a conscious foundation has moral authority, and it cannot conclude that it lacks it. What it can say is only this: consciousness, unity, creation, power and endurance do not automatically supply binding reasons, and the inspected arguments establish neither consciousness as the unique ground of reasons nor a completed relation from a foundation to its authority. That is component boundary H-A, stated as a finding about the ledger and not about the world. Korsgaard's grounding of practical authority in reflective agency, reported earlier in this chapter, is the constructivist remainder that stands beside that boundary; it is not evidence that no derivation from consciousness was ever offered. No component of the fundamental-consciousness package receives support from this chapter: not fundamentality, universal subjecthood, brain independence, survival beyond irreversible death, timelessness, preservation, intention, goodness or moral authority. And all twelve withheld stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld through it.
