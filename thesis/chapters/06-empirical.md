<!-- id: c06-p001 -->
The two preceding chapters priced philosophical routes to a conscious foundation. This chapter turns to the empirical record inherited from the article's fifth section: what controlled neural interventions establish about the dependence of experience on brain activity, and what crisis-experience reports — the near-death and resuscitation material — can and cannot show. The chapter's hinge is a conjunctive evidential standard: a crisis report discriminates between rival accounts of mind only when externally accurate content, a defensible acquisition interval and adequate information about the processes available during that interval are all established together. Four case dossiers, three prospective studies, a historical and cross-cultural corpus and their critic-and-reply chains are assessed against that standard, and the chapter closes with the evidential asymmetry between the two streams, the dependence accounting and an explicit statement of what none of this material concludes.

<!-- id: c06-p002 -->
The standing caution of the previous chapters binds here with additions specific to empirical material. Every clinical number, timing estimate and experience description in this chapter is an attribution to an identified inspected version, not a project-verified measurement: two of the case dossiers rest partly on user-supplied purported transcripts whose audio, speaker identity and timestamps were never verified; two rest partly on converted ebooks whose conversion metadata postdates publication; several rest on current webpages that are not authenticated snapshots of their original wording. A verified report is not a verified event. Each narrator's crisis and recovery episodes form one reporting dependency group, so retellings, translations and republications never add observations; and counts keep their units throughout — people, resuscitation episodes, interviews, EEG images and analysis windows are not interchangeable. The specific dependency structure is stated in the closing section.

## Controlled intervention evidence and its scope

<!-- id: c06-p003 -->
The article's demand is for discrimination, not covariation, and the record's cleanest intervention case supplies it at single-patient scale. In Parvizi and colleagues' 2012 study, electrical stimulation of a right fusiform face-selective region pair in one 45-year-old epilepsy patient elicited reported face distortions on seven real-face trials, while four sham trials and three trials stimulating a nearby control pair did not [@S-T09-004]. The sham and control contrasts support a causal contribution within this case, which is more than correlation supplies. The record prices the limits with equal care: nonspecific nonface changes also occurred, the two stimulated regions were not separated, current can affect connected sites, the small famous-face and place naming tests differed procedurally and their null differences support no equivalence conclusion, and randomization and assessor masking were not established. A 2013 correction changes only an author name [@S-T09-005]. One patient's repeated trials are not independent people, and no unitary face center follows — the local-versus-network question stays open.

<!-- id: c06-p004 -->
Tasserie and colleagues' 2022 macaque study extends the intervention evidence to state functions. Under maintained propofol anesthesia, high central-thalamic deep brain stimulation raised the reported behavioral arousal score from 0 of 11 to 9 of 11 in both stimulated animals, while low central-thalamic stimulation reached 3 of 11 and ventrolateral control stimulation remained 0 of 11; awake animals score 11 of 11 [@S-T09-002]. Stimulation also shifted fMRI dynamics and enhanced hierarchical auditory responses. The record's qualifications are structural. The intervention unit is two animals, with three separate awake comparators and reused awake auditory data; blinding and randomized condition order were not established in the inspected text; subjective awareness is inferred from signatures, since macaques give no trialwise reports; the auditory paradigm had no ventrolateral control arm; and the supplementary methods disclose that each animal's high stimulation setting was selected empirically just above its behavioral arousal threshold, which limits interpretation as an unselected dose-response estimate [@S-P2T19-002]. Per-run volume counts and overlapping analysis windows are not run totals by animal and condition; those totals were not located in the inspected extents.

<!-- id: c06-p005 -->
Scheinin and colleagues' anesthesia-and-sleep study, published in 2021, supplies the record's sharpest caution against equating unresponsiveness with absent experience. Forty healthy young men were recruited; 39 underwent randomized open-label propofol (19) or dexmedetomidine (20) anesthesia, and 37 returned for a sleep arm on average eighteen weeks later — overlapping arms, not 76 participants [@S-T09-003]. Among interviewable states, subjective experience was reported in 80% of anesthesia interviews and 71% of sleep interviews, including contentless impressions; these are interview rates, not population prevalence, and the exact integer numerators and denominators behind the percentages are not supplied in the inspected main text. A targeted Phase 2 search of the study's extended-data appendices found regional PET cluster tables rather than interview-count tables, so the integers remain unrecovered. The paper's PET contrasts locate state-linked thalamic, cingulate and angular blood-flow changes — associations within manipulated states, not focal necessity tests — and its authors expressly note that absent response or absent recalled content cannot conclusively establish absent experience.

<!-- id: c06-p006 -->
The Cogitate adversarial collaboration, whose main results appeared in Nature in 2025, is the record's largest theory-level test. The consortium recruited 256 participants across fMRI (120), MEG (102) and intracranial EEG (34), retaining 108, 97 and 32; main noninvasive tests used 73 held-out fMRI and 65 held-out MEG datasets, while invasive data were not split [@S-T09-001]. Suprathreshold visual stimuli in four categories at three durations were tested under varying task relevance; the design did not directly contrast conscious with unconscious processing or perturb candidate regions. Operational predictions contrasted posterior versus prefrontal decoding, posterior duration tracking versus prefrontal onset/offset ignition, and posterior versus long-range prefrontal synchrony. The outcome is mixed: sustained posterior representations were partly supported and transient prefrontal category decoding appeared, but the predicted prefrontal offset profile and sustained posterior gamma synchrony were not established, and the preregistered pairwise-phase-consistency analysis supported neither critical connectivity prediction [@S-T09-008]. An exploratory amplitude-based dynamic functional connectivity analysis partly supported the workspace theory's expectation while still lacking sustained posterior connectivity; the record refuses to relabel it as preregistered confirmation. No overall theory winner follows.

<!-- id: c06-p007 -->
The Phase 2 protocol audit qualifies the confirmatory weight of those results without erasing them. The selected OSF amendment file, named v4 but internally identified as Version 4.5 and dated 22 December 2022, already specifies a one-third optimization split and unsplit intracranial data, whereas the February 2023 published protocol contains different half-split wording; the contrast cannot date the change, the node is not a certified immutable preregistration, and the earlier amendment chain and analyst data-access history were not audited [@S-P2T19-001]. The amendment is internally inconsistent about fMRI counts (110 quality-passed datasets alongside a 36/80 division), while the final paper uses 35 optimization and 73 held-out datasets and its supplement explains the discrepancy only partly. The supplement also declares analysis departures: representational similarity analysis used all trial pairs rather than the planned split sets, the main fMRI connectivity analysis pooled task relevance, the multiple-comparison method changed, synchrony-channel eligibility was relaxed relative to the amendment's active windows, and three below-threshold intracranial patients were retained as a declared deviation. Reporting a departure neither proves outcome-driven bias nor removes the departure.

<!-- id: c06-p008 -->
The reply chain is preserved with the same discipline. Naccache and colleagues' 2025 critique argues that attended offset and continuing awareness were not established, that macroscopic prefrontal decoding may miss mixed selectivity, and that activity-silent storage is not obligatory for workspace theory [@S-T09-006]. The record verifies these as documented arguments, not as measured inattention, and it retains two qualifications on both sides. First, the 2023 protocol itself conditions a workspace offset response on conscious detection in its overview while elsewhere treating onset and offset as tested expectations — so the offset objection was not entirely post hoc, and the failed test was not unconditional either [@S-T09-007]. Second, the critique's page-2 wording includes category among allegedly absent prefrontal decodings although the Nature results report transient prefrontal category decoding; the record preserves this as a local wording problem, not a substantive correction. The theory proponents' supplement replies — coverage, frequency choice and region-criteria explanations from the integrated-information side, and the consortium's own statement that shared successful predictions do not uniquely confirm either theory — are contemporaneous interpretations [@S-T09-008]. A bounded forward search found no later consortium rebuttal to the critique; absence of discovery is not absence of reply.

<!-- id: c06-p009 -->
What the intervention stream establishes, on the record as inspected, is causal contribution: site-dependent stimulation changes reported perceptual content in a human patient, and state-targeted stimulation restores arousal and access markers in anesthetized animals, under controls that make passive correlation an incomplete description. Models denying any neural contribution to embodied experience are thereby challenged. The article's framing of the transmission reply is inherited: a theorist who holds that altering an interface alters what appears through it escapes logical refutation, but bare compatibility is a weak explanatory achievement — the alternative must explain why this intervention changes this content, and a metaphor of reception supplies no such relation. What the stream does not establish is equally recorded: no unique universally necessary and sufficient neural mechanism, no physicalist identity, and no adjudication among identity, realization, interface and idealist accounts that specify the dependence explicitly. Neural dependence in the tested contexts is a constraint every surviving model must carry, not a verdict on ontology.

## The conjunctive standard for crisis-report discrimination

<!-- id: c06-p010 -->
Crisis reports enter the record because of what they would show if a demanding conjunction held. Accurate information acquired under conditions excluding the relevant ordinary processes would put pressure on a specified neural account — and the operative evidence is conjunctive: externally accurate content, a defensible interval of acquisition and adequate information about the processes available during that interval. The article's framing, which this chapter applies throughout, is that a striking report with uncertain timing does not have the same discriminatory force, and a physiological interval does not establish when remembered content was formed. Retrospective memory that accurately dates its own content to an excluded interval is precisely what the cases below cannot independently demonstrate; every dossier's positive argument is conditional on that bridge.

<!-- id: c06-p011 -->
The standard is symmetric, and the record enforces the symmetry. A proposed ordinary mechanism — restored circulation, residual hearing, sedation-shaped dreaming, later reconstruction — also needs case-specific grounds before it counts as the demonstrated explanation; a plausible possibility is not a diagnosis. The recorded cross-case inference for the Reynolds and dentures dossiers states the first direction: those two dossiers support specific, traceable perception claims and substantive disputes, but lack independently fixed perception timing plus complete information-access controls needed to identify extracerebral acquisition. The other two dossiers carry their own packet conclusions: the Neal material supports attributed experience and a conditional argument, not survival; the Alexander documentation supports richer attributions without independently fixing all recalled stages to an interval excluding relevant ordinary acquisition. And conversely, in no dossier has a critic's mechanism been demonstrated as the actual cause of any particular report. Both failures matter, and neither substitutes for the other.

## Case dossiers: Neal, Alexander, Reynolds, dentures/TG

<!-- id: c06-p012 -->
The Neal dossier concerns a reported 1999 kayak entrapment on a Chilean river and a later intensive-care episode, reaching the record through one narrator's reporting chain. The inspected layers are a publisher-hosted author interview of unknown date [@S-T04-001], a user-supplied purported talk transcript whose 1,210 cues were fully read but whose audio identity and timing were never verified [@S-T04-009], and a converted WaterBrook ebook of the memoir — eISBN 9780984819218, copyright 2011/2012, version 3.1, with 2019 conversion metadata and no print-page equivalence; the original 2011 edition was identified in catalogs but never inspected [@S-P2T14-009]. Both reported episodes — river and ICU — carry their own case identifiers inside a single narrator dependence group, and the memoir's Q&A itself states that the first edition did not clearly identify Jesus — a self-reported change with no independent collation against the original 2011 text.

<!-- id: c06-p013 -->
The reported experience is specific and internally articulated. The transcript and memoir describe calm surrender during entrapment, an apparent external vantage on the rescue, intermittent returns to breathe, and seeming simultaneity alongside narrated sequence [@S-T04-009]. The memoir names four rescuers and reports that the author later interviewed each individually while preparing the book; it also narrates bodily sensations during release, gaps in physical memory, and a later hospital encounter narrated across two chapters [@S-P2T14-009]. The record's treatment is uniform: these are attributed experiences whose external accuracy is not thereby established. Named witnesses and a reported interview method strengthen specificity, but no separately authored witness statement, interview transcript or information-exposure history was inspected, and witness thoughts reach the record only through the narrator. Altered temporal phenomenology, including the hours-of-experience report, does not establish intrinsically nonsuccessive consciousness.

<!-- id: c06-p014 -->
The rescue-interval audit shows why elapsed time cannot do physiological work. The memoir gives eleven to fourteen minutes from repeated headcounts to CPR on a named rescuer's watch; the transcript instead gives fifteen minutes starting when rescuers inferred her location; a 2013 CNN report explicitly calls its thirty-minute oxygen interval an estimate; and a whitewater accident database's compiled description asserts thirty minutes underwater while its injury field says near-drowning [@S-P2T14-008; @S-P2T14-002]. The sources also conflict on the accident date (January 14 versus February 3, 1999) and the age of a family member, and no original timekeeper account resolves them. The record's conclusion is deliberately limited: none of these intervals — estimated, watch-timed or compiled — measures circulation, oxygen delivery or neural activity; the memoir's later saturation figures concern hospital care, not a timed river neural trace; and survival with reported testing without brain damage is a narrated outcome, not a chart diagnosis [@S-P2T14-009].

<!-- id: c06-p015 -->
The foreknowledge claim receives the dossier's most exacting chronology. The narrator reports learning during the crisis that her oldest son would die; dated newspaper reporting establishes his death in a roller-ski collision on June 21, 2009, and the complete inspected article discloses no earlier prediction — a bounded content check, not proof that no prediction was made elsewhere [@S-P2T14-003]. Every inspected prediction disclosure postdates the fulfillment. The memoir narrates early confidants and a manuscript saved before news of the death, but its own draft labels conflict — a spring 2009 first draft revised and saved June 21 in the body, against a Q&A calling the hours-before-death manuscript the first complete draft — and the transcript's wording does not explicitly state same-day completion [@S-P2T14-009; @S-T04-009]. The same memoir separately attributes to the son a childhood belief that he would not reach eighteen, then narrates his life past eighteen with a changed-plan interpretation — an internally narrated counterexample under a literal reading. With no independently dated prefulfillment record, no fixed deadline, cause or scoring set, and no all-prediction denominator, the record retains attributed prediction and documented reporting chronology while withholding verified foreknowledge and any odds.

<!-- id: c06-p016 -->
The dossier's critic-and-reply chain is journalistically mediated but real. A 2016 National Geographic feature reports Nelson accepting the intensity of Neal's memory while challenging its survival interpretation and proposing REM intrusion related to hypoxia [@S-P2T14-004]; Barker's published response — a different experiencer, not Neal — argues that physiological correlates could accompany a spirit's departure and need not settle the interpretation [@S-P2T14-005]. The record's verdict keeps the exchange in proportion: the critic's mechanism is a possibility, not an established case cause; the reply blocks an automatic dismissal without supplying the missing measurements; and the packet's strongest positive argument remains conditional — worth exact documentation, supporting attributed experience, and not establishing survival. Available testimony cannot fix the experience to a measured absence of circulation and relevant neural function, and the possible ordinary explanations remain equally unconfirmed for this case.

<!-- id: c06-p017 -->
The Alexander dossier concerns a 2008 Escherichia coli meningitis with reported onset November 10, 2008, and it is the record's most heavily documented single case. The inspected layers are a user-supplied purported talk transcript, audio unverified [@S-T06-001]; a converted 2012 ebook of the memoir with 2023 conversion metadata, disclosing changed names and family plus professional editorial assistance [@S-T06-006]; current webpages — Lam's research report bearing an October 11, 2011 publication label with an April 2014 update [@S-P2T15-003], a Skeptiko interview transcript whose November 22, 2011 date is archive-search metadata only [@S-P2T15-002], and the author's current account page [@S-T06-005] — none authenticated as original historical wording; a treating physician's separately authored signed statement; and a 2018 external clinical review. The memoir itself says the author reconstructed that week's ordinary hospital events from others because he remembers none of them, so the published dialogue is reconstruction, not witness transcripts.

<!-- id: c06-p018 -->
The dossier's developed argument is candidly strong in form: claimed global cortical incapacity plus ultra-real, intricate experience is offered to exclude brain production, and the memoir's appendix rejects nine alternative hypotheses principally on those two grounds [@S-T06-006]. The record preserves the argument's internal qualifications, which matter more than any critic's. The appendix's own sixth item says full-thickness cortical destruction is unnecessary for its functional-disruption argument, which cannot then confirm the subtitle-level claim of demonstrated destruction across eight lobes; the memoir allows deeper housekeeping function while arguing neocortical nonfunction; and the talk transcript reports a proposed brain-death assessment as a next step, not a completed diagnosis. These are the author's premises and interpretation; neither credentials nor felt certainty validates them, and the claimed cortical exclusion is asserted, not measured.

<!-- id: c06-p019 -->
The clinical documentation is stronger than memoir alone and still short of the standard. Wade's signed appendix attests acute E. coli meningitis, prompt antibiotics, coma with ventilation, day-7 awakening and remarkable recovery — a treating physician's separately authored statement, not a raw chart, and its mortality figures (including over 97% after prolonged coma) come without denominators or a calculation model [@S-P2T15-004]. The 2018 external reviewers, reporting a review of the original records that this project has not itself inspected, describe severe meningitis, an admission Glasgow Coma Scale of 11 with subsequent low scores, sedation, and — decisively for this chapter — no EEG [@S-T06-002]. Documented comparisons stay unreconciled: the 2018 paper places extubation on day 6 and improvement on day 7 where memoir, physician statement and current page place awakening on day 7; admission GCS 11 stands against the memoir's and a defender's 8; laboratory timing differs across tellings. The record preserves these as version limits rather than harmonizing them.

<!-- id: c06-p020 -->
Timing anchors and the photograph episode carry the dossier's positive weight, and both are retrospective. The 2018 paper reports disclosure on day 9, retrospective corroboration of praying visitors reportedly present on day 5, and an inferred experience interval within days 1–5 — with no original interview dates or validated exclusion of ordinary acquisition [@S-T06-002]. The memoir itself identifies its principal temporal anchors as one absent-person report and six recalled faces, explicitly calling other simultaneity conjectural [@S-T06-006]. The photograph episode is the strongest single claim: the author knew before the coma that a birth sister had died (2000) and had reunited with birth relatives (2007); the memoir places receipt of her photograph four months after discharge, with recognition the following morning during a reading of an analogous case. No blinded comparison, no dated pre-photo description and no independently dated 2009 document exist in the record; a defender's 2013 interview testimony that a prior description matched the photograph is retrospective, unblinded and family-circle corroboration [@S-T06-004]. The reported six-week, more-than-20,000-word account written before reading the NDE literature rests on later statements, with no dated manuscript inspected.

<!-- id: c06-p021 -->
The dispute chain ends in documented underdetermination. A 2013 Esquire article reports the attending physician describing chemical sedation and delirious responsiveness, challenging the wholly-nonfunctioning-brain premise [@S-T06-003]; a defender's long reply disputes the portrayal's context, printing an attributed objection from the same physician relayed through family and publisher, with her nonresponse to direct calls recorded [@S-T06-004]; and the 2018 reviewers explicitly leave the relative contributions of infection and sedation undetermined while arguing against a solely drug-induced account [@S-T06-002]. Shermer's published criticism adds the general argument that explanatory gaps and vivid experience do not establish supernatural causes [@S-T06-008]. The record's verdict on the whole dossier is the audit's: the combined documentation supports richer attributions than memoir alone, and it does not independently fix all recalled stages to an interval excluding relevant ordinary acquisition. Specific ordinary and nonordinary causes remain underdetermined — which licenses neither dismissal nor the memoir's survival-and-creator conclusion.

<!-- id: c06-p022 -->
The Reynolds dossier concerns a reported August 1991 aneurysm operation under hypothermic circulatory arrest, and it reaches the record almost entirely through Sabom's 1998 account, whose relevant chapter remains uninspected — catalog metadata only [@S-P2T16-001]. Around that inaccessible original stands an extended critic-and-reply chain. Woerlee's 2011 investigation develops an ordinary-hearing account: awareness plus drug effects, with technical detail supplemented from related surgical protocols because Sabom's account is nontechnical [@S-T19-005]. Hameroff's response disclaims case expertise, conditionally permits awareness under anesthesia, and challenges the missing brainstem-evoked-response results [@S-T19-006]. Carter's response supplies chronology through Sabom — placing the externally checkable perceptions before the recorded absence of brainstem responses, with the reported relatives-and-light episode not independently timed — and quotes the monitoring technologist locating herself in the adjacent operating room and requesting an exact-parameter experiment [@S-T19-007]. The article's verdict on this exchange is inherited: the disputes over auditory access, operative phases and monitoring are consequential precisely because specific reported details invite explanation, and general protocols cannot be substituted for the actual case protocol.

<!-- id: c06-p023 -->
The later exchange narrows claims on both sides without deciding the case. Woerlee's rejoinder accepts the distinction between a measurable brainstem auditory response and conscious hearing, hypothesizes that a different monitoring modality was likely used despite its absence from the cited surgical article, and — decisively for the record — explicitly leaves the immaterial explanation unexcluded because case data are insufficient [@S-P2T16-002]. His inference that the patient plausibly felt neither pain nor anxiety rests on separate reported symptom percentages; Carter's further reply objects that margins do not establish their intersection, and the recorded arithmetic bound — with 25 awareness descriptions, pain reported by 7 and anxiety by 9, those reporting neither can range from 9 to 16 — is conditional on quoted margins from a reproduced study of other patients, not a Reynolds sample [@S-P2T16-004]. Smit's letter reports actually performing the prescribed consumer sound demonstration: the clicks were intolerable, yet ambient sounds remained audible and speech worsened with tighter headphones; two quoted third-party reports add partial conversation and clear television speech under differing setups [@S-P2T16-003]. Smit's further inference — that unbearable clicks would have been remembered, so their absence supports nonphysical hearing — is preserved as argument, with the recorded limitation that absence from retellings does not prove inability to hear or actual nonphysical acquisition.

<!-- id: c06-p024 -->
Applied to the conjunctive standard, the Reynolds exchange illustrates the article's point that such debates narrow causal alternatives without increasing observations. One patient, one event, one reporting chain through one investigator: repeated publications are reuse, not replication. The proposed institutional hearing test, Smit's performed self-demonstration and an actual calibrated case-equivalent experiment are three different evidential states, and only the middle one exists in the record; a later webpage titled as a successful test remains uninspected after a stopped access route, its title supplying no outcome [@S-P2T16-005]. Skepticism about the exclusion claim does not establish that the ordinary-hearing mechanism actually produced every reported detail, and objections to that mechanism do not time-lock the experience to an interval excluding all relevant processing. The unresolved conjunction — original charts and traces missing, prefeedback accuracy record missing, no complete calibrated experiment — rather than the narrative's unusualness, is what limits the survival inference.

<!-- id: c06-p025 -->
The dentures dossier — a late-1970s Dutch resuscitation in which a patient reportedly later identified the nurse who had removed his dentures and described an unusual cart — has the record's longest documentation chain and its narrowest witness base. The chain runs from a 1991 nursing-journal article (identified, uninspected) through an Addink interview with nurse TG on February 2, 1994, its transcript dated February 3 (translated excerpts only; the full unpublished twelve-page transcript was never recovered), Smit's 2008 journal article [@S-T19-002], Rivas's detailed 2008 follow-up interview — published as a nine-page summary with selected literal passages from a record of more than thirty A4 pages [@S-P2T17-003] — the Winter 2008 critic-and-reply set, and the 2010 rejoinder, which states that the official medical records were destroyed [@S-T19-004]. Every substantive patient detail reaches the record through TG: the intermediary between TG and the 1991 author reported the story without participating in the resuscitation, and no investigator ever interviewed the patient. The event year itself is an inference — probably 1979 in the Dutch comparison, stated more definitely in a translator's introduction than the body warrants.

<!-- id: c06-p026 -->
The content and its corrections show a single witness's account under pressure, preserved without harmonization. TG describes a converted cart with a flat wooden pull-out shelf already extended and attributes a matching description to the patient — which specifically challenges an explanation based on hearing a metal drawer — while the Dutch interview article itself contains a colloquial drawer word alongside the television caption the defenders blame for it [@S-P2T17-003]. The published interview contains a pump-first passage in a prompted physiology exchange; TG's direct Winter 2008 reply explicitly corrects the sequence to denture removal before the mechanical compression device was switched on, and estimates more than five minutes of transport without cardiac massage — late estimates by the same witness, which do not exclude ambulance or bystander resuscitation before the device was connected, and whose Dutch no-massage wording a later translation weakens [@S-P2T17-005]. The reported experience has two phases — pain with a wish to stop, later fear that resuscitation would be abandoned — with the patient reportedly unable to locate the experience's beginning or end. The record preserves both the original passages and the corrections as retrospective statements of one witness, resolving neither into a contemporaneous clock.

<!-- id: c06-p027 -->
The critic's account and the audit's verdict close the dossier. Woerlee's Dutch criticism — expressly correcting his own earlier assumptions made from sparse information — proposes hypothermia, CPR-restored awareness, sensation, hearing and reconstruction as an ordinary account [@S-P2T17-004]; the defenders' 2010 rejoinder contests pump timing, felt-versus-seen removal and auditory reconstruction, uses flat-EEG language while supplying no tracing, and proposes a ten-participant blindfold experiment that was never performed [@S-T19-004]. The recorded inference limits run in both directions: a plausible ordinary account is not a demonstrated explanation of the whole event, and rejecting the metal-drawer mechanism or accepting TG's sincerity does not establish extracerebral perception. The dependency sensitivity is stated in the ledger: removing TG-dependent retrospective testimony removes the packet's entire positive contribution, and no number of publications or translations restores independence. Publication alongside the Dutch prospective study creates no roster membership in that cohort — the anecdote remains one retrospective case, contextually linked and never summed.

## Prospective studies: AWARE, Dutch cohort, denominator discipline

<!-- id: c06-p028 -->
AWARE I, published in 2014 from fifteen hospitals, defines the prospective baseline and its arithmetic frictions. The study reports 2,060 recorded arrest events and 330 discharge survivors; 140 completed stage-one interviews — though the same layout prints 52 in-hospital plus 90 post-discharge interviews and a table denominator of 142, an unresolved discrepancy the later AWARE II introduction compounds by citing 143 [@S-T07-001]. Of 101 stage-two interviewees, 46 reported no memory, 46 non-NDE memories, and 9 met the Greyson threshold, including two with auditory or visual event recall. Both event-recall cases occurred at locations without the study's concealed target shelves, and only one could be followed up, the other's health failing. The authors report medical-record corroboration for one case and infer roughly three minutes of awareness from a defibrillator sequence; the record keeps the inference's units apart — awareness up to three minutes and arrest lasting at least three minutes are different quantities — and notes that no participant-linked neural recording accompanies any reported content.

<!-- id: c06-p029 -->
The Dutch prospective study, published in 2001 from ten hospitals, contributes the largest early cohort and a lesson in version discipline. It reports 344 consecutively resuscitated patients across 509 resuscitation episodes — people and episodes are different units — with 62 broad-definition NDE reports (18%) and 41 core reports (12%); a printed 248 interviewed within five days (74%) lacks a clear matching denominator, and the record declines to manufacture one [@S-T07-004]. The 2002 published correction, inspected directly, changes one drug name in one sentence and no cohort counts or follow-up results [@S-P2T09-003]. Longitudinal reclassification changed the two-year groups from 37/37 to 35/39, with eight-year groups of 23 and 15 — the same retained people, not independent cohorts. The authors acknowledge possible site selection and use estimated arrest timing; no participant-specific EEG during any reported experience is presented.

<!-- id: c06-p030 -->
AWARE II, published in 2023 from a multi-center design spanning 25 sites in its main phase, is the record's most instrumented attempt and its clearest demonstration of the gap between deployment and opportunity. Of 567 recruited arrest subjects, 213 had sustained return of circulation, 53 survived to discharge, 28 completed interviews, and 11 reported any memory or perception, with overlapping thematic categories and six transcendent experiences at the Greyson threshold [@S-T07-007]. Tablet-and-headphone equipment was deployed in 365 of 567 cases, but deployment is not valid stimulus delivery — the protocol starts visual images at one minute and auditory fruit names at five minutes after activation, and the delivered-stimulus-by-interview intersections are never reported. No explicit recall of any target and no implicit visual identification occurred; one of 28 interviewees made a correct fruit choice, for which the protocol's any-three-fruits request supplies no uniform chance model; and a separate single participant's overt recollection of clinical surroundings received chart verification of a shock event, with unmeasured prior resuscitation knowledge acknowledged by the authors. Twenty-five discharge survivors were never interviewed.

<!-- id: c06-p031 -->
The AWARE II EEG arm changed the debate's terms while inheriting its unit discipline. The study captured 851 EEG screenshot images from 85 subjects during pulse-check pauses on a limited frontotemporal montage; 466 images from 53 subjects were interpretable, 49 of those subjects also had cerebral oximetry, and the authors' body-text summary reports near-normal or physiological rhythms up to 35 to 60 minutes into resuscitation — figure points extend beyond that summary, leaving the exact maximum unresolved — and an alpha-coma interpretation could not be excluded [@S-T07-007]. Images and people are different denominators; the paper reports suppression in 47% of EEG data, a data-unit figure a later correspondent paraphrases as about half the patients, and the record retains the original unit. The decisive intersection is stated in the study's own flow diagram: of the 28 interviewed survivors, exactly two had EEG data, and neither was among those with explicit cognitive recall. Aggregate rhythm timing cannot date any particular memory's formation, and missing EEG-report links do not establish absent consciousness.

<!-- id: c06-p032 -->
The published EEG exchange sharpens exactly that point, and the record preserves both its force and its limits. Greyson and van Lommel's letter argues that the EEG-consciousness interpretation fails for lack of overlap between recorded EEG and explicit recollections in the interviewed subset, and questions artifact exclusion [@S-P2T09-001]. Parnia's reply answers that absent explicit recall does not prove absent consciousness — invoking implicit memory — and defends the sampling, artifact removal and expert interpretation procedures [@S-P2T09-002]. The recorded adjudication is symmetric: the study does not establish a positive association between its EEG findings and experience, and the critics' missing association does not establish that the recorded patterns cannot be consciousness markers. The reply succeeds against the simple negative inference while supplying no participant-specific experience content, timing or independent artifact audit. An unmeasured association is not a demonstrated negative association, and neither letter supplies the missing intersection.

<!-- id: c06-p033 -->
The parallel methodological exchange with Martial and colleagues receives the same treatment. Their letter challenges EEG classification and missing data, the renaming of the experiences, the common-fruit interpretation and the timing exclusions [@S-T07-003]. The authors' direct reply — which explicitly answers Martial, a distinct chain from the Greyson reply — defends adapted EEG standards, attributes the missing time-frequency analysis to practical multichannel constraints, argues that stable clinical states cannot model evolving arrest physiology, and rebuts the fruit-guessing concern by citing more than 80,000 English nouns [@S-P2T09-005]. The record's annotations are pointed: a practical explanation for an omitted analysis does not supply its result, and the noun-count reference class does not match the protocol's actual request to name any three fruits, so no probability calculation is licensed in either direction. The reply's larger interpretive program — a disinhibition account of lucid experience near death, with its functional benefit expressly left unknown — is preserved as the authors' proposal, its physiological and evolutionary assertions not independently validated facts.

<!-- id: c06-p034 -->
The denominator discipline these studies enforce is the article's point about prospective improvement: defined populations and awareness tests improve the setting without converting every report into an effective target trial. AWARE II's 126-narrative community arm is a selected retrospective sample whose overlap with the prospective participants and with previously published narratives is unknown; its companion EEG analyses in separate manuscripts must be checked for shared participants before counting as replication [@S-T07-007]. The availability of a target somewhere in a study does not establish that a particular experiencer had the relevant opportunity, and margins describing separate subsets cannot supply their missing intersection. Across all three studies, the record's preserved nulls — eligibility counts, exposure intersections, interview denominators — are not zeros, and no pooled rate across studies is computed anywhere in the ledger.

## Historical and cross-cultural recurrence and dependence

<!-- id: c06-p035 -->
The historical record establishes that the reported phenomenon predates modern publicity, under heavy literary dependence. Bede's Ecclesiastical History, composed around 731 and inspected in a 1907 revised English translation, transmits Dryhthelm's return-from-apparent-death narrative through a named intermediary and dates the episode around 696; two adjacent chapters supply fatal deathbed visions with explicit admonitory purposes [@S-T08-001]. One author, one genre, one translation: the record counts a single documentary dependence group, no clinical status, and no independent witness chain. What the material supports is narrow and real — a documented medieval narrative with a luminous guide, punishment scenes, a pleasant landscape, return and lasting behavioral change — which weakens any claim that every such motif was invented after modern near-death literature.

<!-- id: c06-p036 -->
Heim's 1892 collection of mountaineering-fall experiences, inspected as a digitized German separatum with acknowledged OCR limits and no visual verification of the Fraktur scan, is the corpus's other historical anchor [@S-P2T18-001]. Heim draws on his own accidents, survivors' oral and written reports and literature, explicitly extrapolating survivors' sensations to the dying; his qualified claim that roughly 95% experienced the described state has no recoverable denominator — his approximately thirty examples concern potentially saving actions, a different question. His own fall report locates intensified thought, subjective time-lengthening and luminous life scenes during conscious falling, expressly ending at impact; the reported half-hour unresponsiveness afterward rests on his account of companions' statements. The text also documents its own contamination pressures: additional letters arrived after newspapers covered the talk, and the editor records a contested incident. The selected published English translation from 1972 remains body-uninspected — metadata only — so no rendering is certified [@S-P2T18-002]. A nineteenth-century document, not a clinical study; the record uses it for dated recurrence and nothing else.

<!-- id: c06-p037 -->
The cross-cultural comparisons contribute variation under unequal measurement. Pasricha and Stevenson's 1986 India report includes 16 of 18 incidentally identified cases — ten subject interviews, five firsthand-informant cases, one secondhand — with a median investigation delay around fourteen years; elicitation was unequal between the Indian and American material, and the comparison table's blanks mean no instance learned, possibly never asked [@S-T08-002]. Its recurring motifs — messengers in 12 of 16, mistaken-identity return in 10 of 16 — are selected-case descriptions, and the authors themselves argue that cultural variation neither proves belief-only production nor an afterlife. Ohkado and Greyson's 2014 reanalysis of 22 published Japanese interviews codes heavenly places in 16 and borders in 12 — flower gardens in 13 of the 16, rivers in 10 of the 12 — against zero panoramic life reviews in the sample, while acknowledging outside-sample counterexamples; the underlying interview books were not inspected and no independent bilingual audit exists [@S-T08-003]. Both papers propose cultural explanations without isolating culture as the sole cause; the record retains the motif differences as measured-in-sample only.

<!-- id: c06-p038 -->
The retrospective collections establish heterogeneity and its selection costs. Greyson and Bush's 1992 distressing-experience collection — fifty self-selected accounts from correspondence and a newsletter request — motivates three proposed types, including void and hellish experiences, persistent despair alongside fear-to-peace transitions, and one account of endless succession; the paper supplies no class frequency table, no person-episode crosswalk and a candid discussion of positive-affect scale bias and reporting reluctance [@S-T08-004]. Charland-Verville and colleagues' 2014 comparison retained 190 of 215 volunteered reports — 50 from non-life-threatening events and 140 from reported coma, at a mean reporting delay of 24 ± 15 years — and found no significant intensity or item-frequency differences between groups, which is not an equivalence result; its cutoff wording is internally inconsistent, and its clinical statuses are medically uncontrolled self-report [@S-T08-005]. Greyson's 1990 comparison of 183 self-described experiencers with 63 deniers, recruited through a newsletter, shows the scale and the self-label pulling apart in both directions [@S-T08-006]. Voluntary recruitment, long delays and unknown invitation denominators run through all three.

<!-- id: c06-p039 -->
Parnia and colleagues' 2001 Southampton study — the corpus's early prospective anchor, inspected in the exact repository journal PDF — interviewed 63 cardiac-arrest survivors: 56 with no recall, seven with some recall, four meeting the Greyson criterion [@S-P2T18-003]. Two participants endorsed the scale's lost-bodily-awareness item while the prose explicitly reports no out-of-body experience and no claimed opportunity to view the study's upward-facing ceiling boards; no target identification occurred, and the paper supplies no confirmed target-present-arrest-interview-viewpoint intersection from which effective trials could be counted. The Augustine-Greyson exchange over this and kindred studies is preserved with its concessions on both sides: Augustine concedes that possible embellishment or witness error is not thereby demonstrated while defending an antecedent-probability preference for ordinary explanations [@S-P2T18-004; @S-P2T18-006]; Greyson concedes the survival evidence is not compelling and that controlled failures require serious alternative explanations, while disputing the claim that paranormal-feature stability went untested [@S-P2T18-005; @S-P2T18-007]. One count conflict is adjudicated in the ledger: Greyson's four Parnia out-of-body cases conflict with Parnia's explicit report of none, so the exchange's twelve-case effective-trial denominator is withheld. Both authors locate part of the disagreement in antecedent probabilities; neither author's estimate becomes a project prior.

<!-- id: c06-p040 -->
The dependence audit is what disciplines any recurrence argument built on this corpus. Charland-Verville's historical comparison table pools 42 prospective reports — 27 from Greyson's 2003 study, 11 from Schwaninger's 2002 study, 4 from Parnia 2001 — which is documented analytic reuse, not new recruitment [@S-T08-005]; its broader figure pools 429 retrospective and 47 prospective reports across published datasets whose legend names Greyson 1990 and Parnia 2001, labeling the current study 2013 although the article appeared in 2014 — a legend-year mismatch that establishes no separate 2013 sample — with actual participant overlap between separately recruited cohorts unknown. Its relabeling of Parnia's two bodily-awareness responses as out-of-body experiences is a scale-category use the original prose contradicts; the record qualifies the label without altering any published number. Heim's later citations and translation are transmissions of one corpus, not new samples. The recorded conclusion is nonadditive by construction: recurrence across documents, cultures and centuries supports investigating a shared reported phenomenon, and no pooled independent sample count, global denominator or numerical metaphysical likelihood survives the audit.

## The evidential asymmetry

<!-- id: c06-p041 -->
The asymmetry the article states, this chapter now grounds record by record. On one side, controlled stimulation provides positive evidence of neural influence — on reported perceptual content in one human case, and on behavioral arousal and access markers, with awareness inferred from signatures, in two macaques — within stated scopes and with dependence structures that survive audit. On the other, the crisis material provides reports requiring explanation and possibilities worth testing, but in no inspected case does the conjunction hold: nowhere are externally verified content, an independently fixed acquisition interval and adequate process information established together. The prospective studies define populations and deploy targets without producing a single validated concealed-target success or a confirmed opportunity denominator; the celebrated retrospective cases rest on single reporting chains whose decisive measurements were never made or never preserved. This is not a proof that every account has an ordinary cause, and a negative target result establishes no impossibility. It is a statement about which side currently possesses discriminating observations.

<!-- id: c06-p042 -->
The dependence structure gives the asymmetry its sensitivity form, inherited from the article and confirmed in the ledger. Removing one celebrated retrospective case — Reynolds, say, or the dentures anecdote, each of which collapses to a single witness chain under audit — leaves the prospective questions intact; removing all retrospective narratives reduces both the favorable recurrence arguments and the cultural countercomparisons, since the same collections supply both. Anecdotal accumulation therefore cannot do the work of a discriminating observation: retellings, translations and analytic reuse multiply publications without multiplying observations, and the ledger's dependency groups make that double-counting visible. What survives sensitivity analysis on the positive side is bounded and worth stating: recurring reported experiences across prospective, retrospective and historical settings are a documented phenomenon warranting continued investigation with better-controlled designs — a conclusion about research value, not about mechanism or metaphysics.

## Dependence accounting, access gaps and non-conclusions

<!-- id: c06-p043 -->
The dependence accounting for this chapter is the project's largest, and its rule is one group per underlying evidence chain. Each intervention study carries its own group — the Cogitate protocol-and-critique chain, the macaque study, the shared anesthesia-sleep cohort, the single-patient stimulation case — and their cross-study inference is recorded separately. Each case dossier is one narrator-rooted group: the Neal river and ICU episodes share one chain; the Alexander illness, recovery episode and every defender and critic text about it share one; the Reynolds event and its entire exchange share one; the dentures event and its Dutch document set share one, with a contextual — never additive — link to the Dutch cohort. The prospective studies keep their study-level sample groups — AWARE I, AWARE II with its article-linked ancillary narratives, and the Dutch cohort — while the Phase 2 reinspection and correspondence material forms a separate overlay group across them, adding no participants. The historical and cross-cultural corpus divides into the Bede documents, the Heim transmission chain, the India collection, the Japan reanalysis, the pooled IANDS-descended retrospective collections and the Parnia-comparison exchange. Nothing in this chapter sums across a group, and the reused Parnia reports appear in three collections while counting once.

<!-- id: c06-p044 -->
The access gaps are declared rather than smoothed, because several of them are decision-relevant. No raw clinical chart, EEG tracing, operative record or original interview recording was inspected for any case dossier; no private-record requests were authorized, and their absence is an access limit, not evidence of absence. Originals that remain uninspected include the Sabom 1998 chapter at the root of the Reynolds chain, the 1991 Meijers article and full 1994 interview transcript behind the dentures chain, the published Noyes-Kletti translation of Heim, the original 2011 Neal edition, the 2012 AANS Alexander text, the AWARE II supplement, and the complete OSF version history behind the adversarial collaboration; the Phase 2 dentures reinspection reopened only the first page of Woerlee's 2010 response, leaving later-page comparison a declared gap against the earlier task's historical inspection. Both user-supplied transcripts remain audio-unverified. A bounded forward search found no later consortium reply to the 2025 theory-test critique; that absence is a search result, not a literature fact. Where a percentage appears without recoverable integers — the anesthesia and sleep interview rates, the Dutch 74% — the missing numbers are recorded as nulls.

<!-- id: c06-p045 -->
What this chapter cannot conclude, it now states. It cannot conclude that consciousness is brain-independent: no inspected crisis case establishes the conjunction that claim requires, and the strongest dossiers reduce to single uncorroborated reporting chains under audit. It cannot conclude that any particular ordinary mechanism produced any particular report: the critics' accounts remain case-unmeasured possibilities, and the symmetric standard withholds both verdicts. It cannot convert controlled neural dependence into physicalism, an identity thesis or a refutation of every transmission model — the intervention stream constrains all surviving models without selecting one — and it cannot convert any theory-test failure into support for survival. It cannot upgrade reported experiences into observed events, recovery into miracle, subjective timelessness into ontological timelessness, or recurrence into mechanism. No component of the fundamental-consciousness package — fundamentality, universal subjecthood, brain independence, survival beyond irreversible death, timelessness, preservation, intention, goodness, moral authority — receives support from this chapter, and all twelve withheld stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld through it.
