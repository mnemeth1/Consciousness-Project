<!-- id: c06-p001 -->
The two chapters before this one weighed the philosophical routes to a conscious foundation. We now turn to the empirical material, taken over from the article's fifth section. Two questions organize it. What do controlled neural interventions establish about the dependence of experience on brain activity? And what can crisis-experience reports, the near-death and resuscitation material, show and not show? Everything in this chapter turns on one standard, and it is a conjunctive one. A crisis report discriminates between rival accounts of mind only when three things are established together: externally accurate content, a defensible interval of acquisition, and adequate information about the processes available during that interval. Four case dossiers, three prospective studies, a historical and cross-cultural corpus, and their chains of critic and reply are all measured against that standard. The chapter closes with the evidential asymmetry between the two streams, the dependence accounting, and a plain statement of what none of this material concludes.

<!-- id: c06-p002 -->
The caution that governed the previous chapters governs this one too, with some additions that empirical material requires. Every clinical number, timing estimate and description of experience in this chapter is an attribution to an identified inspected version. None of it is a measurement this project verified. Two of the case dossiers rest partly on user-supplied purported transcripts whose audio, speaker identity and timestamps were never verified. Two rest partly on converted ebooks whose conversion metadata postdates publication. Several rest on current webpages that are not authenticated snapshots of their original wording. The distinction to hold onto is simple: a verified report is not a verified event. Each narrator's crisis and recovery episodes form one reporting dependency group, so retellings, translations and republications never add observations. Counts keep their units throughout. People, resuscitation episodes, interviews, EEG images and analysis windows are not interchangeable. The dependency structure itself is set out in the closing section.

## Controlled interventions and their scope

<!-- id: c06-p003 -->
The article asks for discrimination, not mere covariation, and the cleanest intervention case in the record supplies it, at the scale of a single patient. In Parvizi and colleagues' 2012 study, electrical stimulation of a right fusiform face-selective region pair in one 45-year-old epilepsy patient produced reported face distortions on seven real-face trials. Four sham trials and three trials stimulating a nearby control pair produced none [@S-T09-004]. That is more than correlation gives us. The sham and control contrasts support a causal contribution within this case. The limits deserve equal care, and the ledger states them. Nonspecific nonface changes also occurred. The two stimulated regions were not separated, and current can affect connected sites. The small famous-face and place naming tests differed in procedure, so their null differences support no conclusion of equivalence. Randomization and assessor masking were not established. A 2013 correction changes only an author name [@S-T09-005]. One patient's repeated trials are not independent people, and no unitary face center follows from them. The question of local versus network remains open.

<!-- id: c06-p004 -->
Tasserie and colleagues' 2022 macaque study carries the intervention evidence from content to state. Under maintained propofol anesthesia, high central-thalamic deep brain stimulation raised the reported behavioral arousal score from 0 of 11 to 9 of 11 in both stimulated animals. Low central-thalamic stimulation reached 3 of 11, and ventrolateral control stimulation stayed at 0 of 11; awake animals score 11 of 11 [@S-T09-002]. Stimulation also shifted fMRI dynamics and enhanced hierarchical auditory responses. The qualifications here are structural rather than incidental. The intervention unit is two animals, with three separate awake comparators and reused awake auditory data. Blinding and a randomized order of conditions were not established in the inspected text. Subjective awareness is inferred from signatures, since macaques give no trialwise reports. The auditory paradigm had no ventrolateral control arm. And the supplementary methods disclose that each animal's high stimulation setting was chosen empirically, just above its own behavioral arousal threshold, which limits any reading of the result as an unselected dose-response estimate [@S-P2T19-002]. One more caution about units. Per-run volume counts and overlapping analysis windows are not run totals by animal and condition, and those totals were not located in the inspected extents.

<!-- id: c06-p005 -->
Does an unresponsive patient have no experience? Scheinin and colleagues' anesthesia-and-sleep study, published in 2021, is the record's sharpest warning against assuming so. Forty healthy young men were recruited. Of these, 39 underwent randomized open-label propofol (19) or dexmedetomidine (20) anesthesia, and 37 returned for a sleep arm on average eighteen weeks later. These are overlapping arms, not 76 participants [@S-T09-003]. Among interviewable states, subjective experience was reported in 80% of anesthesia interviews and 71% of sleep interviews, including contentless impressions. Two cautions apply. These are interview rates, not population prevalence, and the exact integer numerators and denominators behind the percentages are not supplied in the inspected main text. A targeted Phase 2 search of the study's extended-data appendices found regional PET cluster tables rather than interview-count tables, so the integers remain unrecovered. The paper's PET contrasts locate state-linked changes in thalamic, cingulate and angular blood flow. Those are associations within manipulated states, not tests of focal necessity. And the authors themselves expressly note that absent response, or absent recalled content, cannot conclusively establish absent experience.

<!-- id: c06-p006 -->
The Cogitate adversarial collaboration, whose main results appeared in Nature in 2025, is the largest theory-level test in the record. The consortium recruited 256 participants across fMRI (120), MEG (102) and intracranial EEG (34), and retained 108, 97 and 32. The main noninvasive tests used 73 held-out fMRI and 65 held-out MEG datasets, while the invasive data were not split [@S-T09-001]. Suprathreshold visual stimuli in four categories at three durations were tested under varying task relevance. The design did not directly contrast conscious with unconscious processing, and it did not perturb candidate regions. The operational predictions set posterior against prefrontal decoding, posterior duration tracking against prefrontal onset/offset ignition, and posterior against long-range prefrontal synchrony. The outcome is mixed. Sustained posterior representations were partly supported, and transient prefrontal category decoding appeared. But the predicted prefrontal offset profile and sustained posterior gamma synchrony were not established, and the preregistered pairwise-phase-consistency analysis supported neither critical connectivity prediction [@S-T09-008]. An exploratory amplitude-based dynamic functional connectivity analysis partly supported the workspace theory's expectation, while still lacking sustained posterior connectivity. Exploratory it remains; it cannot be relabeled a preregistered confirmation. No overall winner among the theories follows.

<!-- id: c06-p007 -->
The Phase 2 protocol audit qualifies the confirmatory weight of those results. It does not erase them. The selected OSF amendment file is named v4 but internally identified as Version 4.5 and dated 22 December 2022. It already specifies a one-third optimization split and unsplit intracranial data, whereas the published protocol of February 2023 contains different half-split wording. The contrast cannot date the change. The node is not a certified immutable preregistration, and neither the earlier amendment chain nor the analysts' data-access history was audited [@S-P2T19-001]. The amendment is also internally inconsistent about fMRI counts (110 quality-passed datasets alongside a 36/80 division), while the final paper uses 35 optimization and 73 held-out datasets, and its supplement explains the discrepancy only partly. The supplement declares further departures from the analysis plan. Representational similarity analysis used all trial pairs rather than the planned split sets. The main fMRI connectivity analysis pooled task relevance. The multiple-comparison method changed. Synchrony-channel eligibility was relaxed relative to the amendment's active windows. Three below-threshold intracranial patients were retained as a declared deviation. We should be fair on both sides here: reporting a departure neither proves outcome-driven bias nor makes the departure go away.

<!-- id: c06-p008 -->
The chain of replies is handled with the same discipline. Naccache and colleagues' 2025 critique argues that attended offset and continuing awareness were not established, that macroscopic prefrontal decoding may miss mixed selectivity, and that activity-silent storage is not obligatory for workspace theory [@S-T09-006]. These are verified as documented arguments, not as measured inattention, and two qualifications cut both ways. First, the 2023 protocol itself conditions a workspace offset response on conscious detection in its overview, while elsewhere treating onset and offset as tested expectations. So the offset objection was not entirely post hoc, and the failed test was not unconditional either [@S-T09-007]. Second, the critique's page-2 wording lists category among the allegedly absent prefrontal decodings, although the Nature results report transient prefrontal category decoding. That is a local wording problem, not a substantive correction. The theory proponents' replies in the supplement are contemporaneous interpretations: coverage, frequency choice and region-criteria explanations from the integrated-information side, and the consortium's own statement that shared successful predictions do not uniquely confirm either theory [@S-T09-008]. A bounded forward search found no later consortium rebuttal to the critique. Absence of discovery is not absence of reply.

<!-- id: c06-p009 -->
What, then, does the intervention stream establish? Causal contribution, within the inspected record. Site-dependent stimulation changes reported perceptual content in a human patient, and state-targeted stimulation restores arousal and access markers in anesthetized animals, under controls that make passive correlation an incomplete description. Any model that denies a neural contribution to embodied experience is thereby challenged. What of the transmission reply? The article's treatment of it is adopted here. A theorist who holds that altering an interface alters what appears through it cannot be refuted by logic alone. But bare compatibility is a weak explanatory achievement. The alternative must explain why this intervention changes this content, and a metaphor of reception supplies no such relation. What the stream does not establish must be stated just as plainly. It establishes no unique universally necessary and sufficient neural mechanism, no physicalist identity, and no adjudication among identity, realization, interface and idealist accounts that spell out the dependence explicitly. Neural dependence in the tested contexts is a constraint every surviving model must carry. It is not a verdict on ontology.

## The conjunctive standard for crisis reports

<!-- id: c06-p010 -->
Why do crisis reports enter the record at all? Because of what they would show if a demanding conjunction held. Accurate information, acquired under conditions that exclude the relevant ordinary processes, would put pressure on a specified neural account. The operative evidence is conjunctive. It requires externally accurate content, a defensible interval of acquisition, and adequate information about the processes available during that interval, all three together. The article's framing, applied throughout this chapter, is that a striking report with uncertain timing does not have the same discriminatory force, and that a physiological interval does not establish when remembered content was formed. The point is this. What the cases below cannot independently demonstrate is a retrospective memory that accurately dates its own content to an excluded interval. Every dossier's positive argument is conditional on that bridge.

<!-- id: c06-p011 -->
The standard cuts both ways, and this chapter holds it to both. A proposed ordinary mechanism, whether restored circulation, residual hearing, sedation-shaped dreaming or later reconstruction, also needs case-specific grounds before it counts as the demonstrated explanation. A plausible possibility is not a diagnosis. The cross-case inference recorded for the Reynolds and dentures dossiers states the first direction. Those two dossiers support specific, traceable perception claims and substantive disputes. But they lack the independently fixed perception timing, together with complete information-access controls, that would be needed to identify extracerebral acquisition. The other two dossiers carry their own packet conclusions. The Neal material supports attributed experience and a conditional argument, not survival. The Alexander documentation supports richer attributions without independently fixing all recalled stages to an interval that excludes relevant ordinary acquisition. Now the other direction. In no dossier has a critic's mechanism been demonstrated as the actual cause of any particular report. Both failures matter, and neither makes up for the other.

## Case dossiers: Neal, Alexander, Reynolds, dentures/TG

<!-- id: c06-p012 -->
The Neal dossier concerns a reported 1999 kayak entrapment on a Chilean river and a later intensive-care episode. Both reach the record through one narrator's reporting chain. Three layers were inspected. The first is a publisher-hosted author interview of unknown date [@S-T04-001]. The second is a user-supplied purported talk transcript: its 1,210 cues were fully read, but its audio identity and timing were never verified [@S-T04-009]. The third is a converted WaterBrook ebook of the memoir, eISBN 9780984819218, copyright 2011/2012, version 3.1, with 2019 conversion metadata and no print-page equivalence. The original 2011 edition was identified in catalogs but never inspected [@S-P2T14-009]. The two reported episodes, river and ICU, carry their own case identifiers inside a single narrator dependence group. One further detail deserves notice. The memoir's own Q&A states that the first edition did not clearly identify Jesus. That is a self-reported change, and no independent collation against the original 2011 text was made.

<!-- id: c06-p013 -->
The reported experience is specific and internally articulated. The transcript and memoir describe calm surrender during the entrapment, an apparent external vantage on the rescue, intermittent returns to breathe, and a seeming simultaneity that sits alongside a narrated sequence [@S-T04-009]. The memoir names four rescuers and reports that the author later interviewed each of them individually while preparing the book. It also narrates bodily sensations during release, gaps in physical memory, and a later hospital encounter told across two chapters [@S-P2T14-009]. All of this is treated in one way. These are attributed experiences, and their external accuracy is not thereby established. Named witnesses and a reported interview method make the account more specific, it must be said. But no separately authored witness statement, interview transcript or information-exposure history was inspected, and the witnesses' thoughts reach the record only through the narrator. Nor does altered temporal phenomenology, including the report of hours of experience, establish an intrinsically nonsuccessive consciousness.

<!-- id: c06-p014 -->
Can elapsed time do physiological work here? The audit of the rescue interval shows why it cannot. The memoir gives eleven to fourteen minutes from repeated headcounts to CPR, timed on a named rescuer's watch. The transcript instead gives fifteen minutes, counted from the moment rescuers inferred her location. A 2013 CNN report explicitly calls its thirty-minute oxygen interval an estimate. And a whitewater accident database's compiled description asserts thirty minutes underwater while its injury field says near-drowning [@S-P2T14-008; @S-P2T14-002]. The sources also conflict on the accident date (January 14 versus February 3, 1999) and on the age of a family member, and no original timekeeper account resolves them. The conclusion is deliberately limited. None of these intervals, whether estimated, watch-timed or compiled, measures circulation, oxygen delivery or neural activity. The memoir's later saturation figures concern hospital care, not a timed neural trace from the river. And survival, with reported testing showing no brain damage, is a narrated outcome, not a chart diagnosis [@S-P2T14-009].

<!-- id: c06-p015 -->
The foreknowledge claim gets the most exacting chronology in the dossier, and it needs it. The narrator reports learning during the crisis that her oldest son would die. Dated newspaper reporting establishes his death in a roller-ski collision on June 21, 2009, and the complete inspected article discloses no earlier prediction. That is a bounded content check, not proof that no prediction was made elsewhere [@S-P2T14-003]. Every inspected disclosure of the prediction postdates the fulfillment. The memoir narrates early confidants and a manuscript saved before news of the death. But its own draft labels conflict. The body describes a spring 2009 first draft, revised and saved on June 21, while a Q&A calls the manuscript written hours before the death the first complete draft, and the transcript's wording does not explicitly state same-day completion [@S-P2T14-009; @S-T04-009]. The same memoir separately attributes to the son a childhood belief that he would not reach eighteen, and then narrates his life past eighteen with a changed-plan interpretation. Read literally, that is a counterexample the memoir itself supplies. So we have no independently dated prefulfillment record, no fixed deadline, cause or scoring set, and no denominator of all predictions made. What survives is attributed prediction and a documented reporting chronology. Verified foreknowledge, and any odds, are withheld.

<!-- id: c06-p016 -->
The dossier has a chain of critic and reply. It runs through journalism, but it is real. A 2016 National Geographic feature reports Nelson accepting the intensity of Neal's memory while challenging its survival interpretation and proposing REM intrusion related to hypoxia [@S-P2T14-004]. Barker's published response (Barker is a different experiencer, not Neal) argues that physiological correlates could accompany a spirit's departure and need not settle the interpretation [@S-P2T14-005]. The exchange should be kept in proportion. The critic's mechanism is a possibility, not an established cause of the case. The reply blocks an automatic dismissal, but it supplies none of the missing measurements. And the packet's strongest positive argument remains conditional: worth exact documentation, supporting attributed experience, and not establishing survival. The available testimony cannot fix the experience to a measured absence of circulation and relevant neural function. The possible ordinary explanations remain equally unconfirmed for this case.

<!-- id: c06-p017 -->
The Alexander dossier concerns a 2008 Escherichia coli meningitis with a reported onset of November 10, 2008. It is the most heavily documented single case in the record. Several layers were inspected. There is a user-supplied purported talk transcript, audio unverified [@S-T06-001]. There is a converted 2012 ebook of the memoir with 2023 conversion metadata, which discloses changed names and family plus professional editorial assistance [@S-T06-006]. There are current webpages: Lam's research report, bearing an October 11, 2011 publication label with an April 2014 update [@S-P2T15-003]; a Skeptiko interview transcript whose November 22, 2011 date is archive-search metadata only [@S-P2T15-002]; and the author's current account page [@S-T06-005]. None of these webpages is authenticated as original historical wording. There is a treating physician's separately authored signed statement. And there is a 2018 external clinical review. One fact about the memoir governs how all of this is read. The author says he reconstructed that week's ordinary hospital events from others, because he remembers none of them. The published dialogue is therefore reconstruction, not witness transcripts.

<!-- id: c06-p018 -->
Let us give the dossier's argument its due, because in form it is strong. Claimed global cortical incapacity, joined to ultra-real and intricate experience, is offered to exclude brain production, and the memoir's appendix rejects nine alternative hypotheses principally on those two grounds [@S-T06-006]. The qualifications that matter most are the argument's own, more than any critic's. The appendix's sixth item says that full-thickness cortical destruction is unnecessary for its functional-disruption argument; but then that argument cannot confirm the subtitle-level claim of demonstrated destruction across eight lobes. The memoir allows deeper housekeeping function while arguing neocortical nonfunction. And the talk transcript reports a proposed brain-death assessment as a next step, not a completed diagnosis. These are the author's premises and the author's interpretation. Neither credentials nor felt certainty validates them, and the claimed cortical exclusion is asserted, not measured.

<!-- id: c06-p019 -->
The clinical documentation is stronger than the memoir alone, and it still falls short of the standard. Wade's signed appendix attests acute E. coli meningitis, prompt antibiotics, coma with ventilation, day-7 awakening and remarkable recovery [@S-P2T15-004]. It is a treating physician's separately authored statement, not a raw chart, and its mortality figures (including over 97% after prolonged coma) come without denominators or a calculation model. The 2018 external reviewers report a review of the original records, records that this project has not itself inspected. They describe severe meningitis, an admission Glasgow Coma Scale of 11 with subsequent low scores, sedation, and, decisively for this chapter, no EEG [@S-T06-002]. The documented accounts do not agree with one another. The 2018 paper places extubation on day 6 and improvement on day 7, where the memoir, the physician statement and the current page place awakening on day 7. Admission GCS 11 stands against the memoir's and a defender's 8. Laboratory timing differs across tellings. These are kept as version limits. No attempt is made to harmonize them.

<!-- id: c06-p020 -->
The positive weight of the dossier rests on its timing anchors and on the photograph episode. Both are retrospective. The 2018 paper reports disclosure on day 9, retrospective corroboration of praying visitors reportedly present on day 5, and an inferred experience interval within days 1–5. It supplies no original interview dates and no validated exclusion of ordinary acquisition [@S-T06-002]. The memoir itself identifies its principal temporal anchors as one absent-person report and six recalled faces, and it explicitly calls any other simultaneity conjectural [@S-T06-006]. The photograph episode is the strongest single claim. The author knew before the coma that a birth sister had died (2000) and had reunited with birth relatives (2007). The memoir places receipt of her photograph four months after discharge, with recognition the following morning during a reading of an analogous case. What is missing? A blinded comparison, a dated description made before the photograph, and an independently dated 2009 document: none of these exists in the record. A defender's 2013 interview testimony that a prior description matched the photograph is retrospective, unblinded, and corroboration from within the family circle [@S-T06-004]. The reported six-week account of more than 20,000 words, written before the author read the NDE literature, rests on later statements. No dated manuscript was inspected.

<!-- id: c06-p021 -->
Where does the dispute end? In documented underdetermination. A 2013 Esquire article reports the attending physician describing chemical sedation and delirious responsiveness, which challenges the premise of a wholly nonfunctioning brain [@S-T06-003]. A defender's long reply disputes the context of that portrayal and prints an attributed objection from the same physician, relayed through family and publisher; her nonresponse to direct calls is recorded [@S-T06-004]. The 2018 reviewers explicitly leave the relative contributions of infection and sedation undetermined, while arguing against a solely drug-induced account [@S-T06-002]. Shermer's published criticism adds the general argument that explanatory gaps and vivid experience do not establish supernatural causes [@S-T06-008]. The verdict on the whole dossier is the audit's verdict. The combined documentation supports richer attributions than the memoir alone. It does not independently fix all recalled stages to an interval that excludes relevant ordinary acquisition. Specific ordinary and nonordinary causes remain underdetermined, and that licenses neither dismissal nor the memoir's conclusion of survival and a creator.

<!-- id: c06-p022 -->
The Reynolds dossier concerns a reported August 1991 aneurysm operation under hypothermic circulatory arrest. It reaches the record almost entirely through Sabom's 1998 account, and the relevant chapter of that account remains uninspected; we have catalog metadata only [@S-P2T16-001]. Around that inaccessible original stands an extended chain of critic and reply. Woerlee's 2011 investigation develops an ordinary-hearing account, awareness plus drug effects, with technical detail supplemented from related surgical protocols because Sabom's account is nontechnical [@S-T19-005]. Hameroff's response disclaims case expertise, conditionally permits awareness under anesthesia, and challenges the missing brainstem-evoked-response results [@S-T19-006]. Carter's response supplies a chronology through Sabom. It places the externally checkable perceptions before the recorded absence of brainstem responses, while the reported episode of relatives and light is not independently timed. It also quotes the monitoring technologist, who locates himself in the adjacent operating room and asks for an experiment at the exact parameters [@S-T19-007]. The article's verdict on this exchange is adopted here. The disputes over auditory access, operative phases and monitoring are consequential for a simple reason: specific reported details invite explanation. And general protocols cannot stand in for the actual protocol of the case.

<!-- id: c06-p023 -->
The later exchange narrows the claims on both sides. It decides nothing. Woerlee's rejoinder accepts the distinction between a measurable brainstem auditory response and conscious hearing. It hypothesizes that a different monitoring modality was likely used, despite its absence from the cited surgical article. And, decisively for the record, it explicitly leaves the immaterial explanation unexcluded, because the case data are insufficient [@S-P2T16-002]. His inference that the patient plausibly felt neither pain nor anxiety rests on separate reported symptom percentages. Carter's further reply objects that margins do not establish their intersection. The arithmetic bound recorded for that objection runs as follows: with 25 awareness descriptions, pain reported by 7 and anxiety by 9, those reporting neither can range from 9 to 16. But that bound is conditional on quoted margins from a reproduced study of other patients, not a Reynolds sample [@S-P2T16-004]. Smit's letter reports actually performing the prescribed consumer sound demonstration. The clicks were intolerable, yet ambient sounds remained audible, and speech worsened with tighter headphones; two quoted third-party reports add partial conversation and clear television speech under differing setups [@S-P2T16-003]. Smit draws a further inference: unbearable clicks would have been remembered, so their absence supports nonphysical hearing. That is kept as an argument, with the recorded limitation that absence from retellings proves neither an inability to hear nor actual nonphysical acquisition.

<!-- id: c06-p024 -->
Set against the conjunctive standard, the Reynolds exchange illustrates the article's point: such debates narrow the causal alternatives without adding a single observation. One patient, one event, one reporting chain through one investigator. Repeated publications are reuse, not replication. Three evidential states must be kept apart here: the proposed institutional hearing test, Smit's performed self-demonstration, and an actual calibrated experiment equivalent to the case. Only the middle one exists in the record. A later webpage titled as a successful test remains uninspected after a stopped access route, and its title supplies no outcome [@S-P2T16-005]. Skepticism about the exclusion claim does not establish that the ordinary-hearing mechanism actually produced every reported detail. Objections to that mechanism do not lock the experience to an interval that excludes all relevant processing. What limits the survival inference is not the strangeness of the narrative. It is the unresolved conjunction: the original charts and traces are missing, the prefeedback accuracy record is missing, and no complete calibrated experiment exists.

<!-- id: c06-p025 -->
The dentures dossier concerns a late-1970s Dutch resuscitation. A patient reportedly later identified the nurse who had removed his dentures and described an unusual cart. Of all the dossiers, this one has the longest documentation chain and the narrowest witness base. The chain begins with a 1991 nursing-journal article (identified, uninspected). It passes through an Addink interview with nurse TG on February 2, 1994, whose transcript is dated February 3; only translated excerpts were available, and the full unpublished twelve-page transcript was never recovered. Then comes Smit's 2008 journal article [@S-T19-002]. Then Rivas's detailed 2008 follow-up interview, published as a nine-page summary with selected literal passages from a record of more than thirty A4 pages [@S-P2T17-003]. Then the Winter 2008 set of critic and reply, and finally the 2010 rejoinder, which states that the official medical records were destroyed [@S-T19-004]. Every substantive detail about the patient reaches the record through TG. The intermediary between TG and the 1991 author reported the story without taking part in the resuscitation, and no investigator ever interviewed the patient. Even the year of the event is an inference. It is given as probably 1979 in the Dutch comparison, and a translator's introduction states it more definitely than the body warrants.

<!-- id: c06-p026 -->
The content and its corrections show a single witness's account under pressure, and it is kept here without harmonization. TG describes a converted cart with a flat wooden pull-out shelf already extended, and attributes a matching description to the patient. That detail specifically challenges an explanation based on hearing a metal drawer. Yet the Dutch interview article itself contains a colloquial drawer word, alongside the television caption the defenders blame for it [@S-P2T17-003]. The published interview contains a pump-first passage in a prompted exchange about physiology. TG's direct Winter 2008 reply explicitly corrects the sequence: the dentures were removed before the mechanical compression device was switched on. The same reply estimates more than five minutes of transport without cardiac massage. These are late estimates by the same witness. They do not exclude ambulance or bystander resuscitation before the device was connected, and a later translation weakens their Dutch no-massage wording [@S-P2T17-005]. The reported experience has two phases, pain with a wish to stop and a later fear that resuscitation would be abandoned, and the patient reportedly could not locate the experience's beginning or end. Both the original passages and the corrections are retrospective statements of one witness. Neither resolves into a contemporaneous clock.

<!-- id: c06-p027 -->
The critic's account and the audit's verdict close the dossier. Woerlee's Dutch criticism, which expressly corrects his own earlier assumptions made from sparse information, proposes hypothermia, CPR-restored awareness, sensation, hearing and reconstruction as an ordinary account [@S-P2T17-004]. The defenders' 2010 rejoinder contests the pump timing, the question of felt versus seen removal, and the auditory reconstruction; it uses flat-EEG language while supplying no tracing; and it proposes a ten-participant blindfold experiment that was never performed [@S-T19-004]. The limits on inference run in both directions. A plausible ordinary account is not a demonstrated explanation of the whole event. Rejecting the metal-drawer mechanism, or accepting TG's sincerity, does not establish extracerebral perception. The ledger states the dependency sensitivity plainly: remove the retrospective testimony that depends on TG and the packet's entire positive contribution goes with it, and no number of publications or translations restores independence. Publication alongside the Dutch prospective study creates no roster membership in that cohort. The anecdote remains one retrospective case, contextually linked and never summed.

## Prospective studies: AWARE, Dutch cohort, denominator discipline

<!-- id: c06-p028 -->
AWARE I, published in 2014 from fifteen hospitals, sets the prospective baseline, and it also shows the frictions in its arithmetic. The study reports 2,060 recorded arrest events and 330 discharge survivors. It reports 140 completed stage-one interviews, yet the same layout prints 52 in-hospital plus 90 post-discharge interviews and a table denominator of 142 [@S-T07-001]. That discrepancy is unresolved, and the later AWARE II introduction compounds it by citing 143, a figure it labels as survivors of the earlier study [@S-T07-007]. Of 101 stage-two interviewees, 46 reported no memory, 46 reported non-NDE memories, and 9 met the Greyson threshold, including two with auditory or visual recall of the event. Both event-recall cases occurred at locations without the study's concealed target shelves, and only one could be followed up, since the other's health was failing. The authors report medical-record corroboration for one case and infer roughly three minutes of awareness from a defibrillator sequence. The units of that inference must be kept apart. Awareness lasting up to three minutes and an arrest lasting at least three minutes are different quantities. And no participant-linked neural recording accompanies any reported content.

<!-- id: c06-p029 -->
The Dutch prospective study, published in 2001 from ten hospitals, contributes the largest early cohort and a lesson in version discipline. It reports 344 consecutively resuscitated patients across 509 resuscitation episodes; people and episodes are different units. It reports 62 broad-definition NDE reports (18%) and 41 core reports (12%). A printed figure of 248 interviewed within five days (74%) lacks a clear matching denominator, and none is manufactured here [@S-T07-004]. The 2002 published correction, inspected directly, changes one drug name in one sentence and changes no cohort counts or follow-up results [@S-P2T09-003]. Longitudinal reclassification moved the two-year groups from 37/37 to 35/39, with eight-year groups of 23 and 15. These are the same retained people, not independent cohorts. The authors acknowledge possible site selection and use estimated arrest timing. No participant-specific EEG during any reported experience is presented.

<!-- id: c06-p030 -->
AWARE II, published in 2023 from a multi-center design spanning 25 sites in its main phase, is the most instrumented attempt in the record. It is also the clearest demonstration of the gap between deploying a test and giving anyone the opportunity to pass it. Of 567 recruited arrest subjects, 213 had sustained return of circulation, 53 survived to discharge, 28 completed interviews, and 11 reported any memory or perception, with overlapping thematic categories and six transcendent experiences at the Greyson threshold [@S-T07-007]. Tablet-and-headphone equipment was deployed in 365 of 567 cases. But deployment is not valid delivery of a stimulus. The protocol starts visual images at one minute and auditory fruit names at five minutes after activation, and the intersections of delivered stimulus with interview are never reported. No explicit recall of any target occurred, and no implicit visual identification. One of 28 interviewees made a correct fruit choice, and the protocol's request to name any three fruits supplies no uniform chance model for it. A separate single participant's overt recollection of clinical surroundings received chart verification of a shock event, with prior knowledge of resuscitation unmeasured, as the authors acknowledge. Twenty-five discharge survivors were never interviewed.

<!-- id: c06-p031 -->
The AWARE II EEG arm changed the terms of the debate, and the same discipline about units applies to it. The study captured 851 EEG screenshot images from 85 subjects during pulse-check pauses, on a limited frontotemporal montage. Of these, 466 images from 53 subjects were interpretable, and 49 of those subjects also had cerebral oximetry. The authors' body-text summary reports near-normal or physiological rhythms up to 35 to 60 minutes into resuscitation; figure points extend beyond that summary, so the exact maximum is unresolved, and an alpha-coma interpretation could not be excluded [@S-T07-007]. Images and people are different denominators. The paper reports suppression in 47% of EEG data. That is a data-unit figure, which a later correspondent paraphrases as about half the patients; the original unit is retained here. The decisive intersection appears in the study's own flow diagram. Of the 28 interviewed survivors, only two had EEG data, and neither was among those with explicit cognitive recall. Aggregate rhythm timing cannot date the formation of any particular memory. And missing links between EEG and report do not establish absent consciousness.

<!-- id: c06-p032 -->
The published exchange over the EEG sharpens that very point, and both its force and its limits are kept. Greyson and van Lommel's letter argues that the EEG-consciousness interpretation fails for lack of overlap between recorded EEG and explicit recollections in the interviewed subset, and it questions the exclusion of artifacts [@S-P2T09-001]. Parnia's reply answers that absent explicit recall does not prove absent consciousness, invoking implicit memory, and defends the sampling, artifact-removal and expert-interpretation procedures [@S-P2T09-002]. The adjudication is symmetric. The study does not establish a positive association between its EEG findings and experience. The critics' missing association does not establish that the recorded patterns cannot be markers of consciousness. The reply succeeds against the simple negative inference, but it supplies no participant-specific content of experience, no timing, and no independent artifact audit. An unmeasured association is not a demonstrated negative association. Neither letter supplies the missing intersection.

<!-- id: c06-p033 -->
The parallel methodological exchange with Martial and colleagues gets the same treatment. Their letter challenges the EEG classification and the missing data, the renaming of the experiences, the common-fruit interpretation, and the timing exclusions [@S-T07-003]. The authors' direct reply explicitly answers Martial; it is a distinct chain from the reply to Greyson. It defends adapted EEG standards, attributes the missing time-frequency analysis to practical multichannel constraints, argues that stable clinical states cannot model the evolving physiology of arrest, and rebuts the fruit-guessing concern by citing more than 80,000 English nouns [@S-P2T09-005]. Two annotations are pointed. A practical explanation for an omitted analysis does not supply its result. And the noun-count reference class does not match the protocol's actual request to name any three fruits, so no probability calculation is licensed in either direction. The reply also advances a larger interpretive program: a disinhibition account of lucid experience near death, with its functional benefit expressly left unknown. That is kept as the authors' proposal. Its physiological and evolutionary assertions are not independently validated facts.

<!-- id: c06-p034 -->
The discipline about denominators that these studies enforce is the article's point about prospective improvement. Defined populations and awareness tests improve the setting. They do not convert every report into an effective target trial. AWARE II's community arm of 126 narratives is a selected retrospective sample, and its overlap with the prospective participants and with previously published narratives is unknown. Its companion EEG analyses in separate manuscripts must be checked for shared participants before they can count as replication [@S-T07-007]. That a target was available somewhere in a study does not establish that a particular experiencer had the relevant opportunity. Margins describing separate subsets cannot supply their missing intersection. Across all three studies, the preserved nulls (eligibility counts, exposure intersections, interview denominators) are nulls and not zeros, and no pooled rate across studies is computed anywhere in the ledger.

## Historical and cross-cultural recurrence and dependence

<!-- id: c06-p035 -->
Does the reported phenomenon predate modern publicity? The historical record shows that it does, in at least one documented medieval narrative, though under heavy literary dependence. Bede's Ecclesiastical History, composed around 731 and inspected in a 1907 revised English translation, transmits Dryhthelm's narrative of return from apparent death through a named intermediary, and dates the episode around 696. Two adjacent chapters supply fatal deathbed visions with explicit admonitory purposes [@S-T08-001]. One author, one genre, one translation. That counts as a single documentary dependence group, with no clinical status and no independent witness chain. What the material supports is narrow and real. It is a documented medieval narrative with a luminous guide, punishment scenes, a pleasant landscape, a return, and lasting behavioral change. That weakens any claim that every such motif was invented after the modern near-death literature.

<!-- id: c06-p036 -->
Heim's 1892 collection of experiences from mountaineering falls is the corpus's other historical anchor. It was inspected as a digitized German separatum, with acknowledged OCR limits and no visual verification of the Fraktur scan [@S-P2T18-001]. Heim draws on his own accidents, on survivors' oral and written reports, and on literature, and he explicitly extrapolates survivors' sensations to the dying. His qualified claim that roughly 95% experienced the described state has no recoverable denominator; his approximately thirty examples concern potentially saving actions, which is a different question. His own report of his fall locates intensified thought, subjective lengthening of time and luminous life scenes during conscious falling, expressly ending at impact. The reported half-hour of unresponsiveness afterward rests on his account of what companions said. The text also documents its own contamination pressures: additional letters arrived after newspapers covered the talk, and the editor records a contested incident. The selected published English translation from 1972 remains uninspected in its body, metadata only, so no rendering is certified [@S-P2T18-002]. This is a nineteenth-century document, not a clinical study. It is used here for dated recurrence and for nothing else.

<!-- id: c06-p037 -->
The cross-cultural comparisons contribute variation, measured unequally. Pasricha and Stevenson's 1986 India report includes 16 of 18 incidentally identified cases: ten subject interviews, five firsthand-informant cases, and one secondhand. The median investigation delay was around fourteen years. Elicitation was unequal between the Indian and American material, and a blank in the comparison table means that no instance was learned, possibly because the question was never asked [@S-T08-002]. Its recurring motifs, messengers in 12 of 16 and mistaken-identity return in 10 of 16, are descriptions of selected cases. The authors themselves argue that cultural variation proves neither belief-only production nor an afterlife. The direct critique of the Indian tunnel finding, and the strongest reply to it, were not inspected. Ohkado and Greyson's 2014 reanalysis of 22 published Japanese interviews codes heavenly places in 16 and borders in 12, with flower gardens in 13 of the 16 and rivers in 10 of the 12, against zero panoramic life reviews in the sample. It acknowledges counterexamples outside the sample. The underlying interview books were not inspected, and no independent bilingual audit exists [@S-T08-003]. Both papers propose cultural explanations without isolating culture as the sole cause. The motif differences are retained as measured in sample, and only that.

<!-- id: c06-p038 -->
The retrospective collections establish heterogeneity, and they show what selection costs. Greyson and Bush's 1992 collection of distressing experiences, fifty self-selected accounts from correspondence and a newsletter request, motivates three proposed types: familiar features experienced fearfully, void or nonexistence experiences, and hellish imagery. Its examples include persistent despair alongside transitions from fear to peace, and one account of endless succession. The paper supplies no class frequency table and no crosswalk from persons to episodes, and it discusses candidly both positive-affect scale bias and reluctance to report [@S-T08-004]. Charland-Verville and colleagues' 2014 comparison retained 190 of 215 volunteered reports, 50 from non-life-threatening events and 140 from reported coma, at a mean reporting delay of 24 ± 15 years. It found no significant differences in intensity or item frequency between the groups. That is not an equivalence result. Its cutoff wording is internally inconsistent, and its clinical statuses are medically uncontrolled self-report [@S-T08-005]. Greyson's 1990 comparison of 183 self-described experiencers with 63 deniers, recruited through a newsletter, shows the scale and the self-label pulling apart in both directions [@S-T08-006]. Voluntary recruitment, long delays and unknown invitation denominators run through all three.

<!-- id: c06-p039 -->
Parnia and colleagues' 2001 Southampton study is the corpus's early prospective anchor, and it was inspected in the exact repository journal PDF. It interviewed 63 cardiac-arrest survivors: 56 with no recall, seven with some recall, and four meeting the Greyson criterion [@S-P2T18-003]. Two participants endorsed the scale's lost-bodily-awareness item, while the prose explicitly reports no out-of-body experience and no claimed opportunity to view the study's upward-facing ceiling boards. No target identification occurred. The paper supplies no confirmed intersection of target present, arrest, interview and viewpoint from which effective trials could be counted. The Augustine-Greyson exchange over this and kindred studies is kept with its concessions on both sides. Augustine concedes that possible embellishment or witness error is not thereby demonstrated, while defending an antecedent-probability preference for ordinary explanations [@S-P2T18-004; @S-P2T18-006]. Greyson concedes that the survival evidence is not compelling and that controlled failures require serious alternative explanations, while disputing the claim that the stability of paranormal features went untested [@S-P2T18-005; @S-P2T18-007]. One conflict of counts is adjudicated in the ledger. Greyson's four Parnia out-of-body cases conflict with Parnia's explicit report of none, so the exchange's twelve-case effective-trial denominator is withheld. Both authors locate part of the disagreement in antecedent probabilities. Neither author's estimate becomes a project prior.

<!-- id: c06-p040 -->
Any recurrence argument built on this corpus has to pass the dependence audit, and the audit is what keeps it honest. Charland-Verville's historical comparison table pools 42 prospective reports: 27 from Greyson's 2003 study, 11 from Schwaninger's 2002 study, and 4 from Parnia 2001. That is documented analytic reuse, not new recruitment [@S-T08-005]. Its broader figure pools 429 retrospective and 47 prospective reports across published datasets whose legend names Greyson 1990 and Parnia 2001. The legend labels the current study 2013, although the article appeared in 2014; that mismatch of years establishes no separate 2013 sample. Actual participant overlap between the separately recruited cohorts is unknown. Its relabeling of Parnia's two bodily-awareness responses as out-of-body experiences is a use of a scale category that the original prose contradicts. The label is qualified here, and no published number is altered. Heim's later citations and translation are transmissions of one corpus, not new samples. The conclusion is nonadditive by construction. Recurrence across documents, cultures and centuries supports investigating a shared reported phenomenon. No pooled independent sample count, no global denominator and no numerical metaphysical likelihood survives the audit.

## The evidential asymmetry

<!-- id: c06-p041 -->
The article states an asymmetry. This chapter has now grounded it record by record. On one side, controlled stimulation provides positive evidence of neural influence: on reported perceptual content in one human case, and on behavioral arousal and access markers in two macaques, with awareness inferred from signatures. That evidence stands within stated scopes, and its dependence structures survive audit. On the other side, the crisis material provides reports that require explanation and possibilities worth testing. But in no inspected case does the conjunction hold. Nowhere are externally verified content, an independently fixed interval of acquisition, and adequate process information established together. The prospective studies define populations and deploy targets without producing a single validated concealed-target success or a confirmed opportunity denominator. The celebrated retrospective cases rest on single reporting chains whose decisive measurements were never made or never preserved. Let me be careful about what this asymmetry is. It is not a proof that every account has an ordinary cause, and a negative target result establishes no impossibility. It is a statement about which side currently possesses discriminating observations.

<!-- id: c06-p042 -->
The dependence structure gives the asymmetry its sensitivity form. The form comes from the article, and the ledger confirms it. Remove one celebrated retrospective case, Reynolds, say, or the dentures anecdote, each of which collapses to a single witness chain under audit, and the prospective questions remain intact. Remove all the retrospective narratives, and both the favorable recurrence arguments and the cultural countercomparisons shrink, since the same collections supply both. So anecdotes cannot accumulate into a discriminating observation. Retellings, translations and analytic reuse multiply publications without multiplying observations, and the ledger's dependency groups make that double-counting visible. What survives sensitivity analysis on the positive side is bounded, and it is worth stating. Recurring reported experiences across prospective, retrospective and historical settings are a documented phenomenon, and they warrant continued investigation with better-controlled designs. That is a conclusion about research value. It is not a conclusion about mechanism or metaphysics.

## Dependence accounting, access gaps and non-conclusions

<!-- id: c06-p043 -->
The dependence accounting for this chapter is the largest in the project, and its rule is simple: one group per underlying chain of evidence. Each intervention study carries its own group. These are the Cogitate protocol-and-critique chain, the macaque study, the shared anesthesia-sleep cohort, and the single-patient stimulation case; their cross-study inference is recorded separately. Each case dossier is one group rooted in one narrator. The Neal river and ICU episodes share one chain. The Alexander illness, the recovery episode and every defender and critic text about it share one. The Reynolds event and its entire exchange share one. The dentures event and its Dutch document set share one, with a link to the Dutch cohort that is contextual and never additive. The prospective studies keep their study-level sample groups, namely AWARE I, AWARE II with its article-linked ancillary narratives, and the Dutch cohort. The Phase 2 reinspection and correspondence material forms a separate overlay group across them, and it adds no participants. The historical and cross-cultural corpus divides into the Bede documents, the Heim transmission chain, the India collection, the Japan reanalysis, the pooled IANDS-descended retrospective collections, and the Parnia-comparison exchange. Nothing in this chapter sums across a group. The reused Parnia reports appear in three collections and count once.

<!-- id: c06-p044 -->
The access gaps are declared rather than smoothed over, because several of them are relevant to decisions. No raw clinical chart, EEG tracing, operative record or original interview recording was inspected for any case dossier. No requests for private records were authorized, and their absence is an access limit, not evidence of absence. Which originals remain uninspected? The Sabom 1998 chapter at the root of the Reynolds chain. The 1991 Meijers article and the full 1994 interview transcript behind the dentures chain. The published Noyes-Kletti translation of Heim. The original 2011 Neal edition, the 2012 AANS Alexander text, the AWARE II supplement, and the complete OSF version history behind the adversarial collaboration. The other early prospective originals, the feasibility and oximetry studies, and the comparator citations, all outside the selected subset. The Phase 2 dentures reinspection reopened only the first page of Woerlee's 2010 response, so comparison of the later pages against the earlier task's historical inspection is a declared gap. Both user-supplied transcripts remain audio-unverified. A bounded forward search found no later consortium reply to the 2025 theory-test critique; that absence is a search result, not a fact about the literature. Where a percentage appears without recoverable integers, as with the anesthesia and sleep interview rates and the Dutch 74%, the missing numbers are recorded as nulls.

<!-- id: c06-p045 -->
Let me be clear about what this chapter has not shown. It has not shown that consciousness is brain-independent. No inspected crisis case establishes the conjunction that claim requires, and the strongest dossiers reduce under audit to single uncorroborated reporting chains. It has not shown that any particular ordinary mechanism produced any particular report. The critics' accounts remain possibilities that were never measured in any particular case, and the symmetric standard withholds both verdicts. It cannot turn controlled neural dependence into physicalism, into an identity thesis, or into a refutation of every transmission model; the intervention stream constrains all surviving models without selecting one. Nor can it turn any failure of a theory test into support for survival. It cannot upgrade reported experiences into observed events, recovery into miracle, subjective timelessness into ontological timelessness, or recurrence into mechanism. No component of the fundamental-consciousness package receives support from this chapter: not fundamentality, universal subjecthood, brain independence, survival beyond irreversible death, timelessness, preservation, intention, goodness or moral authority. And all twelve withheld stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld through it.
