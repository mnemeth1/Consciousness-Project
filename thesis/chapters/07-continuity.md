<!-- id: c07-p001 -->
The previous chapter ended with an asymmetry. Controlled interventions give us discriminating observations about how consciousness depends on the brain; crisis reports give us reports that call for explanation. This chapter takes up a question that would remain even if that asymmetry were reversed. What does personal survival require, over and above the recurrence of a conscious state or the preservation of information? And does timelessness offer a way of avoiding loss altogether? To answer, the chapter expands the article's sixth section through three packets. The first is the physics and philosophy of time audit, which measures the distance between a physical formalism and a timeless conscious bearer. The second is the personal-identity audit, which sets out the psychological, organismic and simple-subject criteria and says what each one costs. The third is the Phase 2 reinspection of the Stump–Kretzmann eternity proposal with its complete chain of critics and replies, including the separately inspected Lewis–Oaklander exchange over tenseless persons and moral responsibility. The chapter closes by treating temporal phenomenology as a finding to be explained, by setting out the dependence accounting, and by saying what none of this material concludes.

<!-- id: c07-p002 -->
The standing caution applies here, with some additions particular to this chapter. Every position discussed below is attributed to an identified inspected version, and the versions deserve listing. The physics sources are a 1999 electronic edition of the 1923 English translation of Einstein's 1905 paper, and an arXiv preprint of Rovelli's partial-observables paper whose version header and body dates conflict. The Boethius is H. R. James's 1897 English translation. The two Craig essays are author-hosted web pages with no publication dates in the inspected files. The Locke is a Bennett modernization. The Olson is a repository accepted version that cannot be assumed identical to the published 2003 chapter. The Swinburne is a short 2019 publisher blog essay. The six journal articles of the two eternity-related exchanges (four in the eternity-personhood exchange and two in the Lewis–Oaklander exchange) were inspected as exact user-supplied journal copies, and their version anomalies are recorded. Three further cautions follow. Attributing an argument accurately is not the same as showing its premises true. The Boethian and Craig discussions share premises, so they are not independent observations. And no paragraph in this chapter reports a new empirical result, except where a study is explicitly cited as one.

## Survival, recurrence and preservation are three different things

<!-- id: c07-p003 -->
The article sets the target, and it is worth stating plainly. Suppose we had evidence of consciousness under unexpected physiological conditions. Personal survival would still remain to be argued. Why? Because survival concerns a further subject who is the same person. It is not merely the recurrence of a type of conscious state, and it is not merely the preservation of information about a life. A duplicate with accurate memories could matter a great deal without settling strict identity. Concern for a successor may be rational without answering the original survival question. The project's component definitions hold this line: survival requires the same person's subjectivity beyond irreversible bodily death. Nothing weaker will do. Resemblance, a record, a representation, or another subject's complete knowledge of a life does not meet that requirement merely by being redescribed as survival.

<!-- id: c07-p004 -->
The audit sorts the candidate models by what each would actually deliver. The first is temporal continuation: the person persists within time, before and possibly after bodily death, and no timeless foundation is required. The second adds a timeless foundation that grounds temporal persons; here individual survival remains an additional condition, and the foundation does not automatically supply it. The third proposes complete-history inclusion, in which every event of a finite life is represented in, or present to, an eternal knower. The fourth is preservation without survival: information, represented relationships or value are retained while the original subject ceases. The recorded inferences keep these four apart. Including a finite life or its record does not, on its own, meet the survival requirement. And a conscious foundation's awareness of a life is not automatically that life's own subjectivity.

<!-- id: c07-p005 -->
Is this distinction pedantic? The handoff inference explains why it is not. To represent or include every event of a finite life is to preserve specified contents. But a record, or a knower who contains that record, is not automatically numerically identical to the person represented. An enlarged history that contains genuinely continuing subjectivity outside embodied succession is a different and stronger hypothesis. It deserves assessment under an explicit identity relation, and that is what the identity-criteria section below supplies. Merely calling the inclusion survival does not make it survival. The burdens are listed in the record: a bearer, a death condition, numerical identity, a continuation domain, memory and branching.

## Eternity proposals and the presence objection

<!-- id: c07-p006 -->
Physics comes first, because timelessness is so often introduced in its vocabulary. Einstein's 1905 kinematic argument derives frame-dependent distant simultaneity from three things: a relativity principle, an invariant vacuum light speed and a procedure for synchronizing clocks. The inspected transformation and moving-clock relations still retain elapsed clock readings, and unequal rates do not do away with temporal succession [@S-T11-001]. In the displayed Lorentz relation, positive elapsed time along a moving clock's path remains positive below light speed. In other words, a complete-history representation need not collapse an ordered series of clock events into one simultaneous event. The inspected version is the 1999 Walker electronic edition of the 1923 Methuen translation, with its notation and editorial corrections disclosed. The original German was not audited. Nor were the modern precision tests.

<!-- id: c07-p007 -->
Rovelli's relational formalism shows what the more radical option costs. The inspected preprint distinguishes measurable partial observables from predictive complete correlations, treats physical clocks relationally, and presents a constrained formulation that needs no privileged external time [@S-T11-002]. Yet it explicitly restricts its detailed development to finite degrees of freedom, and it retains histories and relational predictions. The version identity is disclosed rather than tidied away. The arXiv v3 header is dated 21 January 2002, while the rendered body carries a title date of October 23, 2018; that discrepancy is retained as found, and the published journal version was not separately inspected. What we have, then, is a theoretical framework. It is not an empirically confirmed quantum-gravity cosmology.

<!-- id: c07-p008 -->
The bridge from physics to metaphysics is where the audit does its real work. Coordinate-invariant observables do not entail the absence of physical change, of experience, or of all intrinsic succession. Neither does the omission of a privileged external time. Relativity, a relational formalism and eternalism do not on their own identify an experiential bearer, or the grounding relation that a foundational-consciousness claim needs. The general point is simple: a formal representation does not settle the full ontology of what it represents. Even an eternalist reading contains earlier/later relations, and it identifies no single experiencing mind. The recorded underdetermination principle makes this general. If rival metaphysical models yield the same physical and report predictions, those observations cannot tell the models apart. A discriminating test requires differing expectations that have been specified independently.

<!-- id: c07-p009 -->
The classical eternity proposal is older than its modern formalization. In the inspected 1897 translation of Boethius, the character Philosophy contrasts the whole possession of life with endless sequential existence, and she locates divine priority in simplicity rather than in earlier duration. A separate book distinguishes unified providence from its temporal unfolding, and the reply on foreknowledge distinguishes conditional from simple necessity [@S-T11-003]. The version discipline matters here. This is a dialogue, voiced by a character, in a historical translation. Its "single moment" wording is read against its explicit contrast with temporal succession, not as a coordinate instant. And the account still owes us an adequate relation between infallible knowledge, grounding and free choices.

<!-- id: c07-p010 -->
Stump and Kretzmann's 1981 article, inspected as an exact journal copy, develops the richer proposal that the article credits. They define eternity as life with illimitability, duration and no succession. This is distinguished both from mere atemporality and from beginningless, endless temporal life. The authors expressly disclaim any proof that an eternal entity exists or that God must be eternal [@S-P2T12-001]. Their ET relation holds between an eternal relatum and a temporal one, and it uses presentness relative to observers in both modes. The relation is symmetric but neither reflexive nor transitive. In particular, it does not make two temporal events simultaneous merely because each is related to eternity. That last formal feature matters. The proposal need not collapse distinct temporal moments into one another, and that is the crude objection it was built to avoid.

<!-- id: c07-p011 -->
The 1981 sketch of a timeless mental life is candid about its own limits. It excludes deliberation, anticipation, memory and planning, while considering knowing, willing and awareness. An independently existing immaterial mind is assumed for the sake of the argument, and the authors themselves call the capacity sketch informal and insufficient as a proof of possibility [@S-P2T12-001]. The account separates an atemporal act from its temporally located effect. A prayer and its response-effect, they propose, can be causally dependent without the eternal willing occurring later than the prayer. Their tensed-knowledge response says that an eternal knower can know all events at each dated temporal now, and can know their being experienced as present, without any succession of divine knowledge states. The illustrative examples verify nothing. They state what the model would say if it were true.

<!-- id: c07-p012 -->
Two boundaries inside the 1981 article deserve to be kept, because the survival question turns on them. First, in the authors' hypothetical, a temporal person's life and death can each be present to an eternal entity without that person thereby existing eternally, and without the two temporal events being simultaneous. That is the authors' own denial, later quoted by the article, that presence to eternity confers eternal existence on the temporal relatum [@S-P2T12-001]. Second, the article's discussion of the incarnation invokes one person with divine and human natures, while explicitly leaving the outline rudimentary and unanalyzed. It supplies no general criterion of numerical identity for human persons. At its strongest, then, the proposal concerns the eternal entity's relation to temporal lives. It does not concern the continuation of those lives.

<!-- id: c07-p013 -->
Lewis's 1984 reply, inspected as an exact journal copy, opens the chain of criticism. Direct temporal observation, he argues, requires its object to exist at that time. He treats this principle as broadly necessary, allowing a causal remnant when an object has ceased. So a directly observed God would have at least the temporality of existing alongside temporal things [@S-P2T12-002]. He goes on to challenge whether an eternal cause is part of reality when its temporal effect occurs, and he argues that objects being jointly present to an epistemic subject does not by itself establish a possible causal connection. Then comes the concession that frames everything after it. Lewis grants the eternal being calendar knowledge and propositional knowledge; what is missing, he presses, is knowledge of which events presently exist. One provenance anomaly is recorded: the inspected copy misprints the 1981 source volume as 77, where the inspected 1981 header reads 78. It establishes no alternative chronology.

<!-- id: c07-p014 -->
Lewis's 1988 criticism sharpens the demand from knowledge to existence. He reads the classical doctrine as requiring temporal things to be existentially present in eternity, not merely epistemically present. Its spatial-containment analogies, he argues, require past, present and future things to be ontologically on a par. From this he concludes that eternal omniscience, so understood, entails tenseless time, and he notes the tension between that result and the tensed motivations for defending eternity [@S-P2T12-003]. His further claim, that tenseless persons cannot bear moral responsibility, explicitly depends on his separate 1986 article, which the inspected 1988 paper does not develop. That argument and its actual reply are treated below as an exchange of their own. One caution about provenance: the classical texts Lewis quotes are quoted within his argument. The project did not independently reopen editions of Boethius, Anselm or Aquinas for this packet.

<!-- id: c07-p015 -->
The 1992 defense answers a specified target. It was inspected as an exact journal copy, and a version anomaly is recorded: the issue header reads October 1992, while the body contains an early-1993 reference. The paper explicitly treats perfect possession of life as requiring eternity. It uses analogical predication to resist two moves at once: temporalizing divine duration, and making it wholly equivocal. Its illustrations, a discussion of the specious present and a fictional story, stipulate cross-mode access rather than demonstrate it [@S-P2T12-004]. The revised relation, ET′, adds capacity clauses: direct and immediate causal relations and, where capable, direct awareness, in both reference frames. Against the demand for a shared mode it offers an internal analogy, a nonspatial God's awareness of spatial creatures. The citation discipline is worth stating carefully. The paper explicitly answers Lewis's 1988 existential-presence objection, paired with an objection from Hasker, and it identifies Lewis 1988 in note 35. It does not cite Lewis 1984 anywhere in its complete body or notes. So the direct reply relation is established only for the specified 1988 target.

<!-- id: c07-p016 -->
The recorded adjudication of this exchange is symmetric, and it is unresolved. The revision makes the intended cognitive and causal relation explicit. But defining a capacity does not by itself establish that its clauses are jointly possible. The defenders themselves acknowledge that a three-dimensional "here" has no equivalent of the absolute temporal present, while maintaining in a note that their model need not deny real temporal passage [@S-P2T12-004]. That is an acknowledged limit on the analogy, and it leaves the tense objection substantive. The article's verdict carries over unchanged. The reply improves the proposal by identifying the relation it needs. It does not establish the possibility of that relation by definition, and the disanalogy about the absolute present is just where the spatial comparison may fail.

<!-- id: c07-p017 -->
Craig's essay on tensed facts, inspected as an author-hosted page with no publication date in the inspected file, turns the presence objection into a general dilemma for timeless omniscience. Grant objective A-theoretic tense, and grant that a knower must know what is presently true. Then a cognitively perfect ground of a tensed world requires intrinsic cognitive succession. Craig explicitly says that a B-theory avoids the argument. He also argues that replacing propositional beliefs with direct intuition does not by itself remove the objection, so long as an irreducibly tensed reality must still be known [@S-T11-004]. The recorded limits run in both directions. The disputed semantics and the knowledge premise are not proven merely by the existence of a temporal world. And Craig's summaries of the modern defenders he criticizes are no substitute for their originals, which were not inspected.

<!-- id: c07-p018 -->
Craig's essay on personhood, inspected under the same version limits, defends the other flank. We must not infer the necessary conditions of personhood solely from how finite humans learn. A possible knower may have changeless self-awareness and rational volition without deliberative updating, since not every intention is directed at the future or reached by deliberation [@S-T11-005]. His own preferred model, timeless without creation and temporal with creation, is recorded together with its classification. It is a temporal alternative once a world exists, not foundation-level timelessness throughout the relation, and the transition into time must not posit a time before time. Simple arguments from personhood do not automatically refute timelessness. Yet actual interaction still needs a model, and neither an unexercised capacity nor a Trinitarian illustration supplies one.

<!-- id: c07-p019 -->
What does the timeless-foundation construction amount to? On the record, it is a conditional model with named debts. One can posit a single non-successive act that knows and grounds a temporally ordered history, provided the grounding relation and individuated subjectivity are independently intelligible. That is a project reconstruction; it is not the verbatim model of either source. Two supporting distinctions are recorded as contested rather than settled. Knowing a dated event, and knowing each person's indexical belief, need not mean being that person or acquiring those beliefs one after another. And a changing time-indexed relation to a fixed foundation is distinct from a new intrinsic state of that foundation. The dependence audit adds a deflating footnote. The Boethian and Craig discussions share premises about knowledge and personhood, so their agreement is not independent corroboration. And conditional coherence establishes neither actuality nor equal support against alternatives.

## Preservation in a universal mind versus continued subjectivity

<!-- id: c07-p020 -->
What follows from the whole exchange for survival? A non-entailment, and the record states it twice over. The selected model itself distinguishes the eternal knower and creator from temporal persons. A grounding or awareness relation is not numerical identity, and it is not a rule that determines which later subject is the same human person [@S-P2T12-001; @S-P2T12-004]. Even actual eternal awareness of a finite life would not by itself establish that the life's human subject has experiences beyond bodily death. For a survival conclusion, two things remain necessary: actual further subjectivity, and a justified same-person relation. Notice who draws which part of this boundary. The first part is the defenders' own: in their 1981 example, a person's life and death can each be present to the eternal without the person thereby existing eternally. The second part, that survival would further require actual continued subjectivity and a justified same-person relation, is this project's analysis under its survival premise, drawn from the defenders' text but not asserted by them. Neither part is imported from a critic.

<!-- id: c07-p021 -->
The article diagnoses the preservation substitution, and the record supports the diagnosis. Complete divine awareness could preserve the truth about a finite life while adding no further experience for its subject. A theory that preserves the information while ending its owner answers a different existential concern from the one that motivates the hope of survival. If we want our relationships and our activity to continue, it is no answer that they are eternally known. The recorded inference gives this teeth. Preservation-only models can retain information, represented relationships or value while the original subject ceases. To upgrade preservation into survival, one needs a defended subject-preserving relation. That burden cannot be discharged by renaming the foundation's knowledge as the person's life.

<!-- id: c07-p022 -->
The strongest survival-compatible proposal in the record comes with its costs attached. Call it an enlarged first-person history. The foundation grounds a concrete history that includes subjectivity outside the boundary of the embodied life, and that history is unified with the embodied life by a defensible identity relation. Such a proposal could satisfy the survival requirement even if the foundation has no intrinsic succession. The recorded objection is the honest one. The asserted ownership or extension may simply restate survival, since a universal knower can preserve a finite life without the person continuing. The recorded reply treats subjectivity and individuation as substantive ontology with identifiable constraints. That blocks dismissal by definition, but it adds explanatory and evidential costs. Two of those costs are named. The strict organism criterion rejects disembodied extension unless a same-organism relation is defended. And "beyond" the bodily life must be specified relationally, if not temporally. No observation in the record establishes an enlarged history, and the physics of the earlier section supplies no discriminating prediction for one.

<!-- id: c07-p023 -->
Relativity and dimensional stories recur inside the eternity literature. The reinspection packet treats them just as the physics audit did. In the selected articles they function as analogies, and neither formal simultaneity nor physical theory supplies an observed timeless mind or a discriminating prediction for the model [@S-P2T12-001; @S-P2T12-004]. The recorded boundary on the whole exchange follows from this. The selected exchange supports no winner among the families, and this material does not complete a six-family ranking. And there is no automatic upgrade from conceptual coherence to actuality, fundamentality, universality, survival or the timelessness of human subjects. All twelve withheld stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld through this material.

## Identity criteria and their consequences

<!-- id: c07-p024 -->
The psychological criterion enters through its classical source. Locke was inspected in the Bennett modernization of the chapter added to the 1694 second edition, with the editorial additions marked. He distinguishes the identity of a person, of a human animal and of a substance, and he lets personal identity follow consciousness reaching back to past acts, rather than sameness of material particles or of soul [@S-T13-001]. He considers forgetfulness, sleep, changing substances and the transfer of the prince and the cobbler, which is a conditional illustration and not an observed result. His response to irreversible forgetting distinguishes the person from the man. The version note stands: this is a modernization, not a diplomatic original, and the correct criterion remains contested.

<!-- id: c07-p025 -->
The repaired continuity account, and the ways it is known to fail, are recorded as project analysis. To define genuine memory as memory of one's own past can presuppose the very identity it is meant to establish. So a causal quasi-memory criterion must specify its link independently of sameness of person. Overlapping causal links remove the need for every later stage to remember every earlier stage, and a nonbranching clause deals with duplication. Both repairs need independent justification; stipulation will not do. The branching constraint is structural. If two successors are distinct, strict numerical identity cannot identify both of them with the one predecessor. So branch-preserving concern, or psychological succession, uses a relation other than identity. These are logical observations under the project's definitions. They carry no inspected attribution to Parfit, Butler or Reid.

<!-- id: c07-p026 -->
The organismic criterion is recorded through Olson's argument for animalism. The inspected text is a repository accepted version; it includes a 2006 reference, and it cannot be assumed identical to the published 2003 chapter [@S-T13-002]. The argument is brief. A human organism is present. It thinks. There is exactly one relevant thinker. So the person is the organism. The unique-thinker premise is made explicit, and the costs of denying that animals think, or of multiplying thinkers, are pressed against rival views. An animal can persist without the psychological features by which we recognize it, so a disembodied copy on its own does not preserve the organism. The transplant intuition remains the standing objection, recorded as a competing conditional judgment. Animalism alone is not a complete theory of death, of resurrection or of animal persistence. And Olson's reconstructions of other authors are not inspections of their originals.

<!-- id: c07-p027 -->
The simple-subject criterion is recorded through Swinburne's short 2019 essay, a publisher blog piece rather than the technical argument of his book. Imagine a hypothetical split that leaves two candidates with equal psychological support. If there is a determinate fact, over and above the total qualitative facts, about which of them, if either, is the earlier subject, then that further fact requires a bearer the physical description misses. A soul is the candidate [@S-T13-003]. The recorded objection is sharp. Epistemic underdetermination does not entail an immaterial entity. The inference needs three things: determinate further identity facts, their compatibility with the stipulated evidence, and a reason why those facts require an immaterial bearer. The recorded scope limit matters for this chapter too. A simple soul is not immortality. A surviving soul and continuing experience require additional premises about persistence and activation, and the essay does not supply them.

<!-- id: c07-p028 -->
Two inferences that cut across the criteria discipline how they may be applied to survival. First, interrupted awareness or ordinary forgetting does not by itself decide persistence. An organism, a simple subject or an adequately continuous psychological history may each bridge sleep, under different assumptions. But no inference from bridging sleep to bridging death is recorded. Second, no selected identity criterion automatically establishes universal subjecthood, timelessness, intention, goodness or moral authority. Each of those components needs its own bridge and its own specified bearer. The criteria tell us what survival would require. None of them supplies evidence that it occurs.

<!-- id: c07-p029 -->
The tenseless-persons exchange, added by the Phase 2 reinspection as exact journal copies, supplies the developed human-identity objection that was missing. Lewis's 1986 article asks what human persons are and what sameness across time means; it sets the evidential question aside. It argues that tenseless time requires persons to be extended composites with temporal parts. It then challenges the assignment of responsibility to a tenseless whole, which he denies is conscious; to a present stage for an earlier stage's deed; and to bundles of stages [@S-P2T12-005]. Embodied actions and many mental actions, he argues, require an interval with a persisting agent. His conclusion is conditional: tenseless persons cannot be morally responsible agents. His own hedges are kept in the record. He allows possible instantaneous mental acts through a quoted discussion, and his "perduring" means persistence in general, not the modern technical term.

<!-- id: c07-p030 -->
Oaklander's reply is the actual published critique. Its direct-reply relation was established from the body and the references, not from the title or the order of publication. He reconstructs the argument and offers three diagnoses: a false premise, an invalid inference between senses of persistence, or a question-begging substantialist assumption [@S-P2T12-006]. He disputes that tenselessness entails temporal parts, citing accounts that were inspected only through his presentation of them. He offers temporally indexed properties without incompatible unqualified predicates. He distinguishes reference to a stage from reference to the whole person. He sketches psychological and physical continuity as alternatives compatible with agency. And he separates the ordinary requirement that responsibility involve the same persisting agent from the stronger claim that agency requires a simple substance. He expressly declines to settle the tense debate. His closing suggestion of how Lewis might respond is hypothetical. No later Lewis rebuttal was located in bounded forward searches, and that absence proves nothing.

<!-- id: c07-p031 -->
What does the exchange yield? Something bounded on every side. The claimed necessary incompatibility between tenselessness and human moral agency is contested by explicit alternatives. Neither article establishes an evidential same-person test, a postmortem subject or an actual timeless ground. One further distinction guards the central boundary of this chapter. Tenseless existence across ordered times is not the same as the nonsuccessive life proposed for an eternal ground. So even if human numerical identity were preserved on a tenseless model, that would neither remove temporal succession nor establish a timeless human subject. The moral-persons consequence of the 1988 paper now traces to an inspected original and an inspected direct reply. The chain is complete, and it adds coverage of the argument. It adds no evidence.

## Temporal phenomenology as something to explain

<!-- id: c07-p032 -->
The empirical material within this chapter's scope concerns how altered temporal experience is measured, so the audit of the instrument comes first. Greyson's 1990 comparison uses the self-selected newsletter cohort we met in the previous chapter. Its broadened time item has a highest response option that groups apparent simultaneity, stopped time and time losing its meaning into one category [@S-T08-006]. A high score on that item is therefore not a count of one uniquely defined timeless experience. The original 1983 validation was not reinspected. Composite instruments mix distinct phenomenologies, and any argument from scale endorsements to a specific temporal ontology carries that mixture with it.

<!-- id: c07-p033 -->
The one controlled comparison in scope is the 2018 DMT study by Timmermann and colleagues, inspected in full HTML without the supplement or participant-level data. Thirteen psychedelic-experienced volunteers received placebo and DMT in a fixed placebo-first order, with single blinding and heterogeneous doses. All 13 met the NDE-scale threshold, a score of 7 or above, after DMT; one was at threshold after placebo. These are repeated conditions in 13 people, not 26 independent people [@S-T13-004]. The time-item means were 1.15 (SD 0.80) under DMT and 1.54 (SD 0.78) in the separate 13-person NDE comparator, which was recruited retrospectively and selected as above the standard threshold. No raw list of boundary scores was published and no response-category counts were supplied. A non-significant difference is not an equivalence test. The recorded case notes add three points. The comparator's published sex counts (seven women, six men) differ from the experimental group's (six women, seven men), despite the matching described. The comparator's overlap with other collections is unestablished. And the sample sizes are not to be added together.

<!-- id: c07-p034 -->
The recorded inference from this overlap runs both ways, and this chapter takes it over whole. Phenomenological overlap after DMT shows that similar scale endorsements can arise in a drug context. It does not establish endogenous DMT as the cause of any particular crisis experience, and it does not explain away any specific anomalous detail. Conversely, the survival-friendly reply, that drugs might change access to experience rather than generate it, is a possible interpretation. It requires additional discriminating predictions; it is not a rebuttal that the data supply. Resemblance alone identifies neither mechanism nor ontology. The recorded recommendation is for prospective designs with independently scored information and resolved timing, together with the separate question of what identity relation any result would support.

<!-- id: c07-p035 -->
The crisis-report material already audited contributes one thing here, and only one: reported temporal phenomenology as a documented thing to be explained. Alexander's reviewed account of altered duration, in which months or years seemed to pass, concerns remembered phenomenology. Its attribution is carried over from the talk transcript, whose audio was not verified. And it is not the same claim as a claim of no intrinsic succession [@S-T06-001]. Reports from resuscitated subjects and aggregate brain-monitoring records do not supply the survival component's irreversible-death boundary, episode-specific experience timing or a numerical-identity bridge [@S-T07-007]. Subjective temporal alteration is a finding to explain. It is not evidence of ontological timelessness, whether in the experiencer or in the world.

<!-- id: c07-p036 -->
The coding discipline closes the loop between phenomenology and ontology. Reports of no succession, of simultaneous content, of indefinite duration and of later narrative order must be coded separately. Why? Because testimony may support the occurrence of an experience without establishing the ontology attributed to it. The converse guard is kept as well: an atemporal awareness could be narrated sequentially afterward, so narrative order on its own does not refute it [@S-T08-006; @S-T13-004]. No validated instrument in the record uniquely identifies timelessness. What we have to explain are documented and recurrent reports. Their ontological interpretation is just as open as the eternity exchange left it.

## Dependence accounting, access gaps and non-conclusions

<!-- id: c07-p037 -->
The dependence structure of this chapter is dominated by shared philosophical premises rather than by shared samples. The physics material falls into the Einstein kinematic formalism, the Rovelli relational formalism and the physics-to-metaphysics bridge. The classical and modern eternity material falls into the Boethian timeless-knowledge group, the Craig tensed-knowledge and personhood groups, the timeless-foundation construction, and the eternity-personhood exchange (Stump–Kretzmann 1981 and 1992, Lewis 1984 and 1988). The Lewis 1986–Oaklander 1987–88 exchange is a group of its own. The recorded shared-premises claim marks the Boethius and Craig discussions as non-independent. The identity criteria carry per-author groups (Locke, Olson, Swinburne), plus the project's identity-bridge inferences. The empirical material carries the DMT study group, the IANDS-descended retrospective group for the Greyson instrument, and the Alexander and resuscitation-report chains carried over from chapter 6, which add no new observations here. Nothing in this chapter sums across a group.

<!-- id: c07-p038 -->
The access gaps are declared. The Craig essays lack publication dates in their inspected versions, and repository metadata attributing one of them to April 2000 was not independently checked. The modern defenders Craig criticizes, the authors Olson reconstructs, the accounts Oaklander cites, and the technical literatures behind the third-party discussions in the 1992 defense all remain uninspected originals. Parfit was not directly accessed. The published version of Rovelli's preprint, the original German Einstein, the Latin Boethius, the diplomatic Locke and the published 2003 Olson text were not inspected, and the Swinburne essay is not his book-length argument. A bounded forward search located no later Lewis rebuttal to Oaklander; that is a search result, not a fact about the literature. By contrast, the absence of any citation of Lewis 1984 from the complete body and notes of the 1992 paper is an inspection finding in the exact 1992 copy, not a search result. Let me be clear about what this chapter has not shown. It cannot conclude that any timeless model is actual, or that any is impossible. It cannot conclude that any identity criterion is correct. It cannot conclude that anyone survives death, or that any reported temporal phenomenology reveals the world's temporal ontology. The eternity proposal remains an improved but still unproven relation. The preservation substitution fails as an answer to survival. And every component of the fundamental-consciousness package (fundamentality, universal subjecthood, brain independence, survival beyond irreversible death, timelessness, preservation, intention, goodness, moral authority) remains just as unestablished after this chapter as it was before it. All twelve withheld stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld.
