<!-- id: c04-p001 -->
The previous chapter set out the positions. This chapter asks what moves a theorist to take experience as basic, and what that move costs. The question is not which family of views is true. That question is gated, and it stays gated. The question is what a theory buys when it counts experience among its primitives, and what debts it takes on in the same transaction. Four exchanges from the record carry the analysis: the explanatory-gap debate between Chalmers and Papineau, the illusionism debate between Frankish and Nida-Rümelin, the causal-emergence debate among O'Connor and Wong, Kim and Shoemaker, and the constitution debate between Strawson and Goff. The chapter closes with the burdens that every conscious-ground proposal takes over, because those burdens shape everything the later, empirical chapters can and cannot test.

<!-- id: c04-p002 -->
One caution governs every sentence that follows. Nothing in this chapter's inputs is an empirical observation. They are inspected philosophical texts, together with reviewed project analyses of those texts. So every "argues," "proposes" and "concedes" below is a documented attribution to an identified version of a text, and an attribution never establishes the truth of what is attributed. Each exchange is also internally dependent. The gap debate and its phenomenal-concepts sequel share authors and a running dispute, and each debate forms a single dependency group (a set of records that counts as one source of support, not several). However many claims this chapter yields, none of them multiplies into independent lines of support.

## The demand that survives functional explanation

<!-- id: c04-p003 -->
The demand comes from Chalmers, and the record carries it as a distinction rather than a doctrine. Explaining discrimination, access or behavioral control is one kind of task. Explaining why those processes have a subjective character is another [@S-T03-001]. The inspected text is an author-hosted version of the 1995 article, examined at its relevant sections, and it does not claim that functional explanation has achieved nothing. Its claim is conditional and structural: finishing the functional program would not obviously finish the different task of accounting for experience. That conditional is what survives ordinary explanatory success. It is also what every position in this chapter is answering, deflating or refusing.

<!-- id: c04-p004 -->
A theory that includes experience among its primitives meets the demand in one definite sense. It no longer has to make the experiential arise from a vocabulary that was stipulated to contain nothing experiential. That is genuine relief, but it is relief by definition. To decline to derive experience is not yet to explain anything about it: which experiences occur, in which systems, and with what structure. The article frames the matter in a way this thesis keeps. The advantage is real only if the demand has been correctly identified, and that identification is the very thing the physicalist side contests.

<!-- id: c04-p005 -->
The developed antiphysicalist argument in the record runs through knowledge and conceivability. On the author text, inspected at its relevant sections, Chalmers argues against the phenomenal-concept strategy and for a conditional conclusion. If the physical facts can be conceived without the experiential facts, and if the relevant conceivability entails the relevant possibility, then physicalism is false [@S-T05-003]. Three bridge principles are recorded with that argument. The modal bridge takes ideal conceivability to relevant metaphysical possibility. The knowledge bridge takes new epistemic access to an ontologically new fact, and the record treats it as an additional requirement of the knowledge argument rather than as part of the conceivability conditional itself. The explanatory bridge is a standard: a physical explanation must make the high-level truth transparently derivable, and one standard must be applied consistently across physical and experiential cases. The record marks all three as contested, and the conclusion as conditional on them. The text itself is an author PDF identified by its exact URL, and its publication year appears as both 2006 and 2007 in the discovery metadata.

<!-- id: c04-p006 -->
The physicalist defense in the record has two prongs. The first is the phenomenal-concept strategy itself. Papineau defends the view that phenomenal and physical concepts are two distinct epistemic routes to one and the same property, so that an epistemic difference, a difference in the way we know, need not refute identity [@S-T05-004]. The inspected text is an author-hosted typeset chapter, and the record leaves its exact publication year unresolved. Chalmers answers with a dilemma, in the same dependency group. Roughly, the conceptual features that are supposed to do the explaining either resist physical explanation themselves, or they fail to explain the epistemic situation they were invoked to explain, including its truth and justification. The record leaves that dilemma, like everything else here, as an attributed argument with contested premises.

<!-- id: c04-p007 -->
The second prong attacks the standard of explanation the demand assumes. On the inspected published article, an author-hosted text of the 2011 *Philosophia* paper, Papineau challenges what an identity owes us. Identities, once warranted, need no further explanation. So the leftover question, "why is this process experienced this way," may be a demand for something no true identity could supply [@S-T05-005]. The felt gap is moved out of ontology and into psychology: it becomes a resistance to identification whose causes are open to empirical study. Notice what this deflation does. It does not close the gap; it reclassifies it.

<!-- id: c04-p008 -->
The lesson for method, stated in the article this chapter expands, is that the burden runs both ways. The antireductionist must defend the step from a gap in explanation to a gap in the world. That explanatory bridge is a premise, not a datum. The reductionist must show that the proposed identification preserves its explanandum, the thing to be explained, rather than replacing it. An identity earns its explanatory relief only once it is warranted, and the mere possibility of an identity explains nothing in particular. Neither move comes free, and the record counts the cost of both.

## Illusionism and the acquaintance exchange

<!-- id: c04-p009 -->
Illusionism makes the reductionist's burden, to preserve the explanandum, unusually explicit. It does so by refusing to preserve it, openly. On the inspected author version, Frankish keeps experience in an inclusive sense: perceptual activity, access, reports and introspective representation. What he rejects is genuinely qualitative properties, whether physical or nonphysical. The central task then becomes explaining why a system represents itself as possessing such properties [@S-P2T05-001]. Two qualifications from the record go with this. The account describes itself as a programme with proposed content mechanisms, not as a completed explanation. And its motivations (conservative explanation, introspective fallibility, and abduction from anomalousness) are proposals whose premises are contested.

<!-- id: c04-p010 -->
The programme's structural discipline deserves to be separated from its truth. It avoids the conscious spectator. No inner audience inspects the internal representations, because such an audience would recreate the problem one level up. It also meets the regress worry, which is that each apparent feel needs a higher representation, so that we face either a circle or a concealed phenomenal starting point. Its answer is fine-grained nonphenomenal seemings and a limited higher-order stop. The record's assessment is careful on this point. Once functional seeming and limited introspection are allowed, the regress identifies a real explanatory constraint, not an automatic contradiction. What the constraint costs is a worked account of why nonphenomenal representations present determinate apparent qualities. No such completed account is in the record.

<!-- id: c04-p011 -->
The recorded objection is stronger than the slogan that an illusion must itself be conscious. Its author notes that quick line and sets it aside. The text of Nida-Rümelin's that was inspected is an author manuscript; its final print was not available for byte-level or visual verification. On that manuscript she identifies experiential properties through subjects and examples, so that their reference does not depend on any description an illusionist could reject. She then argues that to instantiate the relevant property is already to be aware of it [@S-P2T05-002]. Acquaintance, on this account, is not a second introspective judgment that could misrepresent some independently occurring state. There is no gap in which the illusionist's error theory can live. Her conclusion is expressly conditional on an experiential example actually being instantiated.

<!-- id: c04-p012 -->
Frankish's inspected reply grants the ordinary examples, and it grants the reformulation in terms of subjects and properties, for the sake of argument. What it denies is the one thing the objection needs: that reference by example fixes the property's realist nature [@S-P2T05-003]. What the examples pick out may admit a representational interpretation. Cognitive access to structural physical properties requires mediation, so robust unmediated acquaintance is rejected. The reply candidly derives that rejection from physicalist commitments, and it expects not to persuade this critic. So the exchange ends with each side's decisive premise intact and openly identified: the adequacy of the functional account on one side, the identification of awareness with instantiation on the other.

<!-- id: c04-p013 -->
The dispute, then, is about what must be preserved when the examples are explained, and this thesis keeps the article's framing of the stalemate. To define every seeming as phenomenal is to assume the realist premise. To define apparent character as nothing beyond representation is to assume too much the other way. The stronger illusionist response must explain the richness and authority of phenomenal judgments without quietly relying on their truth. The stronger realist response must show why its identification of the target resists that explanation. Within this dialectic, experiential primitiveness gives the realist a genuine dialectical advantage, and the record states it carefully: it preserves the target rather than revising it. What it does not yet establish is that preservation is explanatorily superior to a successful account of misrepresentation. Whether a misrepresentation account can succeed is the very thing the record marks unresolved.

## Emergence, exclusion and latent powers

<!-- id: c04-p014 -->
Accepting the target does not choose an ontology, and the causal-emergence exchange shows why. O'Connor's 1994 formulation required supervenience, nonstructurality and downward causal influence [@S-P2T01-003]. The 2005 account written with Wong explicitly repudiates the supervenience requirement. In its place it posits basic nonstructural properties that organized physical systems causally generate and sustain over time [@S-P2T01-001]. Both are inspected originals. The record treats the two dependence accounts as distinct, and they must not be merged. The model that matters downstream is the temporal one, on which the emergent is generated and maintained.

<!-- id: c04-p015 -->
Kim's challenge is narrower than its reputation, and sharper for it. The text inspected is the 2008 reprint; the original 1999 journal pagination was not inspected. He allows ordinary downward causation, and he treats phenomenal qualia as promising candidates for irreducibility. His argument is this. Where an emergent has a sufficient noncausal basal condition, the base threatens to preempt the emergent as a cause, and moving the emergent's effects later in time does not by itself remove the threat [@S-P2T01-002]. The argument rests on premises of its own: noncausal determination, basal sufficiency, and the exclusion of systematic overdetermination. The record marks these as substantive commitments, not logical truths.

<!-- id: c04-p016 -->
The structural lesson of the reply is that naming an emergent property answers nothing. A premise has to be rejected. The 2005 account rejects the noncausal-dependence premise. If the base causally generates the emergent, then the emergent is an intermediate cause in a temporal process rather than a redundant competitor, and the preemption inference fails against this model. The escape has its costs, and the record lists them. There is a disputed simplicity comparison against latent-power alternatives. There is a thought experiment that stipulates its own discriminating conditions. The first-person motivation is contested. And there is no independently confirmed emergence law.

<!-- id: c04-p017 -->
Shoemaker's inspected countercase supplies the other horn of a productive choice. His micro-latent powers are powers of ordinary microconstituents that are manifested only in suitable, emergence-engendering combinations. They let the complete microfacts fix the macrofacts, while still distinguishing causal power from mere nomic necessitation. His preferred treatment, in terms of identity and description, differs from irreducible emergent properties. He also considers a distinct-property option and rejects it on the strength of his commitments about essential individuation [@S-P2T01-004]. The record's point of comparison is a demanding one. A successful reply to Kim does not certify the nonstructural ontology. To decide between the replies, one has to adjudicate property individuation, dependence and specified empirical discriminators. Counting replies settles nothing.

<!-- id: c04-p018 -->
What the exchange establishes, then, is a choice of explanatory commitments rather than a winner. Reject the qualified completeness of a nonexperiential causal account, and irreducible experiential causation becomes intelligible. But that rejection carries an obligation to explain the actual causal organization, including the neural-dependence patterns that any such model inherits and must reproduce. Preserve completeness through latent powers instead, and the mirror obligation follows: one must specify powers that nothing in their familiar manifestations predicts. Neither side wins by treating its own causal vocabulary as compulsory. The record's inventory of costs is explicitly not a parsimony score and not a ranking.

## Constitution, combination and individuation

<!-- id: c04-p019 -->
Experiential constitution moves the difficulty a third time. The version inspected is the later authorial one. The exact 2006 journal text was never inspected, and the two are one work-version family, not independent corroboration. On that version Strawson argues conditionally. Experience is concrete. Assume that reality is physical and constituted by ultimates. No experiential reality can emerge from wholly nonexperiential reality. So some ultimates involve experience, and panpsychism proper requires a further homogeneity premise [@S-P2T03-004]. The record treats the no-radical-emergence premise as a defended intuition, not a demonstration, and it treats the conclusion as conditional on premises it leaves unassessed.

<!-- id: c04-p020 -->
Goff's inspected objection asks why the forbidden brute emergence does not simply reappear one level down. A plurality of experiences does not transparently constitute a further, unified experience. And if experiential being is exhaustively how it feels, then a constituent's character should show up in the character of the whole. Slight pains do not add up to a severe pain of a new quality [@S-P2T03-002]. The objection also carries a charge of parity. Strawson uses experiential transparency against a posteriori physicalist identification, and that use threatens to prove too much, because experiential constitution is no more transparent than the physicalist's identity.

<!-- id: c04-p021 -->
The inspected reply concedes the ground it can afford and keeps the claim it cannot do without. Strawson accepts that the objection refutes one full-revelation reading of his view. He distinguishes first-order acquaintance from exhaustive disclosure, so that the absence of microsubjects in acquaintance does not establish the absence of microsubjects. He revises his ontology toward active experiences with internal causal powers, and he acknowledges that as a cost. He acknowledges a defective argument in his own text at the recorded locus. And he concedes that he has no account of how microexperience constitutes macroexperience; the claim that it does rests on a transcendental argument plus smallism [@S-P2T03-003]. The record's verdict is restraint in both directions. Constitution is not established, impossibility is not demonstrated, and the positive burden remains undischarged.

<!-- id: c04-p022 -->
Taking experience as basic therefore answers the demand for experiential ingredients, but it transfers the burden to organization and ownership. Which pluralities compose? Under what relation? And why does the result have one subject rather than many, or none? The transfer is not a failure. As the alternatives chapter recorded, every family carries transferred burdens. But it is a debt, and the record forbids paying it with the observation that the ingredients are at least of the right kind. Having the right kind of ingredient is the definitional relief of this chapter's opening section, bought a second time at a lower level. It explains nothing about composition.

<!-- id: c04-p023 -->
A universal consciousness reverses the direction of the same debt. Instead of composing one subject out of many, it must differentiate many private perspectives within one. The article's framing is that the reversal still owes an account of private perspectives and individuation. The legitimate attraction of a conscious ground, in either direction, is explanatory continuity: experience never has to arise from the nonexperiential. But continuity supplies no criterion of subject boundaries, no account of physical structure, and no discount on the individuation debt. Nor does the inspected constitution exchange lend the reversal any support. Micropsychism and experiential constitution entail no universal subject.

## The transferred burdens of a conscious ground

<!-- id: c04-p024 -->
What does primitiveness buy? This chapter answers with the article's two listed advantages. That answer is a chapter-level application of the article's inventory, not a census of the ledger, and it is conditional throughout on the demand being correctly identified. The first advantage is definitional relief. If the explanatory demand is correctly identified, it is met in the definite sense that nothing experiential has to arise from a stipulated nonexperiential base. The second is dialectical position. Against illusionism, the primitivist preserves the target rather than revising it, and that matters if and only if the target's realist identification survives the representational alternative. Both purchases are conditional on the demand being a fact about the world rather than a fact about our concepts. That is the very point Papineau's deflation contests, and the record leaves it standing as contested.

<!-- id: c04-p025 -->
What primitiveness does not buy is any of the following. A recorded boundary blocks each one. It does not buy explanatory superiority, because preservation of the target has not been shown superior to a successful misrepresentation account; no completed account exists on either side. It does not buy a mechanism. Generation laws, constitution relations and individuation criteria are all recorded as unsupplied. It does not buy empirical advantage. The 2005 maintenance model must reproduce the neural-dependence patterns it inherits, and it must specify discriminating dynamics before any empirical comparison can begin. The constitution defender's recorded bound is his own acceptance of the relevant experiential states as brain states. And none of the inspected texts supplies a discriminating prediction. Finally, it does not buy actuality. That a model is conditionally available, whether causal-emergent, constitutive or universal, means only that it is available. It is no evidence that the model is instantiated.

<!-- id: c04-p026 -->
The component boundaries translate these refusals into the thesis's working vocabulary. If the 2005 emergence ontology is granted, a limited property-level reading of conscious fundamentality can be satisfied despite instance-dependence. That is a conditional fact about the taxonomy, not an acceptance of the component. Causal mental influence under a maintenance condition establishes nothing about independence from brain function. Micropsychism and constitution entail no universal subject, no survival and no brain-independent consciousness, and the inspected constitution defender accepts the relevant experiential states as brain states. The inspected constitution chapter treats experience as requiring time, and the reply distinguishes activity from intentional agency. Neither statement supplies timelessness, preservation, intention, goodness or authority as established. Nothing else in these exchanges does either.

<!-- id: c04-p027 -->
The dependence accounting for this chapter is short and strict. The gap debate and its phenomenal-concepts sequel are one dependent conceptual thread with shared authors: a single Chalmers-Papineau group, not two. The illusionism material is one exchange. The emergence material is one exchange. The constitution material is one exchange within one work-version family. That makes four dependency groups, no more and no fewer: DG-T05-epistemic-ontological-gap, DG-P2T05-Frankish-Nida-single-exchange, DG-P2T01-CAUSAL-EMERGENCE-EXCHANGE, and DG-P2T03-2006-symposium-and-2008-version. However many paragraphs this chapter spends, its evidential base is four conversations, and the cumulative chapter will count it that way.

<!-- id: c04-p028 -->
Let me be clear about what this chapter has not shown. It cannot conclude that the explanatory demand is correctly identified. That is the standing dispute, and the record's deflationary attributions are as well documented as its antireductionist ones. It cannot conclude that any experiential-primitive ontology is actual, and it cannot rank the inspected causal-emergent and constitutive models against each other, or against the article's uninspected universal-consciousness reversal. It cannot turn the dialectical advantage of preserving the target into a probability of realism. All twelve withheld stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld through this chapter, and every assessment that uses this material downstream inherits every conditional stated here.
