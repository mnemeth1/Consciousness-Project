<!-- id: c01-p001 -->
Does consciousness belong to the foundation of reality? And if it did, what would follow for existence, for survival, for meaning and for morality? Those are the questions this thesis asks. This opening chapter expands the first section of the article into a full introduction. It explains why a conscious foundation is attractive. It describes the danger the article calls an illicit economy. It states the restricted-unification thesis that the whole work defends, sets out the nine hypothesis components together with what each of them does not entail, and ends with a map of the chapters to come. The framing comes from two places. One is the project's hypothesis specification, a reviewed stipulative memo that was accepted as method material with every substantive premise left unresolved. The other is the article's own opening paragraphs. Let me say plainly at the start: nothing in this chapter is a finding.

<!-- id: c01-p002 -->
It is worth fixing the epistemic register once, here, so that it need not be repeated. The specification this chapter draws on establishes the existence of no entity, the full coherence of no theory, and the independent verification of no experience. Its definitions and its conditional argument skeletons were written by the project. They are not attributed to any external literature. The article's abstract announces the position that the later chapters must earn. It is an asymmetric verdict: stronger grounds for embodied neural dependence stand alongside an unresolved phenomenal explanation and a viable, conditional conscious-foundation programme. This introduction states that thesis. It does not yet argue it, and no reader should credit any component of the proposal on the strength of the framing that follows.

## The promise of unification

<!-- id: c01-p003 -->
Why is the thought that consciousness belongs to the foundation of reality attractive at all? The reason is more substantial than dissatisfaction with materialism. Experience occupies a double position. It is something any account of the world must accommodate, and it is also the medium through which any explanation comes to matter to us. A theory that leaves experience out has therefore done more than omit one phenomenon among many. It has omitted the very phenomenon in which theories are entertained, tested and found significant. That is a gloss on the article's double-position sentence, which is itself the article's opening claim. And the claim is deliberately modest. It says why the proposal deserves examination, not why it is true.

<!-- id: c01-p004 -->
The promise then unfolds in three conditionals, and each of them begins with an "if". If reality is grounded in consciousness, the presence of conscious beings may cease to look like an inexplicable addition to an otherwise complete structure. If that ground also acts intentionally, embodied lives might acquire a place in an intelligible undertaking. If persons endure within it, their attachments and achievements might escape final loss. Notice what is on offer. The proposal promises explanatory and existential unification at the same time: one and the same reality could ground existence, subjectivity and significance. But each "if" is a separate commitment. The distance between those commitments is the subject of this thesis.

<!-- id: c01-p005 -->
The project states the central proposal it will test in a single sentence. Conscious reality is basic; the physical universe and individual experience depend on it; and embodied lives may have enduring significance through a conscious foundation that is not confined to physical succession. That sentence describes a family of hypotheses, and the family needs explicit variants. It is not one claim. Its full and strongest version adds universal subjecthood, personal survival, preservation, intention, goodness and moral authority. Supporting one of those components does not automatically support the whole conjunction. That last sentence is the specification's own warning, and it governs every chapter that follows.

<!-- id: c01-p006 -->
The motivating question is a comparative one. Does such a foundation explain existence, experience, enduring significance, cosmic purpose and morality better than specified alternatives do? The specification makes that question testable in parts. It names components, bearers, packages and disputed bridges. It does so without replacing cosmic purpose with finite-life satisfaction as a substitute target. The thesis asks the same question as the article. The difference is room: room to show the evidence, the reviews and the withheld conclusions in full.

## The risk of an illicit economy

<!-- id: c01-p007 -->
Here is the danger, and the article has a name for it: an illicit economy. The proposal offers several conclusions for the price of one primitive, and it can only do so by quietly changing what that primitive means along the way. At the start, "consciousness" names the qualitative character that a purely structural description leaves out. A few steps later it names a unified subject. Then it names a necessary agent. Finally it names a good authority who preserves persons. The word never changes; the commitment grows at every step. The article's own verdict is measured: each transition may be defensible. But notice what has happened. The article's diagnosis is that this is a progression, and each step adds content that the starting point did not contain.

<!-- id: c01-p008 -->
Each transition may be defensible. None of them, however, is merely an unpacking of the original explanatory demand. The article fixes the point with three contrasts. A plurality of basic experiential properties would answer a different question from a single cosmic person. A necessary mind could have intentions quite unlike ours. And preserving everything known about a life would differ from preserving the person who lived it. Whoever affirms the full proposal owes an argument at each of these junctures. The argument owed is different at each.

<!-- id: c01-p009 -->
How does the project guard against this economy? Its instrument is a register of disputed bridges. The register names fourteen inferential transitions. Each is recorded as a premise to investigate, not as an accepted inference, and each is entered with its strongest motivation and its decisive vulnerability. The register begins with the gap between explanatory failure and ontological fundamentality. It runs through the transitions among fundamentality, universality, survival and timelessness. It then reaches the normative bridges: from unity, creatorhood and necessary goodness to moral authority, and from proposed purpose to goodness, which is the bridge whose price chapter 10 reckons. It ends with the convergence bridge, the claim that shared success across several domains strongly supports the full conjunction. The recorded objection to that last bridge is that extra attributes and reused premises can manufacture the appearance of convergence. One rule protects the register. A downstream task that needs a premise not on the list must register a new bridge; it may not call the addition a definitional consequence in order to bypass review.

<!-- id: c01-p010 -->
Four conditional argument skeletons mark the transitions that the later chapters test at length. They were reviewed and accepted as method material, and not one of their premises was resolved. The first runs from preservation to personal survival: retained information plus a subject-preserving identity relation would yield survival. The recorded objection is that preservation can occur while the original subject ceases, and that duplicates expose the identity problem [@A-T01-001]. The second runs from endurance to enduring significance: persistence contributes significance only under stated normative premises. The objection is that duration alone need not increase value, and that finite value may exist without persistence [@A-T01-002]. The third runs from conscious production to cosmic purpose. Here the occurrence of experience is treated as an assumed explanandum, with no empirical assessment, and a conclusion about intention requires an independently specified end. The objection is that an outcome is compatible with an unintended byproduct [@A-T01-003]. The fourth runs from conscious foundation to moral authority: binding obligations require a normative relation beyond descriptive unity, origin, power or reward [@A-T01-004]. These are skeletons by design. They are the project's declaration, made in advance, of where the joints that carry the weight are.

## The restricted-unification thesis

<!-- id: c01-p011 -->
What, then, is the thesis this work seeks to earn? It is the article's own: a restricted-unification thesis. A conscious foundation is a serious candidate for improving the explanation of experience. Yet its present motivation supports considerably less than a providential account of enduring personal significance. Both halves matter. The first half separates this work from dismissals that treat the proposal as a confusion. The second separates it from defenses that treat the explanatory opening as a down payment on the full package.

<!-- id: c01-p012 -->
It should be said at once what the restriction is not. It is not a demand that every additional commitment carry an entirely independent proof. A suitably constrained joint theory can receive support from shared evidence, and the article concedes this explicitly. The present problem is different. The relevant commitments currently contribute quite different things: an explanatory starting point, disputed modal premises, causal possibilities, interpretations of reports, and normative aspirations. A conjunction of such unlike contributions is not yet a joint theory.

<!-- id: c01-p013 -->
When does convergence become evidence? Only when the connections among the commitments are themselves specified well enough to distinguish the proposal from its alternatives. That is the standard the cumulative chapter applies at the end of this work. It is also why the register's convergence bridge carries the manufactured-convergence objection. A premise reused in several domains and counted once in each, or an attribute added to absorb recalcitrant evidence, imitates the profile of independent support without its content.

<!-- id: c01-p014 -->
The article adds that the comparison should not be neutral by default, and the asymmetries it announces cut in both directions. The selected intervention evidence gives stronger reason to acknowledge neural participation in human experience than to deny it. Yet the inference from such participation to an exhaustive physical ontology is stronger than the evidence warrants. The case against making immortality necessary for every form of meaning is stronger than the case against finding any value in immortality. An assessment can therefore favor some local conclusions while leaving the encompassing ontology unsettled. These are the article's announced positions, and nothing more at this stage. The empirical, meaning and cumulative chapters carry the records that are meant to earn them.

## Nine components and what they do not entail

<!-- id: c01-p015 -->
The central discipline of the specification is decomposition with declared bearers. Every commitment must say whose property it is: the foundation's, every person's, some persons', or that of some other explicitly bounded population. The reason is simple. An existential finding, that something holds for some, must not slide silently into a universal claim, that it holds for all. Nine labeled components carry the proposal's content. Each is recorded together with what it does not entail, so that no chapter can spend one component's support on another component's conclusion.

<!-- id: c01-p016 -->
Take them in order. Fundamentality (H-F) holds that experiential properties, or conscious reality, belong to the basic ontology and are not wholly grounded in nonexperiential reality. Two limits are recorded with it. Irreducibility alone does not establish this stronger status, and fundamentality entails neither necessity, universality, cosmic explanation, survival nor intentional creation. Universality (H-U) holds that every subject in the claimed domain depends on one numerically single conscious subject or conscious whole, and the subject-versus-whole variant is made explicit. Shared laws, similar experiences or common ancestry are not this claim. And one whole entails neither timelessness, personal identity with the whole, intention, goodness nor authority.

<!-- id: c01-p017 -->
Brain independence (H-B) holds that a subject can instantiate experience without the bodily or neural processes that the specified dependence model makes necessary. Two distinctions are stated separately inside it: actual occurrence versus metaphysical possibility, and temporary independence versus independence after irreversible death. The component is not the same as independence from all physical substrates. It does not establish that a particular person survives death, that a mind is fundamental, or that experience is timeless. And the absence of a measurement does not satisfy the criterion. Personal survival (H-S) holds that the numerically same person has subjectivity beyond irreversible bodily death, under an explicitly chosen identity relation. Resuscitation does not satisfy the death condition. Under strict numerical identity, resemblance, a stored record or an indistinguishable replacement is not enough. Survival need not be timeless, universal, morally good or unlimited in duration. Timelessness (H-T) holds that the specified bearer has no intrinsic temporal succession. That is not longevity, not eternity through successive times, and not a reported loss of the awareness of time. A timeless foundation does not entail timeless persons, and it does not entail survival.

<!-- id: c01-p018 -->
Preservation (H-P) holds that a specified item from embodied life remains available or has continuing significance. It is recorded separately for information, accessible memory, relationships, subjectivity and value. Preserved information need not preserve a subject. Preserved significance does not by itself entail a storing mind, intrinsic goodness or an intended purpose. Intention (H-I) holds that a conscious agent directs or grounds the universe toward specified ends. Producing consciousness does not by itself establish existing in order to produce it. Intention entails neither benevolence nor success nor authority. And an impersonal teleology remains a separate alternative.

<!-- id: c01-p019 -->
Goodness (H-G) holds that the foundation's nature or agency meets a stated normative standard. Merely having power is not enough, and merely being desired is not enough. Goodness cannot be defined as whatever the foundation happens to do, and preserving experience, suffering included, need not be good on balance. Moral authority (H-A) holds that the foundation grounds genuinely binding reasons or obligations through a specified normative relation. Power, authorship, unity, rewards, punishment or command alone do not establish it. One clarification remains. The components are assessed separately; they are not asserted to be logically independent of one another. Under a survival version with no replacement substrate, H-S entails that instance of H-B. (Survival by a replacement physical substrate, by contrast, may remain compatible with dependence on a brain of some kind.) H-S also entails continuing subjectivity, which is a narrow form of H-P, without entailing complete informational, relational or evaluative preservation. Such consequences must be recorded whenever the relevant version is selected.

<!-- id: c01-p020 -->
Above the components sit named packages. They run from the minimal fundamental-experience thesis, through the conscious-foundation thesis, its timeless variant, enduring personal subjectivity, universal preservation and the consciousness-purpose proposal, up to the full integrated proposal. Two words in the package definitions need care. The word "required" means required in order to count as the named hypothesis; it does not mean established truth. The word "open" means unconstrained; it does not mean denied. Necessity, omnipotence, omniscience, a particular religious identity and unlimited duration for every person are further optional claims, and no package includes them by default. When a required claim is withdrawn or a mechanism is added, a package revision must be recorded. Supporting the minimal thesis after abandoning survival is a possible substantive result. It is not confirmation of the unchanged full proposal. The specification also reconciles the eight rows of the research plan with the nine components. It tracks irreducibility as a weaker subsidiary claim rather than as fundamentality, and it keeps the explanatory target (why anything exists, why physical reality, why these laws, why consciousness) as a cross-cutting question rather than a tenth attribute.

## A map of the thesis

<!-- id: c01-p021 -->
Chapter 2 sets out the method. It explains how the project turned this specification into an auditable evidence ledger of sources, claims, arguments and cases. It explains how independent adversarial review, dependence accounting, sensitivity tests and withheld-conclusion gates limit what any chapter may assert. And it explains how the text of the thesis is itself tied to the ledger by a paragraph-level crosswalk. The method is what makes the framing above enforceable rather than merely announced.

<!-- id: c01-p022 -->
Chapters 3 through 5 carry the explanatory arc. Chapter 3 builds the six comparison families of the accepted record: physicalism with its illusionist edge, psychophysical-law and strong-emergence views, panpsychism, substance dualism, idealism in its universal and plural forms, and theism, the last present mainly by stipulation. A fairness rule governs the building. Each family is assessed in the strongest formulation available in the project record, and that is a bounded claim about this record, not about the literature as a whole. Chapter 4 asks what experiential primitiveness would and would not explain, and it holds the line between an unresolved explanatory gap and a conclusion of fundamentality. Chapter 5 follows the proposal upward, from an experiential ground to an intending agent. That is where the questions of existence, necessity and design live, and where the bridge from foundation to mind remains open.

<!-- id: c01-p023 -->
Chapter 6 is the empirical center of the thesis, and it has three parts. The first is the neural intervention evidence that constrains any dependence model. The second is the set of selected crisis-experience dossiers. These are examined at their strongest, and on inspection not one of them establishes three things together: externally accurate content, a defensible interval of acquisition, and adequate information about the processes available during that interval. The third is the frame around those dossiers, namely the prospective studies and the historical and cross-cultural corpus. The chapter's verdicts are verdicts about causal constraint. They are not verdicts about ontology, in either direction.

<!-- id: c01-p024 -->
Chapters 7 and 8 take up continuation and significance. Chapter 7 tests the identity side of the promise: timeless foundations, temporal persons, the ownership of experience, and the difference between an eternal representation of a life and the survival of the person who lived it. Chapter 8 tests the value side. Does significance require endurance? There the article's asymmetry between necessity claims and value claims about immortality is defended, and there the register's production-to-purpose bridge meets the consciousness-factory discrimination design.

<!-- id: c01-p025 -->
Chapters 9 and 10 reckon the price of the proposal's moral crown. Chapter 9 maps where moral authority could come from, and it finds that every route from a conscious foundation to binding obligation crosses a contested normative bridge. Chapter 10 asks what it costs, and what it buys, to specify the foundation as benevolent. It takes up the evidential arguments from suffering and hiddenness and their strongest inspected replies. It then states a constraint: a benevolent unification must let its claims about goodness restrict what counts as an expected world. An unconstrained specification discriminates weakly. It cannot face qualitative tests until its ends, population, means, constraints and tradeoffs are fixed in advance. Both chapters run under a declared symmetry rule. The value premises that support the moral-grounding proposal also underwrite the objections, and neither side may count the same premise twice.

<!-- id: c01-p026 -->
Chapter 11 assembles the cumulative comparison under the convergence standard stated above. Its dependence map and its sensitivity withdrawals keep shared premises from being counted once per domain. Chapter 12 states the conclusions. It also states the withheld conclusions, which are as much a result of this project as the asserted ones. The appendices carry the case tables, the alternatives matrix, the complete register of gap and gate dispositions, and the dependence map. Twelve stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld as of this introduction. Every chapter of this thesis, this one included, ends by saying so: its closing paragraph states that all twelve remain withheld, and chapter 11 lists the twelve conclusions that are not drawn.

<!-- id: c01-p027 -->
Let me be clear about what this introduction has not shown. It asserts no component of the proposal: not fundamentality, not universality, not brain independence, and not survival, timelessness, preservation, intention, goodness or moral authority. It asserts no package, minimal or full. The specification it presents is stipulation. Its definitions are precise enough to classify claims and expose contradictions; they are not measurements of metaphysical structure, and they can be revised only through a versioned change of protocol. The restricted-unification thesis and the announced asymmetries are, at this point in the thesis, claims whose evidence still lies ahead of them. All twelve withheld stronger-conclusion gates remain withheld through this introduction. The chapters that follow either earn them from the ledger or record why they remain open.
