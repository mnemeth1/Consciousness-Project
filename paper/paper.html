Abstract

A conscious foundation promises to connect the explanation of experience with the existence of an ordered world, the persistence of persons and the authority of value. This article argues that its central attraction survives a critical comparison, but that its most ambitious consequences receive substantially less support than its starting point. Experiential primitiveness can answer the demand that experience enter the fundamental explanatory vocabulary; it transfers, rather than discharges, the problems of structure and subject individuation. Neural intervention evidence imposes substantive causal constraints, while the selected crisis evidence lacks the conjunction needed to discriminate extracerebral acquisition. Neither an eternal representation of a life nor the desirability of its continuation supplies personal survival. Finally, finite meaningful engagement and practical normativity possess argumentative resources that do not depend on a settled cosmic ontology. The resulting position is asymmetric: stronger grounds for embodied neural dependence coexist with an unresolved phenomenal explanation and a viable, conditional conscious-foundation programme. Holistic confirmation remains possible, but requires a constrained joint account whose connections do explanatory work rather than reproduce the desired conclusions.

1Unification and its price

The thought that consciousness belongs to the foundation of reality is attractive for a reason more substantial than dissatisfaction with materialism. Experience is both what any account of the world must accommodate and the medium through which explanation has significance for us. If reality is grounded in consciousness, the presence of conscious beings may cease to look like an inexplicable addition to an otherwise complete structure. If that ground also acts intentionally, embodied lives might acquire a place in an intelligible undertaking. If persons endure within it, their attachments and achievements might escape final loss. The proposal offers explanatory and existential unification: the same reality could ground existence, subjectivity and significance.

Its danger is an illicit economy. One may purchase several conclusions for the price of a single primitive by changing what the primitive means along the way. Consciousness first means the qualitative character neglected by a structural description; it later means a unified subject, then a necessary agent, and finally a good authority that preserves persons. Each transition may be defensible. None is merely an unpacking of the initial explanatory demand. A plurality of basic experiential properties would answer a different question from a single cosmic person; a necessary mind could have intentions unlike ours; and preserving everything known about a life would differ from preserving the person who lived it.

I defend a restricted-unification thesis. A conscious foundation is a serious candidate for improving the explanation of experience, but its present motivation supports considerably less than a providential account of enduring personal significance. This is not an argument that each additional commitment requires an entirely independent proof. A suitably constrained joint theory can receive support from shared evidence. The problem is that the relevant commitments currently contribute different things: an explanatory starting point, disputed modal premises, causal possibilities, interpretations of reports and normative aspirations. Their convergence becomes evidential only when their connections are themselves sufficiently specified to distinguish the proposal from alternatives.

The resulting comparison should not be neutral by default. The selected intervention evidence gives stronger reason to acknowledge neural participation in human experience than to deny it. The inference from such participation to an exhaustive physical ontology is nevertheless stronger than the evidence warrants. Similarly, the case against making immortality necessary for every form of meaning is stronger than the case against finding any value in immortality. An assessment can therefore favor some local conclusions while leaving the encompassing ontology unsettled. The argument proceeds from phenomenal explanation to existence and design, then uses neural and crisis evidence to test the proposal’s empirical reach, before considering identity, value and goodness.

2Comparative method

The method is critical comparative synthesis of selected philosophical exchanges and empirical studies. Arguments are assessed for explanatory adequacy, coherence, independent motivation, fit with the evidence and discriminatory power. A stopping point is not objectionable simply because it is primitive: every sufficiently ambitious theory encounters some basic commitments. The relevant questions are whether its primitives address the problem, constrain subsequent explanation and impose costs comparable to those accepted in rivals.

Three distinctions guide the empirical comparison: a report and its reported event; an observation and its interpretation; and an observed dependence and an identity claim. Repeated descriptions of one episode are not independent cases. Manuscripts, later editions and replies are cited at the passages actually available, with version information in the bibliography. The comparison is not an exhaustive survey or a quantitative aggregation. Its robustness is tested qualitatively by withdrawing particular cases or premises and asking which reasons remain.

3What experiential primitiveness explains

Chalmers’s distinction between functional problems and the problem of experience identifies a demand that survives many ordinary explanatory successes. Explaining discrimination, access or behavioral control may explain why a system responds as it does without explaining why those processes have a subjective character. The argument is not that functional explanation has achieved nothing. It is that completing that explanatory task would not obviously complete the different task of accounting for experience (Chalmers 1995, §§2–3, 5–6). A theory that includes experience among its primitives meets this demand in one precise sense: it ceases to require the experiential to arise from a vocabulary stipulated to contain nothing experiential.

That is an advantage only if the demand is correctly identified. A physicalist can distinguish epistemic access from ontology: two ways of thinking about one state need not disclose two states. An identity claim could therefore be true despite an unresolved explanatory gap. Conversely, the mere possibility of such an identity is not an explanation of why these physical processes are experienced in these ways. The methodological burden runs both ways. The antireductionist must defend the transition from a gap in explanation to a gap in the world; the reductionist must show how the proposed identification preserves its explanandum.

Illusionism makes the second burden unusually explicit. Frankish proposes retaining experience in an inclusive sense—perceptual activity, access, reports and introspective representation—while rejecting genuinely qualitative properties. The central task becomes explaining why a system represents itself as possessing such properties. This offers a coherent research direction if the relevant seeming can be understood nonphenomenally. It also avoids adding a conscious spectator who inspects internal representations and thereby recreates the problem. The selected account remains a programme with proposed content mechanisms, rather than a completed explanation (Frankish 2016a, eprint pp. 1–4, 8–10, 17–22).

Nida-Rümelin’s objection is stronger than the slogan that an illusion must itself be conscious. She identifies experiential properties through subjects and examples, and argues that instantiating the relevant property already involves awareness of it. On this account, acquaintance is not a second introspective judgment that can misrepresent an independently occurring state. Her conclusion is conditional on an experiential example actually being instantiated (Nida-Rümelin 2016, manuscript §§1–4, pp. 2–9). Frankish’s reply grants ordinary examples and the subject-property reformulation for argument, but denies that reference by example fixes the property’s realist nature. What is identified may admit a representational interpretation (Frankish 2016b, eprint pp. 25–27).

The dispute consequently concerns what must be preserved in explaining the example. Defining every seeming as phenomenal would assume the realist premise. Defining its apparent character as nothing beyond a representation would assume too much in the other direction. The stronger illusionist response must explain the richness and authority of phenomenal judgments without quietly relying on their truth. The stronger realist response must show why its identification of the target resists that explanation. Experiential primitiveness has a genuine dialectical advantage for the realist: it preserves rather than revises the target. It does not yet establish that preservation is explanatorily superior to a successful account of misrepresentation.

Nor does accepting the target select one antireductive ontology. O’Connor and Wong develop causal emergence through nonstructural properties and temporally extended causal maintenance. Their later account explicitly withdraws the earlier supervenience requirement. This matters because Kim’s downward-causation objection asks how an emergent cause avoids preemption by sufficient lower-level causes. It is not answered merely by naming an emergent property; the defender must reject or reinterpret a premise of the exclusion argument (O’Connor and Wong 2005, pp. 662–670, 675 n. 8; Kim 1999, reprint §§7.6–7.8).

Shoemaker’s micro-latent powers offer a different causal picture: powers manifested only within suitable organization may belong to the lower-level constituents without being predictable from their familiar manifestations. His preferred identity-and-description treatment differs from irreducible emergent properties (Shoemaker 2002, pp. 53–63). The exchange establishes a productive choice of explanatory commitments. Rejecting physical causal completeness can make irreducible causation intelligible, but incurs an obligation to explain the actual causal organization. Preserving completeness through latent powers incurs its own burden of specifying those powers. Neither side wins simply by treating its favored causal vocabulary as compulsory.

Experiential constitution relocates the difficulty again. Strawson’s inspected later argument rejects radical emergence from wholly nonexperiential reality; Goff’s combination challenge asks why a plurality of experiences should constitute a further unified experience (Strawson 2008, pp. 53–74; Goff 2006, pp. 53–61). Taking experience as basic thus answers the demand for experiential ingredients while transferring the burden to their organization and ownership. A universal consciousness reverses the direction—from many subjects to differentiation within one—but still owes an account of private perspectives. The legitimate attraction is explanatory continuity, not a free solution to individuation or physical structure.

4From an experiential ground to an intending agent

A foundational theory must also address why this concrete order exists. Gale and Pruss attempt to derive an explanation of the totality of contingent truths from a weak principle of sufficient reason: roughly, the totality could have an explanation. Their argument combines a world-complete contingent conjunction with modal and explanatory commitments to reach an actual explanation. The further move to a necessary agent depends on the inadequacy of a contingent scientific explanation of the whole and on the proposed resources of personal explanation (Gale and Pruss 1999, pp. 461–468). Its attraction is clear: explain contingency through something that does not merely add another unexplained contingent member.

Oppy’s criticism challenges the advertised modesty of the starting point. Under the accompanying assumptions about explanation, the weak principle entails a strong one. A person who initially finds the weaker wording plausible may rationally withdraw assent when its consequences become explicit; psychological willingness to accept a sentence is not stable warrant for everything derivable from it (Oppy 2000, manuscript §§II–IV). Gale and Pruss acknowledge the entailment in their reply. They argue that understanding a proposition need not include awareness of all its consequences, and that explanation-seeking practices give the weaker formulation epistemic appeal (Gale and Pruss 2002, pp. 89–97).

The reply blocks an easy accusation of unintelligibility: an argument can disclose consequences genuinely not evident at first. It does not make the disclosed consequences costless. The question becomes whether commitment to the explanatory principle remains better justified than the alternatives once its modal force is understood. Moreover, the rejection of an unexplained totality does not uniquely select mentality. The classification of adequate explanations, the possibility of a necessary being’s contingent free act, and the proposed self-explanatory character of that act all matter. Personal explanation cannot be privileged solely because the intended conclusion is personal. The cosmological argument provides a structured challenge to brute contingency, not an uncontested passage from intelligibility to providence.

Fine-tuning promises a more discriminatory route. A life-permitting order might be expected if an agent values embodied consciousness and less expected under a specified alternative. Collins develops such a comparison using epistemic probability and a range of possible values, while discussing parameterization and measure difficulties. The expectation of life under design already depends on assumptions about the designer’s goodness or goals (Collins 1998, §II and Appendix ii–iii). This dependence is not inherently circular: a separately constrained conception of agency could predict life. It becomes circular if life is the sole ground for attributing the preference that is then invoked to explain life.

The distinction between life’s existence and the stringency of its conditions sharpens the issue. White argues that newly learned stringency can support design even when our existence is already known. His argument uses evidential probabilities, so the absence of repeated universe creation is not itself a refutation (White 2011, pp. 678–679). Weisberg’s subsequent critique shows how matched conditional distributions can screen off stringency, while allowing initial life evidence to favor design. His constructed model challenges the rationale for a positive comparison without establishing the true distribution of universes (Weisberg 2012, manuscript pp. 2–7).

The appropriate conclusion is conditional discrimination. Observer selection does not trivially erase every relevant difference, and unavailable numerical probabilities do not imply that all qualitative comparisons are empty. But a useful design argument must identify the evidence being updated on, the alternatives and the assumptions governing their expectations. Otherwise an apparently enormous explanatory gain comes from putting a life-valuing preference on one side and an invented uniform distribution on the other. Neither unspecified brute reality nor unspecified agency has that distribution by definition. A conscious ground earns additional support from design only through a defensible account of what it would be inclined to produce.

5Neural dependence and the evidential role of crisis experiences

The demand for discrimination becomes especially important when consciousness is proposed to operate independently of the brain. In one epilepsy patient, Parvizi and colleagues’ stimulation of right fusiform face-selective regions produced reported face distortions; sham stimulation and stimulation of a nearby control pair did not produce those face distortions. This controlled contrast supports a causal contribution within this case. It does not isolate a unitary face center: the two stimulated regions were not separated, and stimulation can affect connected sites. The finding therefore leaves local-versus-network contributions unresolved (Parvizi et al. 2012, Methods and Results). This is more informative than the observation that brains and reports covary. An adequate account of human experience must accommodate systematic changes in what is experienced under changes in neural activity.

A transmission or realization theorist can reply that altering an interface alters what appears through it. That possibility prevents the intervention from logically entailing physical identity. Yet bare compatibility is a weak explanatory achievement. To rival the empirical account, the alternative must explain why the specified intervention changes the specified content, and how its additional subject or field relates to the manipulated system. A metaphor of reception supplies no such relation by itself. The evidence consequently favors genuine neural constraints over their denial, while leaving open whether the constrained process constitutes, realizes or mediates experience. Equal logical compatibility would not amount to equal explanatory support.

Crisis reports are potentially relevant because accurate information acquired under conditions excluding the relevant ordinary processes would put pressure on a specified neural account. The crucial evidence is conjunctive: externally accurate content, a defensible interval of acquisition and adequate information about the processes available during that interval. A striking report with uncertain timing does not have the same discriminatory force. Nor does a physiological interval establish when remembered content was formed. These requirements should be applied symmetrically: a proposed ordinary mechanism also needs case-specific grounds before it counts as the demonstrated explanation.

The Reynolds controversy illustrates both the positive interest and the difficulty. Specific reported surgical details invite an explanation; disputes over auditory access, operative phases and monitoring are therefore consequential. Woerlee develops an ordinary-hearing account, while Hameroff and Carter question its treatment of monitoring and clinical conditions. Hameroff’s response includes a conditional concession about awareness under anesthesia, rather than an unqualified denial of that possibility; Carter’s response introduces technical communications and chronology objections (Woerlee 2011, pp. 4–15, 18–20; Hameroff 2011, p. 27; Carter 2011, pp. 30–48). General monitoring protocols cannot simply be substituted for the actual case protocol, and demonstrations of ordinary audibility cannot be assumed to reproduce the operative conditions.

The value of this exchange lies in narrowing the causal alternatives, not in increasing the number of observations. Repeated accounts of the episode remain dependent on the same event and its reconstruction. Technical testimony quoted by an author is relevant testimony, but differs from possession of the original physiological record. Thus skepticism about an exclusion claim does not establish that Woerlee’s mechanism actually caused every reported detail. Conversely, objections to that mechanism do not time-lock the experience to an interval in which all relevant neural processing was excluded. The unresolved conjunction, rather than the mere unusualness of the narrative, limits the survival inference.

The prospective AWARE studies improve the investigative setting by defining populations and incorporating attempts to test awareness, but they do not make every reported experience an effective target trial. In AWARE II, the prospective participants and the community sample have distinct evidential roles. Community recollections do not inherit prospective EEG observations. The availability of a target somewhere in a study does not establish that a particular experiencer had the relevant opportunity, and margins describing separate subsets cannot supply their missing intersection (Parnia et al. 2014, Methods and Results; Parnia et al. 2023, pp. 2–9, Figs. 1–3).

The direct EEG dispute is especially revealing. Greyson and van Lommel emphasize the absence of a link between explicit recollection and the reported EEG findings. The main article’s flow diagram notes EEG data for two of the twenty-eight interviewed survivors, neither reporting explicit cognitive recall. Parnia replies that failure to recall does not establish absence of consciousness, and defends the recording and interpretation procedures (Greyson and van Lommel 2024, p. 1; Parnia 2024, p. 1; Parnia et al. 2023, p. 7, Fig. 2 footnote). The reply succeeds against the simple negative inference. It does not establish what those individuals experienced or provide the missing link between a particular remembered content and recorded conditions.

There is accordingly an evidential asymmetry. The controlled stimulation case provides positive evidence of neural influence on experience-related outcomes, within its single-patient scope. The selected crisis material provides reports requiring explanation and possibilities worth testing, but lacks the conjunction needed to establish extracerebral acquisition. This is neither a proof that every account has an ordinary cause nor a claim that a negative target result establishes impossibility. Removing a celebrated retrospective case leaves the prospective questions intact; removing all retrospective narratives reduces both favorable recurrence arguments and cultural countercomparisons. Such dependence prevents anecdotal accumulation from doing the work of a discriminating observation.

6Continuation, timelessness and the ownership of experience

Even evidence of consciousness under unexpected physiological conditions would leave personal survival to be argued. Survival concerns a further subject who is the same person, not merely the recurrence of a conscious state or preservation of information. Psychological continuity, persistence of an organism and persistence of a simple subject propose different relations. Each must connect alleged further experience with its earlier owner. A duplicate’s accurate memories could matter greatly without settling strict identity, while concern for a successor may be rational without answering the original survival question.

Timelessness is often offered as a way to avoid loss altogether: perhaps a temporal life is wholly present to an eternal mind. Stump and Kretzmann develop a richer proposal than static information storage. Eternity includes life and duration without succession; their original relation between eternal and temporal entities is symmetric but not transitive. It therefore need not collapse distinct temporal moments into simultaneity. An eternal act could, on their account, produce a temporal effect without a sequence of divine learning and deciding (Stump and Kretzmann 1981, pp. 430–440, 446–451). This is a genuine attempt to explain how temporal dependence could coexist with a nonsuccessive agent.

Lewis challenges whether knowledge of a temporal history is enough for the required presence. If presently actual existence differs from belonging somewhere to an actual history, knowing all dated relations may omit the feature at issue. His 1988 criticism presses the demand for temporal things to be existentially present, rather than merely represented, in eternity (Lewis 1988, pp. 75–86). The later reply revises the original ET relation to ET′, explicitly incorporating capacities for direct causal relations and awareness across the two modes. It argues, by comparison with spatial awareness, that shared access need not require a shared mode of existence (Stump and Kretzmann 1992, pp. 475–478).

The reply improves the proposal by identifying the relation it needs; it does not establish the possibility of that relation by definition. Its analogy deserves consideration, but the existence of an absolute temporal present is precisely a point at which the spatial case may differ. More decisively for survival, a relation between an eternal knower and a temporal person is not an identity relation between them. Stump and Kretzmann themselves deny that a temporal person’s life and death being present to eternity makes that person eternal (1981, p. 443). Complete divine awareness could preserve the truth about a finite life while adding no further experience for its subject.

A defender can posit further experiences belonging to that person, perhaps within an enlarged history or through restoration. That is a substantive additional proposal, requiring both a defensible sameness relation and grounds for its actuality. It cannot be secured by changing “preserved” from continuing subjectivity to eternal availability. This distinction also protects the genuine attraction of continuation: wanting one’s relationships and activity to continue need not be satisfied by their being eternally known. A theory that preserves information while ending its owner answers a different existential concern.

7Does significance require endurance or cosmic authority?

The best argument for endurance begins with loss, not vanity. Love, inquiry and creative activity can be worthwhile yet vulnerable; death removes opportunities to sustain them and may frustrate projects whose value depends on continuation. A final-loss argument can therefore claim that lasting preservation would improve a life without assuming that only infinite achievements matter. But there is a further step from the badness of ending valuable activity to the absence of value in the activity that occurred. If a friendship’s ending is bad because the friendship mattered, that explanation does not by itself erase its earlier significance.

Wolf’s account supplies a developed alternative to that erasure. Meaning arises through active engagement with something worthy of love or attention: subjective attraction and objective worth constrain each other. Pleasure alone is insufficient, as is beneficial activity wholly disconnected from the agent’s engagement. This gives an account of how a finite episode could matter to the person living it and be worth that concern (Wolf 2010, pp. 8–10, 17–26). It does not promise to remove distress about cosmic smallness or to provide an infallible theory of objective value. Its strength is to identify a kind of significance for which endless duration is not part of the proposed explanation.

A defender of immortality can accept this account and argue that the same goods warrant continuation. Williams’s challenge concerns whether an endless life could remain desirable to this person: projects may become exhausted, while sufficiently radical changes might leave the present person without reason to anticipate the result as their own worthwhile future. Fischer replies that meaningful absorption need not erase ownership, that a life can contain varied activities, and that appropriately spaced repeatable pleasures might sustain categorical desires (Williams 1973, pp. 85–98; Fischer 1994, reprint pp. 224–237). The reply undermines an easy inference from endless repetition of one activity to the undesirability of every endless life.

Neither position licenses a blanket verdict. An attractive possible continuation need not make compulsory immortality desirable for everyone; the possibility of boredom need not make every extension worthless. The constructive conclusion is that finite meaning and valuable endurance are compatible claims. A conscious foundation could enhance significance by sustaining worthwhile lives, but need not be invoked to rescue every finite good from meaninglessness. This removes one reason for treating cosmology as a prerequisite of ordinary significance without trivializing death’s harms.

Moral authority raises a further question. An account of why beings exist is not yet an account of what they owe one another. Even shared consciousness would not settle whether another subject’s interest supplies an overriding reason for me. Korsgaard proposes a bridge through reflection: an agent must take some consideration as a reason, and practical identity helps determine which considerations can be endorsed. The argument seeks to connect commitment to one’s humanity with recognition of humanity in others, rather than deriving obligation from sheer power or causal origin (Korsgaard 1996, pp. 92–130, 132–166).

Nagel’s objection is that identity may depend on reasons rather than confer their authority. A reflective egoist can understand a public demand while disputing its force; public intelligibility is not obviously impartial justification. Korsgaard replies by distinguishing incentives from their endorsement and making general commitments constitutive of willing. Yet she explicitly recognizes that formal universality does not itself establish humanity-wide scope or publicity; those require the further arguments (Nagel 1996, pp. 201–208; Korsgaard 1996, pp. 219–233, 242–258). The exchange locates an exact normative burden that a common-consciousness proposal would also face.

Robust realism offers a different stopping point: some reasons may be irreducibly normative. Enoch defends an indispensability route to belief in such truths; Lenman asks whether a thinner practice of critically examining concerns could perform the work without robust realism. Enoch replies that behaviorally similar choice may lack the justified allegiance deliberation requires (Lenman 2014, pp. 837–841; Enoch 2014, §§3–4, 6). The question is whether that richer demand is unavoidable or imports the realist commitment. A theory that begins with consciousness does not dissolve it: explaining how a demand arises is different from explaining why it merits compliance.

Thus the normative comparison remains substantive without waiting for a verdict on fundamental ontology. Constructivism owes a bridge from agency to the scope and force of reasons; realism owes warrant for its normative starting point; conscious authority owes a relation between its nature and legitimate claims on agents. None gains exemption by naming its stopping point. A successful metaphysics of consciousness could illuminate the bearers and objects of value. It would still need to explain the normative relation, including why these reasons apply and when they override others.

8Goodness as an explanatory constraint

Adding goodness to a conscious foundation increases both its attraction and its vulnerability. A life-valuing agent might explain why conscious beings arise, but suffering and nonresistant nonbelief become relevant to what such an agent would permit. These objections target specified benevolence, knowledge, power or openness to relationship. They need not undermine every possible conscious ground to weaken a particular providential explanation.

Adams’s response to horrendous evils insists that a person’s suffering must be defeated within that person’s life, rather than merely outweighed by benefits elsewhere. Participation in divine goodness might integrate even horrors into a life worth living (Adams 1989, pp. 302–310). This is a morally stronger response than an impersonal balance of aggregate goods because it preserves the victim as the subject whose life requires repair. Sutherland’s criticism presses the justice of the harm and the claims of other victims: reassurance experienced by an observer cannot settle their claims for them (Sutherland 1989, pp. 318–323). The resulting distinction is crucial: a possible mode of defeat explains how restoration might matter; it does not establish actual restoration or show that permitting the horror was justified.

Howard-Snyder’s hiddenness defense similarly appeals to the quality of relationship rather than denying its value. Delayed disclosure might allow a person to develop or freely own a better disposition toward God. His account distinguishes belief from willing reciprocity and includes an epistemic fallback concerning our ability to identify seekers to whom no justifying reason applies (Howard-Snyder 1996, pp. 433–450). Schellenberg’s response contests whether the better-relationship rationale warrants withholding an available relationship, rather than improving the person within it (Schellenberg 1996, pp. 455–462). The issue is comparative, not whether spiritual development is conceivable.

The defense also bears costs internal to the larger proposal. A delay lasting an earthly lifetime relies on survival if relationship is eventually to occur; that survival cannot simultaneously be inferred from the defense’s need for it. And if unknown reasons answer every discrepancy between suffering or nonbelief and a good agent’s supposed preferences, the recognition of favorable evidence needs equal scrutiny. This is not a refutation of every appeal to limited human understanding. It is a constraint on using specified intentions to explain life while treating their apparent failures as wholly inscrutable. A benevolent unification must allow its claims about goodness to restrict what counts as an expected world.

9Holistic support without evidential inflation

An important objection to restricted unification is that it asks too much of connecting premises. Scientific and philosophical theories often gain support as packages: evidence need not confirm every component independently before it can confirm their conjunction. A conscious-ground theory could, in principle, explain experiential character, lawful neural mediation, distinct subjects and purposive order together. Its economy might compensate for uncertainty about individual mechanisms. Requiring an independent demonstration of each connection would prejudge that legitimate strategy against it.

I accept the objection to that stronger requirement. The relevant standard is constraint, not evidential isolation. Shared data can favor a joint model when its commitments constrain one another and expose it to failure. The present difficulty is more particular. Basic experience does not yet specify how subjects combine or differentiate. A necessary explanation does not settle the kind of explaining entity. Neural mediation is often asserted without a content-specific account. Actual restoration is used to answer harms before there is evidence of its occurrence. These links can coherently be proposed, but their current flexibility weakens the claimed gain in discrimination.

This diagnosis explains the qualitative sensitivity of the cumulative case. Rejecting the modal or sufficient-reason premise weakens the corresponding grounding argument without resolving the experiential target. Removing designer-preference assumptions weakens a life-order inference without making life evidentially irrelevant under every possible model. Removing a retrospective crisis case reduces a particular empirical contribution without erasing the controlled stimulation evidence or prospective observations. Rejecting immortality as necessary for all meaning leaves possible worthwhile continuation intact. The positive and negative reasons are partly connected and partly separable; neither wholesale confirmation nor wholesale dismissal respects that structure.

Scope of the conclusion

The comparison supports embodied neural dependence more directly than metaphysical identity, and finite meaningful engagement more directly than an all-encompassing preserving purpose. It leaves phenomenal explanation contested because the realist target and its proposed reinterpretation remain in dispute. It leaves survival unresolved because the relevant empirical conjunction and identity relation have not been established together. These conclusions concern the selected evidence and arguments, not the impossibility of a more discriminating model. Missing source versions and incomplete clinical linkages constrain particular attributions and inferences; they do not constitute evidence for the opposite ontology. Broader cultural generalization or numerical ranking of worldviews would require a different evidential basis.

10Conclusion

A conscious foundation earns serious consideration by confronting the experiential explanandum directly. That virtue is neither trivial nor sufficient for the larger promise of enduring significance. Once the proposal undertakes to explain an ordered physical world, private subjects, continued persons and binding reasons, its primitives must be joined by substantive accounts of structure, identity, agency and value. The selected evidence gives asymmetric guidance: the controlled stimulation finding constrains accounts of human consciousness; crisis reports have not yet discriminated extracerebral acquisition; finite engagement can matter without an established cosmic purpose; and goodness makes providential claims answerable to suffering and hiddenness.

The defensible ambition is therefore a constrained unification whose connections earn support together, not a metaphysical vocabulary in which desired consequences are already contained. Such a programme remains open. Its success would consist in explaining more while risking more: preserving the experiential target, recovering the neural constraints, individuating actual subjects and making intelligible predictions about a world governed by its proposed values.
